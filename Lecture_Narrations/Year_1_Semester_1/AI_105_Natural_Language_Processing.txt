PART 1 - WELCOME: GIVING COMPUTERS THE GIFT OF LANGUAGE

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 105, Natural Language Processing. Language is the most human thing we have. We use it to teach, to trade, to argue, to joke, to pray, and to love. Language is how you took notes this morning, how you greeted your friend, and how you understand me right now.

Natural language is the language people speak and write every day: English, Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, French, Arabic, Pidgin, and thousands more. It is different from computer languages like Python, which are strict and formal. Natural language is messy, flexible, poetic, and full of traps, and that is exactly why teaching computers to work with it is one of the greatest challenges in artificial intelligence.

Natural language processing, usually shortened to NLP, is the field that teaches computers to understand, interpret, generate, and translate human language. When your phone suggests the next word while you type, that is NLP. When Google Translate turns English into Yoruba, that is NLP. When a bank's chatbot answers your question about your account balance, that is NLP. When a company reads thousands of customer reviews to learn what people like and hate, that is NLP.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand how computers break language into pieces, how they turn words into numbers, how they measure meaning, and how modern systems like translation engines and chatbots work. You will also see real-life projects you can build. Language is everywhere, so NLP skills open doors in every industry.

Let me warn you in advance: language is full of surprises, even for very smart systems. Part of the fun of NLP is watching computers fail at things that seem obvious to you, and then engineering clever solutions.

PART 2 - WHY LANGUAGE IS HARD FOR COMPUTERS

Before we study solutions, let us understand the problem. Language is hard for computers for several deep reasons.

First, ambiguity. One sentence can have many meanings. Consider the word bank. It can mean a financial bank where you keep money, or the bank of a river where you walk. Now consider the sentence: I saw the man with the telescope. Who has the telescope? You or the man? English allows both readings. Humans use context to choose the right meaning instantly. Computers must be taught to do this.

Second, context dependence. The word it refers to something mentioned earlier, and finding that something is called coreference resolution. In the sentence The bottle fell because it was too heavy, it refers to the bottle. In The bottle fell because it was too slippery, it might refer to the surface. Same word, different reference.

Third, sarcasm and tone. If someone writes, Oh great, another Monday, a human knows the person is unhappy. A naive computer might think the word great means good. Sentiment analysis must learn these subtleties.

Fourth, code-switching and multilingual reality. In Nigeria and across Africa, people mix languages in one conversation: English mixed with Pidgin, Yoruba words inside English sentences. A system trained only on formal English will struggle in real African markets and social media. Building NLP for our context means handling this richness.

Fifth, world knowledge. Understanding language often needs knowledge beyond the words. The sentence The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big needs to know that suitcases hold trophies and big things do not fit. Machines lack a lifetime of common-sense experience, so they stumble here.

Sixth, dialects, slang, and evolution. Language changes fast. New slang appears every year on social media. Spellings vary: colour and color, go and go-o for emphasis. Robust systems must adapt.

Understanding these difficulties makes you humble and clever. Every NLP system you build will face some of these traps. Your job is to anticipate them.

PART 3 - THE NLP PIPELINE: FROM TEXT TO MEANING

Let us walk through the standard steps of processing text.

Step one: text acquisition. Collect the text you need: documents, tweets, reviews, transcripts of speech, or news articles. Think about copyright and privacy while collecting.

Step two: preprocessing, cleaning the raw text. Convert everything to lowercase when capitalization does not matter. Remove noise like strange symbols or HTML tags. Handle emojis deliberately: in social media analysis, emojis carry strong sentiment, so you may convert them to words like smile or angry instead of deleting them.

Step three: tokenization, breaking text into pieces called tokens. Tokens are usually words or sub-words. The sentence AI is fun becomes the tokens AI, is, fun. But tokenization has traps: do we split don't into do and n't? Do we keep New York as one token or two? Different languages need different tokenizers. For Chinese and Yoruba written without clear word spacing, tokenization is itself a research problem.

Step four: normalization, putting words into standard forms. Stemming cuts word endings crudely: running becomes run, studies becomes studi. It is fast but rough. Lemmatization is smarter: it uses a dictionary to return studies to study and better to good. Prefer lemmatization when quality matters and speed allows.

Step five: stop-word removal. Common words like the, is, and at carry little meaning for many tasks, so some pipelines remove them. But be careful: in sentiment analysis, not is a tiny word that flips meaning completely. Never remove words blindly.

Step six: feature extraction, turning tokens into numbers. This is the bridge between language and mathematics, and it is so important that I will give it a full part of its own.

Step seven: modeling. Apply machine learning or deep learning to the numerical features for your task: classification, translation, generation, and so on.

Step eight: evaluation and deployment. Measure with appropriate metrics, improve, deploy, and monitor.

This pipeline is your roadmap. Master each station, and you can build any text system.

PART 4 - TURNING WORDS INTO NUMBERS

Computers eat numbers, not letters. So how do we turn words into numbers?

The oldest trick is the bag of words. We list the vocabulary of our dataset, and each document becomes a vector of word counts. Imagine the vocabulary has only five words: good, bad, food, service, price. The review Good food, good service becomes the vector 2, 0, 1, 1, 0: two good, zero bad, one food, one service, zero price. Simple and effective for many tasks. Notice the limitation: bag of words throws away word order. Good food, bad service and Bad food, good service produce the same counts, though their meanings differ.

TF-IDF improves on raw counts. It stands for term frequency times inverse document frequency. The idea is elegant: give higher weight to words that are frequent in one document but rare across all documents. The word the appears everywhere and gets low weight. The word malaria in one medical document gets high weight. TF-IDF helps models focus on informative words.

Then came the revolution: word embeddings. Instead of counting, we represent each word as a list of numbers, a vector, in such a way that similar words have similar vectors. In a good embedding space, king minus man plus woman lands near queen. Words about cooking cluster together; words about finance cluster together. Famous methods include Word2Vec and GloVe. Modern embeddings are even contextual: the word bank gets different vectors in river bank and bank account, because the surrounding words change the meaning.

Sentence and document embeddings extend this idea, turning whole sentences or paragraphs into single vectors. This powers semantic search: finding documents with similar meaning even when they use different words.

The practical lesson: numbers are the language of models, and good representations are half the victory in NLP. Spend real effort on how you represent text.

PART 5 - CLASSIC NLP TASKS AND HOW THEY WORK

Let us tour the classic tasks.

Sentiment analysis decides whether a text is positive, negative, or neutral. Businesses analyze product reviews and social media to hear their customers. Practical example: a restaurant in Ibadan collects Google reviews. A sentiment model summarizes that eighty percent of comments praise the jollof rice but many complain about slow service on weekends. The owner then adds weekend staff. Data becomes decision.

Text classification sorts documents into categories: spam versus not spam, news into sports, politics, or health, support tickets into billing, technical, or sales. A company routing emails to the right department saves hours daily.

Named entity recognition, NER, finds names in text: people, places, organizations, dates, and money. From the sentence The Bank of Industry funded a factory in Kano in March, a good NER system extracts organization, location, and date. This powers information extraction from news and reports.

Information extraction goes further, pulling structured facts from unstructured text: who did what to whom, where, and when. Search engines and business intelligence tools rely on this.

Machine translation converts text between languages. Google Translate and similar tools use massive neural models trained on millions of parallel sentences. Translation is not just word swapping; it must keep grammar, tone, and meaning. Translating idioms is famously hard: when English says it is raining cats and dogs, a literal Yoruba translation would confuse everyone.

Question answering gives direct answers to questions, from search engines to reading comprehension systems that read a passage and answer questions about it.

Text summarization shortens long documents into key points: extractive summarization picks existing important sentences; abstractive summarization writes new concise sentences. Busy executives and students both love summaries.

Each task has real economic value. Choosing which to learn first depends on your interests and the problems around you.

PART 6 - THE DEEP LEARNING AND TRANSFORMER ERA

Around 2013 to 2017, NLP was transformed by deep learning. Recurrent neural networks processed words one after another, keeping a memory of what came before. Long short-term memory networks, LSTMs, improved this memory to handle longer sentences. They powered early neural translation and chatbots.

Then in 2017 came the transformer architecture, introduced in a paper called Attention Is All You Need. Transformers changed everything. The key idea is attention: instead of processing strictly word by word, the model looks at the whole sentence at once and learns which words matter to which other words. In the sentence The animal did not cross the street because it was too tired, attention helps the model link it to animal rather than street.

Attention computes a relevance score between every pair of words in the input. These scores guide the model to focus on important context. It is like a student who, while reading one word, glances at every other related word in the sentence to grasp the full meaning.

On top of transformers rose large language models, LLMs: BERT for deep understanding of context, GPT-style models for fluent generation, T5 for many tasks at once. These models are pretrained on huge text collections from the internet and books, learning grammar, facts, and styles. Then they are fine-tuned for specific jobs, like legal document analysis or customer support.

Generative AI, systems that write text, code, poetry, and summaries, is powered by these models. Chatbots today can draft emails, explain concepts, and translate languages. They are astonishingly fluent, but they also have a flaw worth remembering: they can generate confident nonsense, hallucination. Fluency is not truth. In critical uses, always verify model output with reliable sources and human judgment.

For you as engineers, transformers are not magic; they are matrices, attention scores, and training tricks at scale. Your future courses on neural networks and deep learning will open the hood. Today, remember the core idea: attention lets models weigh context.

PART 7 - REAL-LIFE NLP IN AFRICA AND THE WORLD

Let me ground NLP in your reality.

Customer service. Banks and telecom companies deploy chatbots that answer common questions: how to reset a PIN, how to check balance, how to buy data. A good bot handles seventy percent of routine queries, freeing human agents for complex problems. Building a reliable banking bot requires careful handling of account security and clear escalation to humans.

Health information. NLP systems analyze clinical notes to flag drug interactions or extract symptoms from reports. Community health apps let people describe symptoms in simple language and suggest whether to seek care. In multilingual regions, voice interfaces in local languages help people who do not write in English.

Agriculture advisory. Farmers ask questions by voice: what fertilizer for maize after rain? NLP systems retrieve trusted agricultural advice and reply in local languages. Text analysis of farmer forums reveals emerging pest problems before official reports.

Education. Essay grading assistants give feedback on grammar and structure. Reading tools help children learn pronunciation. Translation tools let students read global knowledge in their mother tongue.

Media and government. News agencies auto-transcribe interviews. Governments analyze public feedback on policies. Fact-checking tools scan claims on social media and match them against trusted databases.

Social good and safety. NLP helps detect hate speech, scam messages, and misinformation. Nigeria faces waves of fake job offers and fraudulent SMS; classification models can flag suspicious messages for review. The same technology must protect free expression, so balance and oversight are essential.

Creative uses. Lyrics writing assistants, poetry generators, and story tools help artists brainstorm. Local-language content generation supports cultural preservation: documenting proverbs, folktales, and history in digital form.

The common thread: language is the interface between people and services. Every service that can listen and speak well reaches more people. That is the social and economic power of NLP.

PART 8 - PRACTICAL PROJECT: SENTIMENT ANALYSIS OF PRODUCT REVIEWS

Let me guide you through a classic first project: sentiment analysis of product reviews.

Goal: classify each review as positive or negative so a business can track customer satisfaction.

Step one, collect data. Public datasets of reviews exist for movies, phones, and restaurants. You can also collect local data, for example reviews of businesses in your city, respecting privacy and terms of service.

Step two, prepare the data. Clean the text, tokenize, and lemmatize. Keep negation words like not. Convert text to numbers with TF-IDF, or use embeddings for a stronger model.

Step three, split the data. Eighty percent training, twenty percent testing, stratified so both sets have similar positive and negative balance.

Step four, train a baseline. Logistic regression with TF-IDF is a superb baseline. It is fast, interpretable, and surprisingly strong. Train and evaluate.

Step five, evaluate. Look at accuracy, but also precision and recall per class, and read the confusion matrix. Then read misclassified reviews manually. You will learn more from twenty wrong cases than from any score.

Step six, improve. Try n-grams, which are pairs of words like not good, to capture phrases. Try a support vector machine. Try a small transformer model with transfer learning if you have computing resources.

Step seven, deploy simply. A small web page where a user pastes a review and sees positive or negative with a confidence score. Streamlit in Python makes this easy.

Step eight, analyze. Aggregate predictions over hundreds of reviews: what percentage is positive? Which words appear most in negative reviews? Present findings in a simple dashboard. Now you are not just building a model; you are delivering business insight.

Common pitfalls to avoid. Beware of imbalance: if ninety percent of reviews are positive, a lazy model looks accurate but fails at finding complaints. Beware of leakage: words like the product name may correlate with labels in your sample but mean nothing in the real world. And beware of domain shift: a model trained on movie reviews may fail on phone reviews; fine-tune on in-domain data.

PART 9 - CHATBOTS AND DIALOG SYSTEMS

Let me open the hood of chatbots.

Rule-based chatbots follow decision trees: if the user says password, reply with reset instructions. They are simple and safe but rigid. Retrieval-based chatbots match the user's question to a prepared answer from a knowledge base, choosing the closest match. Generative chatbots use language models to compose new replies sentence by sentence.

Modern customer service systems are hybrids. They use rules for security-sensitive actions like money transfers, retrieval for frequent questions, and generation for friendly conversation. They keep conversation state: remembering that you said your account number two turns ago.

Design principles for good chatbots. First, be clear about identity: tell users they are talking to a machine. Second, set scope: say what the bot can and cannot do. Third, fail gracefully: when confused, say so and offer a human agent. Fourth, protect privacy: never ask for full passwords or PINs. Fifth, test with real users and improve continuously.

Voice adds speech recognition for input and text-to-speech for output, plus challenges like accents, background noise at a market stall, and code-switching between English and local languages. Designing voice systems for our context is an open opportunity for engineers like you.

PART 10 - ETHICS AND RESPONSIBILITY IN NLP

With great language power comes great responsibility.

Bias in language models is real. Models trained on internet text absorb stereotypes about gender, ethnicity, and professions. They might associate nursing with women and engineering with men. We must test for bias and use debiasing techniques, balanced data, and careful evaluation.

Misinformation and deepfakes. Text generators can mass-produce fake news and scam messages. Voice cloning can imitate family members for fraud. As engineers, we support detection tools, watermarking, and verification systems.

Privacy. Chat logs and health conversations are deeply personal. Anonymize data, secure storage, and be transparent about what is stored. Regulations like the Nigeria Data Protection Regulation and GDPR apply directly.

Copyright and consent. Training on creators' work without permission is contested legally and ethically. When building datasets, respect licenses and give credit.

Over-reliance. Students and professionals who let models do all their thinking lose skill. Use NLP tools to assist judgment, not replace it. In exams, in reports, in decisions, the human mind must remain the final editor.

Environmental cost. Training giant models consumes significant energy. Choose efficient models, reuse pretrained ones, and right-size solutions.

A final thought: language carries culture and identity. NLP systems for African languages can preserve heritage and include billions of people in the digital economy. Work on Yoruba, Hausa, and Igbo NLP is not only technical; it is cultural service. I encourage you to consider it for your projects.

PART 11 - GLOSSARY AND RECAP

Let us fix the vocabulary in memory.

Natural language: everyday human language, spoken or written.

NLP: the field of teaching computers to work with human language.

Tokenization: splitting text into words or sub-words called tokens.

Stemming: crudely cutting word endings to their root form.

Lemmatization: reducing words to dictionary forms using vocabulary and grammar.

Stop words: very common words that carry little meaning for some tasks.

Bag of words: representing text by word counts, ignoring order.

TF-IDF: weighting words by how frequent they are in one document and how rare across documents.

Word embedding: representing a word as a vector where similar meanings have similar vectors.

Sentiment analysis: detecting positive, negative, or neutral opinion in text.

Named entity recognition: finding names of people, places, organizations, dates, and similar entities.

Machine translation: automatically converting text between languages.

Text summarization: condensing long text into shorter key content.

Token: the basic unit after tokenization.

Attention: a mechanism that lets a model weigh how important each word is to others.

Transformer: the modern neural architecture built around attention.

Large language model: a big neural model pretrained on vast text that can perform many language tasks.

Fine-tuning: adapting a pretrained model to a specific task with focused data.

Hallucination: when a language model confidently generates false or invented content.

Code-switching: mixing two or more languages within one conversation.

To recap. We saw why language is hard: ambiguity, context, sarcasm, and diversity. We built the NLP pipeline from acquisition to modeling. We turned words into numbers with bag of words, TF-IDF, and embeddings. We toured classic tasks: sentiment, classification, NER, translation, QA, and summarization. We met the transformer era and attention. We grounded everything in real-life applications across Africa and the world. We planned a sentiment project, opened chatbots, and faced the ethics of language technology.

Second, collect ten product reviews from your daily life experience and label them positive or negative yourself, then note which words drove your labels. PART 12 - A DEEPER PRACTICAL WALKTHROUGH: BUILDING A VOICE FAQ BOT

Let me add a detailed practical walkthrough that many of you can attempt this semester: building a simple voice FAQ bot for a campus department or a small business.

Scenario: the department of student affairs receives the same questions every day. What are the school fees deadlines? How do I apply for hostel accommodation? Where is the bursary office? When does registration close? Staff answer these hundreds of times. You will build a bot that answers them.

Step one, gather the knowledge base. Interview staff and collect the actual questions and official answers in a simple spreadsheet: question, answer, category. Twenty to fifty entries are enough to start. Keep answers short and clear, and have staff approve every answer for accuracy. This curation step is where trust is built or lost.

Step two, build intent recognition. Each row in your spreadsheet is an intent: fee_deadline, hostel_application, office_location, registration_window. Train a small text classifier that maps an incoming student's question to the right intent. Start with TF-IDF and logistic regression; it trains in seconds and works decently with little data. Later, fine-tune a small transformer if you have the computing resources.

Step three, set a confidence threshold. If the classifier is not confident, the bot must not guess. It should say, I am not sure I understood. Please rephrase, or I can connect you to a staff member. This single design choice separates bots people trust from bots people abandon.

Step four, add speech for accessibility. Many students prefer to speak, especially on low-cost phones. Use a speech recognition service or an offline model to convert voice to text, run the same intent pipeline, and reply with text and synthesized speech. Test with Nigerian accents and with code-switched questions like When school fees deadline go be? A robust bot handles these, and testing with real users will reveal what to add to training data.

Step five, keep conversation state simple. The bot remembers the current topic for a follow-up or two: And when does late registration end? follows from the fees topic. Simple state tracking is enough for FAQ bots; do not build a complex brain before the basics work.

Step six, measure and improve. Log every question the bot failed to answer. Each failure becomes next week's training example. After one month of use, the bot's success rate rises dramatically. This is the flywheel of NLP improvement: real usage creates better data creates a better system.

Step seven, deploy and govern. Host it on a cheap server or on a messaging platform your students already use. Publish the privacy policy: what is logged, how long it is kept, who can read it. Give staff an admin sheet to update answers without programming. A bot that staff can maintain is a bot that survives after you graduate.

What this project teaches: data curation, intent classification, confidence handling, speech integration, state tracking, iterative improvement, and governance. It solves a real annoyance for real people. Projects like this are exactly what final-year defenses and employers love to see.

PART 13 - MORE STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Let me add more questions to strengthen your exam preparation and interview readiness.

Question seven: What is the difference between stemming and lemmatization, and when would you choose each? Model answer: Stemming crudely chops endings and is fast but rough; lemmatization returns words to dictionary forms using grammar and vocabulary and is more accurate. Choose stemming for speed on large informal text like tweets where roughness is acceptable, and lemmatization for quality-focused tasks like document classification and question answering.

Question eight: Why do word embeddings help more than raw word counts for many tasks? Model answer: Counts treat every word as unrelated and ignore similarity. Embeddings place similar words near each other, so the model can generalize from seen words to unseen but similar words. A model that has seen good may handle excellent if their vectors are close. Embeddings also capture nuance that counts cannot.

Question nine: A chatbot confidently gives wrong fee deadlines to students. What went wrong and how do you fix it? Model answer: It likely hallucinated or answered from outdated knowledge with no confidence check. Fixes include grounding answers in an approved knowledge base with retrieval instead of free generation, adding a confidence threshold with human escalation, versioning answers with dates, and staff review of sensitive information.

Question ten: How would you evaluate a machine translation system for English to Yoruba? Model answer: Automatic metrics like BLEU give rough overlap scores with reference translations, but human evaluation is essential for meaning, tone, and idiom handling. Test on everyday domains relevant to users: market language, health instructions, and school content. Include bilingual speakers in review, and measure user satisfaction and task success.

Question eleven: What is the danger of removing stop words blindly in sentiment analysis? Model answer: Words like not, very, and but carry crucial meaning. Removing not turns I do not like this product into I like this product, reversing the sentiment. Pipelines must preserve negation and intensifiers when meaning depends on them.

Question twelve: Explain hallucination in large language models and give one engineering mitigation. Model answer: Hallucination is fluent but false or invented output, because the model predicts plausible text rather than verified truth. Mitigations include retrieval-augmented generation, where the model quotes from trusted documents, plus output verification, citations, and human review for high-stakes content.

PART 16 - ADDITIONAL PRACTICAL SCENARIOS FOR NLP

Let me strengthen this lecture with more practical scenarios you will meet in industry and in your projects.

Scenario one: spam and scam message detection for a telecom. Millions of SMS and WhatsApp-style messages flow daily, and many are fraudulent: fake job offers, fake prizes, impersonation of banks. An NLP system classifies messages as suspicious or safe using text patterns, sender behavior, and link analysis. The practical challenge is balance: blocking real emergency messages is harmful, so thresholds are tuned with care and a reporting channel exists for false alarms. Engineers also watch for evolving tactics, since fraudsters change wording quickly. Regular retraining with fresh samples keeps the system effective.

Scenario two: automatic transcription of lecture recordings. Students with slow internet benefit from searchable text of lectures. A speech-to-text model transcribes audio, then a text pipeline cleans formatting and adds punctuation. The transcripts become study aids, accessible content for students with hearing impairment, and searchable archives. Accuracy tests must include accents, overlapping speech, and technical vocabulary like kinematics or gradient descent, adding those terms to custom dictionaries.

Scenario three: summarizing government policy documents. Citizens rarely read long documents. An abstractive summarization tool produces a one-page plain-language summary with key dates and obligations highlighted. The engineering lesson here is faithfulness: summaries must not distort meaning. Systems that quote source sentences for key claims build more trust than free paraphrasing alone.

Scenario four: multilingual community feedback analysis. A local government collects feedback from markets and town halls in Yoruba, Pidgin, and English. NLP clusters the feedback into themes like waste management, water supply, and road repairs, and tracks sentiment over months. Leaders see emerging problems early. This project blends translation, clustering, and sentiment analysis in one pipeline, and it is a strong final-year project idea.

Scenario five: plagiarism and academic integrity checking. Comparing student essays against a corpus and against each other using text similarity measures flags likely copy-paste cases for human review. The system must handle paraphrasing and quotations fairly, treating its output as evidence for discussion, never as automatic judgment.

Scenario six: voice-based market price information for farmers. Farmers call a number and ask in their own language, what is the price of maize today? The system retrieves verified price data and speaks it back. It combines speech recognition, intent understanding, database retrieval, and speech synthesis. Projects like this show how NLP bridges information gaps in rural economies.

Notice how every scenario mixes several NLP skills: classification, extraction, retrieval, generation, speech, and evaluation. Real systems are soups, not single ingredients. Build your skills as a connected toolkit, and you will be ready to cook any dish a client, community, or company needs. PART 17 - CLOSING REFLECTIONS FOR NLP LEARNERS

As this lecture closes, let me give you a reflection that ties everything together. Natural language processing sits at a special crossroads: it is engineering, because we build systems; it is linguistics, because we study language; it is psychology, because we model understanding; and it is social science, because language shapes society. When you work on NLP, you touch all four worlds at once.

Practical habit number one: read language with new eyes. When you notice ambiguity in a headline, imagine how a classifier would fail on it. When you hear code-switching in a market, think about training data diversity. When a voice assistant misunderstands you, trace which pipeline stage failed: the speech recognition, the intent, or the answer retrieval.

Practical habit number two: collect small datasets continuously. Screenshot funny autocorrects. Save examples of ambiguous sentences. Record family members saying the same word in different accents. Over one year, you will own a personal corpus that no one else has, and your final-year project will thank you.

Practical habit number three: care about inclusion. Over five hundred languages are spoken in Nigeria alone, yet the digital world mostly serves a handful. Every model you help build for Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, or Nigerian Pidgin widens the circle of who technology serves. That is engineering with purpose.

Learn it deeply, use it wisely, and build it inclusively. Thank you for your attention. I will see you in the next lecture.
