PART 1 - WELCOME: HOW COMPUTERS LEARN TO SEE

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 104, Computer Vision. You open your eyes and instantly see a room full of chairs, faces, and light. Seeing feels effortless to you. For a computer, seeing is one of the hardest problems in engineering. That is what makes computer vision so exciting.

Computer vision is the field that teaches computers to understand images and videos. Not just store them, not just display them, but understand them: what objects are present, where they are, what is moving, and even what is happening.

Why does it matter? Because about eighty percent of the information humans receive comes through vision. Machines that can see unlock countless applications. Your phone's face unlock sees your face. Self-driving cars see lanes, signs, and pedestrians. Doctors get help reading X-rays. Farmers use cameras to find sick plants. Factories inspect products at superhuman speed.

In this course, you will learn how images become numbers, how computers find edges and objects, how modern vision systems recognize faces and read text, and where vision systems still fail. By the end, you will think about images the way a vision engineer does.

PART 2 - HOW A DIGITAL IMAGE IS BORN

Let us start at the beginning: how does a digital image exist?

A camera works like a simplified eye. Light from the world passes through a lens and lands on a sensor. The sensor is a grid of tiny light detectors called pixels. Each pixel measures how much light hits it. That measurement becomes a number.

For a grayscale image, each pixel is one number: zero means black, 255 means white, and numbers in between are shades of gray. For a color image, each pixel typically has three numbers: how much red, how much green, and how much blue. This is the RGB system. All the beautiful photos you see are just grids of numbers to a computer. An image from a modern phone might have 12 million pixels, each with three values. That is 36 million numbers for one photo.

Resolution means how many pixels the image has. Higher resolution gives more detail but needs more computing power. A 4K video frame has about 8 million pixels, and video shows 30 or 60 frames every second. Vision systems must process mountains of data at high speed. Efficiency matters.

Let me give you a practical feel. Take a small 100 by 100 pixel grayscale image. That is 10,000 numbers. Now imagine a machine learning model learning patterns in those numbers to tell cats from dogs. The model never sees fur or whiskers as you do. It sees a grid of brightness values. Its job is to find patterns in numbers that mean "cat" or "dog." Keeping this truth in mind will make every vision concept easier.

PART 3 - LOW-LEVEL VISION: PIXELS, EDGES, AND FILTERS

The first job of vision is to find simple structures: points, lines, edges, and textures.

An edge is a place where brightness changes sharply, like the border between a dark bottle and a white table. Edges carry the shape of objects. The famous Canny edge detector, and simpler filters like Sobel, find these brightness changes by comparing each pixel with its neighbors. Mathematically, this is about measuring the gradient, the rate of change of brightness.

A filter, also called a kernel, is a small grid of numbers that slides over the image. At every position, it multiplies and adds values to produce an output. A blur filter averages neighboring pixels, smoothing the image. A sharpening filter exaggerates differences. An edge filter highlights borders. You can try this in any photo editing app: blurring and sharpening are filters in action.

Filters can also remove noise. Images taken in low light look grainy. A median filter can clean salt-and-pepper noise while keeping edges crisp. Every serious vision system begins with such pre-processing, because clean input gives cleaner decisions.

Real-life practical. Imagine you are building a system that counts people entering a shop through a door. First, you might convert the image to grayscale to reduce data. Then you smooth out noise. Then you find edges of people. Then you count blob-like shapes crossing a line. This classic pipeline, cleaning then edges then shapes, ran many industrial systems before deep learning arrived, and it still works beautifully on simple tasks with tiny computers.

PART 4 - MID-LEVEL VISION: SEGMENTATION AND FEATURES

Once we find edges, we group pixels into meaningful regions. This is called segmentation.

Simple segmentation might use a threshold: pixels brighter than 128 become white, darker become black. This is how document scanners turn paper into clean black-and-white text images. Color-based segmentation can isolate ripe red tomatoes against green leaves. Region-growing methods start from seed pixels and absorb similar neighbors.

Then there is feature detection. Features are distinctive points or patterns, like the corner of a book, the tip of a nose, or a unique texture. The Harris corner detector finds corners. SIFT, SURF, and ORB find robust key points that survive changes in size, rotation, and lighting.

Why do features matter? Because they let us compare images. If two photos of the same building are taken from different angles, matching their features tells us they show the same place. This powers panorama stitching in your phone camera. It also helps robots localize themselves by recognizing landmarks.

Optical flow is another mid-level idea: it estimates how pixels move between frames. When you watch a video and see motion arrows over moving objects, that is optical flow. It is used in video stabilization, gesture recognition, and self-driving analysis.

Practical example: building an automatic document scanner with your phone. The system finds the four corners of the paper using edges and lines, corrects the perspective so the paper looks flat, enhances the contrast, and then reads the text. This feature pipeline is in apps students use every day. Understanding it means you understand real vision engineering.

PART 5 - HIGH-LEVEL VISION: RECOGNIZING WHAT IS THERE

Now the big questions: what objects are in the image, and who or what do they belong to?

Image classification says what is in the image overall: this is a photo of a dog. Object detection says what is present and where: there is a dog at these coordinates and a bicycle over there, each with a box and a label. Semantic segmentation labels every single pixel: sky, road, car, person. Instance segmentation goes further and separates each individual object: person number one, person number two.

Face recognition identifies or verifies people from faces. The system detects a face, aligns it, computes a numerical description called an embedding, and compares it against stored embeddings. Your phone does this in a fraction of a second.

Optical character recognition, OCR, reads printed or handwritten text from images. Banks use it to read checks. Logistics companies read shipping labels. Students scan notes into editable documents.

Scene understanding interprets the whole picture: a kitchen, a football pitch, a busy market street. This helps robots and self-driving cars predict what to expect.

For decades, people built these systems by designing features by hand. Then deep learning changed everything. Convolutional neural networks, which you will study in Neural Networks and Deep Learning courses, learn features directly from data. They look at thousands of labeled images and discover useful patterns on their own. Since 2012, deep learning has led every major vision competition and now powers production systems worldwide.

PART 6 - CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORKS: THE VISION REVOLUTION

Let me introduce the hero of modern vision: the convolutional neural network, or CNN.

Recall that an image is a grid of numbers. A CNN slides small learned filters over the image and produces feature maps. Early layers learn simple features: edges, colors, and textures. Middle layers combine them into parts: eyes, wheels, leaves, windows. Deep layers combine parts into whole objects: faces, cars, trees, houses. It is like building understanding step by step from bricks to rooms to houses.

After convolution layers, the network reduces size with pooling layers, keeping only the strongest signals. Finally, fully connected layers make the final decision: cat with 92 percent confidence, dog with 6 percent.

Training a CNN uses supervised learning. You need many labeled images. The network makes a guess, measures its error with a loss function, and adjusts its internal numbers through backpropagation. Repeat thousands of times, and accuracy climbs.

Famous architectures worth remembering: AlexNet, which started the revolution in 2012; VGG, with its simple deep design; GoogLeNet and ResNet, which trained very deep networks; and modern efficient networks that run on phones.

Transfer learning is a powerful practical trick. Instead of training from scratch, you take a network already trained on millions of images and fine-tune it on your small dataset. With just a few hundred images of, say, cassava leaves, you can build a working disease detector. Transfer learning is the great equalizer for students and small teams.

PART 7 - REAL-LIFE APPLICATIONS, DEEP AND WIDE

Let us tour the applications with practical detail.

Healthcare. CNNs read X-rays to suggest pneumonia, analyze retinal images for diabetes damage, and count malaria parasites in microscope slides. These systems do not replace doctors. They act as tireless assistants that flag suspicious regions for expert review. In areas with few radiologists, such assistance saves lives.

Autonomous vehicles. The car's vision system detects lanes, traffic lights, signs, vehicles, cyclists, and pedestrians. It estimates distances and predicts movements. Vision here is safety critical, which is why cars also use radar and LiDAR alongside cameras.

Agriculture. Drones photograph fields. Vision models map plant health, count seedlings, detect weeds, and estimate yield. A practical project idea for this program: build a phone-based cassava disease classifier using transfer learning and a small dataset collected from local farms.

Manufacturing. Vision systems inspect products at high speed: finding cracks in tiles, checking fill levels in bottles, verifying labels and codes. A camera on a production line can inspect 60 bottles per second without fatigue.

Retail and finance. Cameras estimate shelf stock. Banks use face verification for secure mobile payments. Stores analyze queues and foot traffic to improve service.

Security and public safety. Face recognition helps find missing people and control building access. But this power carries serious responsibility: mass surveillance and misidentification risks demand strict rules and oversight.

Accessibility. Apps describe scenes aloud for people with visual impairment: "A red bus is approaching on your left." Vision becomes a pair of digital eyes.

Entertainment and creativity. Filters on social media track your face and add effects. Modern AI can generate images from text, edit photos with a single instruction, and restore old photographs.

Every one of these applications follows the workflow we learned in machine learning: define, collect data, train, test honestly, deploy, and monitor. The difference is that the data is pixels.

PART 8 - CHALLENGES: WHY VISION IS STILL HARD

Computers see well in demos but stumble in the real world. Let us study the failure modes, because knowing them is what separates professionals from hobbyists.

Lighting changes. A face recognition system that works at noon may fail at dusk or under flickering fluorescent lights. Good systems are trained with varied lighting or normalize brightness first.

Occlusion. Objects are often partly hidden. A pedestrian behind a parked car is still a pedestrian. A face behind a mask or a hand is harder to recognize. Systems must handle partial views.

Viewpoint and scale. A chair looks very different from above, from the side, or when tiny in the distance. Models need diverse training views.

Background clutter. Finding a single tool on a messy workbench is much harder than finding it on a clean table.

Adversarial attacks. Tiny, carefully designed changes to pixels, invisible to humans, can fool a model into seeing a panda as a gibbon or missing a stop sign. Safety-critical systems must be tested against such attacks.

Bias. If training data lacks diversity in skin tones, genders, or regions, the model will be less accurate for underrepresented groups. Famous commercial systems have shown higher error rates for dark-skinned women. This is unacceptable in real deployment. Test fairly across groups.

Compute cost. Video at 30 frames per second means 30 full analyses every second. Running deep models at high speed needs powerful hardware or clever optimization.

Data hunger. Deep models want lots of labeled images, and labeling is slow and costly. Techniques like data augmentation, flipping and rotating images to create variants, help squeeze more value from limited data.

The professional mindset: a vision system is only as good as its worst day. Test in rain and at night. Test with strange angles and dirty lenses. Plan for failure with confidence thresholds, meaning the system says "I am not sure" when it is not sure.

PART 9 - BUILDING A SIMPLE VISION PROJECT

Let me sketch your first hands-on project: a fruit ripeness checker. Imagine a small camera looking at bananas on a shelf and sorting them into green, ripe, and overripe.

Step one: define the goal and classes. Three classes: green, ripe, overripe.

Step two: collect data. Take 100 photos of bananas in each class with your phone under different lighting. Keep backgrounds varied.

Step three: clean and prepare. Crop images to focus on the fruit. Resize them to a standard size, say 224 by 224 pixels. Apply augmentation: flip, rotate slightly, adjust brightness.

Step four: choose a model. Use transfer learning with a small pretrained network. Freeze most layers and train only the last layers on your images.

Step five: train and validate. Use an 80-20 split. Watch accuracy and confusion between neighboring classes: green versus ripe is harder than green versus overripe.

Step six: test in the real world. Point the camera at real fruit in a market setting. Note failures honestly. Is the model confused by yellow lighting at dusk? Collect those cases and retrain.

Step seven: deploy simply. A phone app or a small laptop script that classifies in real time.

What you learn from this project is the full vision pipeline in miniature: data collection, cleaning, augmentation, transfer learning, honest testing, and deployment. And the result is useful: a shop owner can plan discounts on overripe stock, and a factory can automate sorting.

Tools for this journey: OpenCV for image processing, PyTorch or TensorFlow for models, Google Colab for free GPU computing, and Roboflow or LabelImg for labeling. All are student friendly.

PART 10 - VISION FOR ROBOTS: WHERE AI 102 MEETS AI 104

Since your degree combines AI and robotics, let me connect vision to robots.

A robot with a camera can do much more than a blind robot. It can find parts to pick, inspect welds, read labels, and navigate by seeing landmarks. Eye-in-hand cameras move with the gripper for precise final alignment. Fixed cameras watch the whole workspace. Both have design trade-offs in coverage and precision.

Calibration is the process of teaching the camera its own geometry: how pixels relate to real-world distances and angles. Without calibration, a robot cannot trust measurements from vision. This is a hands-on skill you will practice in labs.

Visual servoing means controlling robot motion directly from camera feedback, like you reaching for a cup while watching it continuously. It is a beautiful example of closed-loop control with vision as the sensor.

Depth perception gives robots 3D understanding. Stereo cameras use two views to triangulate distance, like your two eyes. Structured light projectors paint patterns on objects and read the distortion. Time-of-flight cameras measure how long light takes to bounce back. Each approach has cost and accuracy trade-offs.

Practical scenario: a mobile robot delivering medicines in a hospital. Vision reads corridor signs, recognizes doors, detects people to avoid them gently, and verifies the right room number before delivery. None of this is science fiction; such systems exist today. Your studies are preparing you to build them.

PART 11 - GLOSSARY AND FINAL RECAP

Let us seal the vocabulary.

Pixel: the smallest unit of an image, holding a brightness or color value.

Resolution: the number of pixels in an image, which determines detail.

Grayscale: an image using only shades of gray, one value per pixel.

RGB: a color system with red, green, and blue values per pixel.

Filter or kernel: a small grid of numbers that slides over an image to blur, sharpen, or find edges.

Edge: a boundary where brightness changes sharply, often outlining objects.

Segmentation: dividing an image into meaningful regions or labeling every pixel.

Feature: a distinctive pattern in an image, like a corner or texture, used for matching and recognition.

Classification: assigning one label to a whole image.

Object detection: finding and locating multiple objects with boxes and labels.

Optical flow: estimating motion of pixels between video frames.

OCR: reading printed or handwritten text from images.

CNN: a convolutional neural network, the leading model family for vision.

Training: adjusting a model's internal numbers using labeled images.

Transfer learning: starting from a pretrained model and adapting it to a new task.

Data augmentation: expanding a dataset with transformed versions of existing images.

Occlusion: when objects are partly hidden from view.

Adversarial attack: crafted inputs that fool a vision model.

Calibration: learning the mapping between pixels and real-world geometry.

Visual servoing: controlling robot motion using live camera feedback.

PART 12 - IMAGE FORMATS, COLOR SPACES, AND PRACTICAL ENGINEERING DETAILS

Let me add practical engineering knowledge that working vision engineers use daily.

File formats matter. A JPEG file compresses images by throwing away small details the eye rarely notices. This keeps files small but can blur fine texture. A PNG file keeps every pixel exactly and supports transparency, which is great for logos and masks. RAW files store minimally processed sensor data and give photographers and engineers maximum flexibility for later processing. For training datasets, choose formats and quality settings carefully: if you compress images too heavily, you may destroy the very texture clues your model needs.

Color spaces matter too. RGB is what cameras capture and screens display, but it is not the only way to describe color. HSV separates hue, the color itself, from saturation, the intensity of the color, and value, the brightness. Engineers love HSV for color-based segmentation because it is robust to shadows. A banana looks yellow in HSV across many lighting conditions more reliably than in raw RGB. Grayscale discards color and keeps brightness, which is often enough for edge detection and saves computation.

Bit depth matters. A standard image uses 8 bits per channel, giving 256 levels per color. Medical and satellite images may use 16 bits or more, giving thousands of levels of subtle detail. When you process such images, standard tools can silently destroy precision. Know your data's bit depth before you write code.

Resizing is not free of risk. Shrinking a high-resolution image to a tiny one can erase small objects, like a distant traffic sign or a tiny tumor in a scan. Always resize consistently for training and deployment, and prefer to crop around the object of interest rather than squash the whole scene.

Practical lab habit: when you start any vision project, spend the first hour just looking at data. Show yourself fifty images. Check labels. Find weird cases: broken images, wrong labels, watermarks, black borders, extreme lighting. This one habit prevents more wasted weeks than any other practice. Professional vision engineers are detectives of their own datasets.

PART 13 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: Why does a computer need a filter to find edges when edges are obvious to humans? Model answer: A computer only sees numbers in a grid. Brightness changes are just number changes. A filter computes how fast numbers change between neighboring pixels, which reveals edges quantitatively and consistently at high speed, without human intuition.

Question two: What is the difference between image classification and object detection? Model answer: Classification gives one label for the whole image, such as dog. Detection finds every object of interest, gives each a bounding box and a label, such as dog here and bicycle there. Classification answers what; detection answers what and where.

Question three: A face recognition system works well in the lab but fails at a busy market entrance at night. Give three likely causes and fixes. Model answer: Poor lighting at night; fix with infrared illumination or training with night images. Faces at angles or partly covered; fix with diverse training data including masks and side views. Camera motion or blur; fix with faster shutter, stabilization, and blur augmentation during training.

Question four: How does transfer learning make vision projects cheaper? Model answer: Instead of training a huge network from scratch with millions of images and heavy computing, we reuse a network already trained on big datasets. We fine-tune it on our small set. This cuts required data, time, and cost dramatically while keeping high accuracy.

Question five: A self-driving car vision system must never miss a pedestrian. Which metric matters most and why? Model answer: Recall for the pedestrian class matters most, because a miss, a false negative, can cost a life. Precision matters too to avoid unnecessary braking, but the safety priority is catching every pedestrian, even at the cost of some false alarms.

Question six: What is an adversarial attack on a vision model? Model answer: It is a deliberately crafted input with tiny pixel changes, invisible to humans, designed to make the model give a wrong answer, like misreading a stop sign. Defenses include adversarial training, input checks, and sensor fusion so that one fooled sensor is caught by others.

Answer these aloud in your own words before reading the models. That struggle is where learning happens.

PART 14 - CASE STUDY: A VISION SYSTEM FOR A BOTTLING LINE

Let me walk you through a realistic industrial case so you can feel how vision projects unfold in production.

A beverage company fills thousands of bottles per hour. Defects cost money: cracked bottles leak, missing caps spoil product, wrong labels damage brand trust. The company asks your team to build an automated inspection station with a camera above the conveyor belt.

Step one, define the inspection tasks clearly. Task A: detect cracks in glass. Task B: verify the cap is present and properly seated. Task C: read the printed date code with OCR and confirm it is correct and legible. Each task is a different vision problem: anomaly detection for cracks, detection plus geometry checks for caps, and character recognition for codes.

Step two, design the imaging. Lighting is half the battle. For glass cracks, dark-field lighting makes scratches glow against dark background. For cap inspection, a diffuse dome light gives even illumination without harsh reflections. For date codes, a focused bright light gives high contrast for OCR. The camera choice follows the task: resolution must be fine enough that a hairline crack spans several pixels; frame rate must cover every bottle at full belt speed; the lens must give the right field of view with low distortion.

Step three, build the mechanical trigger. An encoder on the belt tells the controller exactly when each bottle is in position, so the camera fires at precisely the right moment. Motion blur is controlled with short strobe lighting. This marriage of mechanics and vision is the essence of machine vision engineering.

Step four, develop algorithms. Start with classical methods: thresholding and edge analysis for cap position, template matching for label alignment, and OCR with preprocessing for codes. If classical methods stumble on cracks, add a small anomaly detection model trained on many images of good bottles; anything unusual is flagged. Run initial offline tests on collected images before live testing.

Step five, integrate decisions. The system must act in milliseconds: a reject arm pushes flawed bottles into a bin within a fixed delay from the camera. Timing calculations with belt speed are critical. The system logs every rejection with its image for quality audits.

Step six, validate in production. Test with deliberate defects: bottles with cracks, missing caps, smeared codes. Measure false rejects, good bottles wrongly thrown away, and false accepts, defects wrongly passed. Tune thresholds to balance quality against waste. False rejects cost material; false accepts cost reputation. Agree targets with management before tuning.

Step seven, support and maintain. Dust on lenses, aging lights, and belt vibrations change imaging over time. Design for easy cleaning, calibration checks, and alarm when image quality drifts. Train operators to understand alerts.

What this case teaches is the full machine vision picture: physics of lighting, optics, mechanics, algorithms, real-time integration, statistics of errors, and human factors of operation. Every step matters; skipping lighting design or validation is how vision projects fail. When you later take the Machine Vision course, you will meet these steps again with deeper tools.

PART 15 - CAREERS AND THE FUTURE OF COMPUTER VISION

Let me sketch careers and the road ahead, so you can steer your studies deliberately.

Machine vision engineer in manufacturing: designing inspection stations like our case study, combining cameras, lighting, and software. Strong demand exists in food, pharmaceuticals, automotive, and packaging industries.

Robotics perception engineer: giving robots sight for grasping, navigation, and quality checks, often using ROS and depth cameras. This role sits exactly at the heart of your AI and robotics degree.

Medical imaging analyst: building tools that help radiologists read scans faster and more accurately. Requires careful validation and regulatory awareness.

Autonomous vehicle perception engineer: detecting lanes, signs, and road users, with safety-critical standards. Competitive field, rewarding work.

Computer vision researcher: pushing boundaries in labs and universities, publishing new methods, and creating the tools of tomorrow.

Technical founder: building vision products for local markets, such as agricultural inspection apps, retail analytics for small shops, or identity verification for fintech. Africa has problems that vision can solve, and local understanding is your unfair advantage.

The future trends to watch. Vision and language are merging: systems that answer questions about images and video. Edge computing brings powerful vision to cheap cameras and phones. Self-supervised learning reduces the need for labels. Neural radiance fields and 3D Gaussian splatting reconstruct 3D scenes from photos, powering next-generation mapping and virtual reality. Event cameras that sense change at microsecond speed open new possibilities in high-speed robotics. And trustworthy AI, vision systems that explain and resist attacks, will be a professional specialty.

Whichever path calls you, the recipe is the same: master the fundamentals of pixels, features, and models; practice on real images; and never stop asking what the numbers are really doing.
