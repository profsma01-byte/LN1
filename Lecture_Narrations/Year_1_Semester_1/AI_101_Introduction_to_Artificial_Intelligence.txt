PART 2 - WHAT IS ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE?

So, what is artificial intelligence? Let us break the words apart. "Artificial" means something made by people, not by nature. "Intelligence" means the ability to learn, understand, think, and solve problems. Put the two words together, and artificial intelligence means intelligence that is made by people. It means building machines and computer programs that can do things that normally need human thinking.

Now, let us make this even simpler. Artificial intelligence is the science of making computers act in smart ways. A normal computer program follows fixed rules. You tell it exactly what to do, and it does exactly that, step by step. An artificial intelligence system can look at information, learn from it, and make decisions. It can handle new situations that the programmer did not write down line by line.

Here is a simple example. Imagine you write a program to sort exam scores from the lowest to the highest. That program just follows rules. It is not intelligent. Now imagine a program that looks at a student's past scores, homework, and attendance, and then predicts how well the student will do next term. That program has learned from data. That is closer to artificial intelligence.

Think about your daily life. When you type a message on your phone and the phone suggests the next word, that is AI. When you open YouTube and it shows you videos you like, that is AI. When your bank sends you a warning that a payment looks suspicious, that is AI. When a farmer uses a drone to see which part of a farm needs more water, that is AI. When a hospital uses software to read an X-ray and find a sickness early, that is AI.

One important thing to remember is this: AI does not think exactly like a human being. A human brain is amazing. It feels emotions, it dreams, it understands jokes, it has common sense. Today's AI is very good at specific tasks, like recognizing faces or translating languages, but it does not truly understand the world the way you do. Keep this in mind as we go forward.

Let us also define a few other words that people often mix up. Data is the raw information that AI systems learn from, like numbers, pictures, text, or sounds. A model is the brain of the AI system. It is a mathematical structure that has learned patterns from data. Training is the process of teaching the model using data. Prediction is what the model does after training, when it gives an answer about new data. Remember these four words: data, model, training, prediction. You will hear them again and again in this program.

PART 3 - A SHORT HISTORY OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Now let us travel back in time and see how AI began. The dream of intelligent machines is old. Ancient myths and stories talk about statues that come alive and mechanical servants. But real AI as a science started in the twentieth century.

In 1950, a British mathematician named Alan Turing asked a famous question: "Can machines think?" He wrote a paper that described a test called the Turing Test. In this test, a human talks to something behind a curtain. The human does not know if it is a machine or another person. If the machine can chat so well that the human cannot tell the difference, then, Turing said, we can call that machine intelligent. Whether you agree with this test or not, it shaped the whole field.

In 1956, a group of scientists met at a place called Dartmouth College in the United States. At that meeting, they used the words "artificial intelligence" for the first time as the name of a new field of study. That is why we say AI was officially born in 1956.

The 1960s and 1970s were exciting years. Scientists built programs that could solve math problems, play games, and understand simple sentences. People became very hopeful. Some even said that machines as smart as humans were just twenty years away. But the world turned out to be much harder than they thought. Computers were slow and expensive. Real life is messy and full of surprises, and the early programs could not handle the mess. This disappointment led to a period called the AI winter. Money for research dried up, and many projects stopped.

Then, in the 1980s, expert systems became popular. An expert system is a program that collects the knowledge of human experts in one area and uses rules to give advice. For example, a hospital could have an expert system that helps doctors guess which disease a patient has, based on symptoms. Expert systems worked well in narrow areas, but they were expensive and hard to keep updated.

In the 1990s and 2000s, three things changed the game. First, computers became much faster and much cheaper. Second, the internet gave us mountains of data, photos, videos, texts, and clicks. Third, the algorithms, which are the recipes that computers follow, became much better. In 1997, an IBM computer called Deep Blue beat the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov. That was a historic moment.

The modern AI revolution truly began around 2012, when a kind of AI called deep learning started winning big competitions in image recognition. Since then, AI has moved faster and faster. Today we have self-driving cars being tested on real roads, voice assistants in millions of homes, AI tools that can write essays and create images, and robots working in factories and warehouses. You are living in the golden age of AI, and you are starting your studies at exactly the right time.

PART 4 - TYPES OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Let us now talk about the different types of AI. There are two common ways to classify AI. The first way looks at capability, which means what the AI can do. The second way looks at how the AI works inside.

First, the capability view. There are three levels here. Level one is narrow AI, also called weak AI. Narrow AI is built for one specific task. It can do that one task very well, but it cannot do anything else. Your phone's face unlock is narrow AI. It recognizes faces, but it cannot write a poem or drive a car. Almost every AI in the world today is narrow AI.

Level two is general AI, also called strong AI. This is an AI that can do any intellectual task that a human can do. It could learn cooking in the morning, write code in the afternoon, and give legal advice in the evening. It would be as flexible and adaptable as a human mind. We do not have general AI yet. Scientists are still working on it, and there is big debate about when or if we will reach it.

Level three is super AI. This would be an AI that is smarter than every human in almost every way, including creativity, wisdom, and social skills. Super AI does not exist today. It is a topic for science fiction and serious research discussion, not for today's engineering.

Now the second way to classify AI looks at how it behaves. Researchers sometimes describe four stages. The first stage is reactive machines. These can react to what they see right now, but they have no memory of the past. Deep Blue, the chess computer, was like this. It saw the chess board and chose a move, but it learned nothing from previous games.

The second stage is limited memory AI. These systems remember the recent past and use it to make better decisions. Self-driving cars are like this. They watch the road, remember where the cars around them were a second ago, and decide whether to slow down. Most AI today is in this stage.

The third stage is theory of mind. This would be an AI that understands that people have thoughts, feelings, beliefs, and intentions, and it can guess what others are thinking. This kind of AI would be important for true teamwork between humans and robots. We are only taking small steps toward this.

The fourth stage is self-aware AI. This would be an AI with its own feelings and sense of self. It is completely science fiction for now.

Understanding these types helps you stay realistic. When someone says "AI will take over the world," you can ask, "Which type of AI do you mean?" The AI in your phone is narrow and limited memory. That is very different from a self-aware super AI.

PART 5 - AI, MACHINE LEARNING, DEEP LEARNING, DATA SCIENCE, AND ROBOTICS

Students often ask, "What is the difference between AI and machine learning?" Let me explain it with a simple picture in your mind. Imagine three circles, one inside the other, like a matryoshka doll.

The biggest circle is artificial intelligence. It is the whole idea of making machines act smart. Inside AI is machine learning, which is one powerful way to build AI. Machine learning means teaching a computer by giving it data instead of writing every rule by hand. Inside machine learning is deep learning. Deep learning uses special structures called neural networks with many layers, inspired loosely by the brain. Deep learning is behind face recognition, language translation, and modern chatbots.

So all deep learning is machine learning, and all machine learning is AI, but not all AI is machine learning. Some AI systems use rules written by humans, not learning. For example, a simple traffic light that changes on a fixed timer is automation, and a traffic light system that uses cameras to count cars and adjust its timing is closer to AI. If that system learns better timing patterns from months of traffic data, that is machine learning.

Now, where does data science fit? Data science is the broad practice of collecting data, cleaning it, analyzing it, and finding useful patterns to help people make decisions. A data scientist might use AI tools, but might also use simple statistics and charts. AI is more about building systems that act. Data science is more about understanding information to advise humans.

And where does robotics fit? Robotics is about building physical machines, robots, that can sense the world, move around, and do work. A robot without AI follows fixed movements, like a simple factory arm that welds the same spot every time. A robot with AI can see, learn, and adapt, like a warehouse robot that plans its own route around obstacles. Your degree combines both, AI and robotics, which makes you very special in the job market.

Let me give you one more practical picture. Think about a smart irrigation system on a farm. The sensors in the soil collect data, like moisture and temperature. That is data collection. Machine learning models predict how much water the crops will need tomorrow. That is machine learning inside AI. A robotic valve opens or closes the water pipe. That is robotics. And the farmer looks at a weekly report showing water saved and crop health. That is data science. One system, many fields working together. This is exactly why your program teaches all of them.

PART 6 - HOW AI THINKS: AGENTS, SEARCH, AND KNOWLEDGE

Let us look under the hood now. How does an AI system actually work? One useful way to think is through the idea of an agent. An agent is anything that can sense its environment and act upon it. You are an agent. You sense with your eyes and ears, and you act by moving and speaking. A robot vacuum cleaner is an agent. It senses the floor with its sensors, and it acts by moving its wheels and its brush. A software agent, like a shopping bot, senses prices on websites and acts by buying when the price is low.

Many AI systems follow a simple loop: sense, think, act. The system observes the world, it processes what it sees, and then it chooses an action. Then it starts again. This loop runs many times per second.

Now, how does the "think" part work? One classic method is search. When you play a game like chess, you think ahead: "If I move here, my opponent might move there, and then I could move here." AI does the same, exploring many possible futures and choosing the best one. Algorithms like breadth-first search, depth-first search, and A-star search are famous tools for this. You will study them properly in your Data Structures and Algorithms course, but the big idea is simple: explore possible options and pick the best path.

Another key idea is knowledge representation. This means storing facts and rules in a way that a computer can use. For example, a medical expert system might store facts like "Malaria causes fever" and "Fever and headache together may mean malaria or typhoid." Then the system uses logic to combine rules and reach a conclusion. Knowledge can be stored as rules, as decision trees, as graphs of connected ideas, or in modern times as huge numerical patterns inside neural networks.

There is also machine learning, where the system finds patterns by itself. Instead of giving the computer rules like "if fever, then malaria," you give it thousands of patient records with labels, and the system learns the pattern that connects symptoms to diseases. This is the approach that powers most modern AI.

The choice between rules and learning depends on the problem. If the rules are clear and few, like the rules of a board game, programming rules can work well. If the problem is messy and complex, like understanding human speech or recognizing objects in photos, learning from data usually works better. As engineers, you will often combine both approaches. This is called hybrid AI, and it is very powerful in real projects.

PART 7 - THE BRANCHES OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

AI is a big family with many children. Let me introduce the main ones.

First, machine learning, which we already mentioned. It is the heart of modern AI.

Second, natural language processing, often shortened to NLP. This is about helping computers understand and use human language. When you talk to Siri, Google Assistant, or any chatbot, that is NLP. When your email filters out spam, that is NLP. When a website translates English to Yoruba or Hausa, that is NLP.

Third, computer vision. This is about helping computers understand pictures and videos. Face unlock on your phone uses computer vision. A self-driving car reading road signs uses computer vision. A quality-check camera in a factory finding cracked bottles uses computer vision.

Fourth, robotics. This is about machines that move and do physical work. Robot arms in car factories, delivery drones, and surgical robots all live here.

Fifth, speech recognition and speech synthesis. This is about machines hearing and speaking. Voice assistants listen to your words and reply with a computer-generated voice.

Sixth, expert systems and knowledge-based systems. These give advice using stored expert knowledge, like medical diagnosis helpers or farming advisory systems.

Seventh, planning and decision-making systems. These help choose the best action, like software that plans delivery routes for trucks or schedules classes in a university.

Eighth, machine learning's cousins, like evolutionary computing and fuzzy logic, which copy ideas from nature and handle uncertainty in clever ways. You will meet fuzzy logic later in this program.

These branches do not work alone. A modern self-driving car uses all of them together: computer vision to see, NLP to understand voice commands from the driver, planning to choose a route, and robotics to control the steering and pedals. Real engineering projects are like soups with many ingredients. Your job is to learn each ingredient well and then mix them wisely.

PART 8 - REAL-LIFE APPLICATIONS OF AI

Now my favorite part. Let us walk through real-life applications of AI, one by one.

Healthcare. AI helps doctors find diseases earlier and more accurately. Machine learning models read X-rays, CT scans, and microscope images to find cancer, tuberculosis, and malaria parasites. AI also predicts which patients in a hospital might become very sick soon, so nurses can help them early. In Nigeria and across Africa, projects use phone-based tools to help community health workers screen for eye disease and skin conditions. Practical tip: if you are interested in health tech, start learning about medical images and privacy rules for patient data.

Agriculture. AI helps farmers grow more food with less waste. Drones fly over fields and take pictures. Computer vision finds spots where plants are sick or where insects are attacking. Machine learning predicts the best time to plant and the best time to harvest. Smart irrigation systems give water only when the soil is dry. Imagine a cassava farmer in Oyo State using an app that says, "Your farm in the north corner needs water today, and there are early signs of disease on the west side." That saves money and protects the harvest.

Finance and banking. AI detects fraud. When someone tries to use your ATM card in another country at midnight, the bank's AI flags it. AI also scores loan applications, helps with customer service chatbots, and powers mobile money services. Practical tip: fraud detection is a great beginner machine learning project. You can find public datasets of transactions and try to build a model that separates normal transactions from suspicious ones.

Transportation. AI powers ride-hailing apps that match drivers and riders and calculate prices. AI manages traffic light timing in smart cities. Self-driving vehicles use AI to see lanes, signs, and pedestrians. Logistics companies use AI to plan the shortest delivery routes, saving fuel and time.

Education. AI tutors give students personal practice. Software checks essays and gives feedback. Systems predict which students might drop out, so schools can support them early. During the COVID-19 pandemic, AI tools helped teachers grade and organize online classes.

Security and safety. Cameras with face recognition help find missing persons and control access to buildings. AI systems detect unusual behavior in video feeds, like someone entering a restricted area at night.

Entertainment and media. Recommendation systems on Netflix, Spotify, and TikTok learn your taste and suggest content. Video games use AI for smarter opponents and realistic characters.

Science and engineering. AI helps design new medicines, predicts the shapes of proteins, helps engineers design better machines, and helps scientists analyze huge amounts of data from telescopes and satellites.

Let me give you one full practical example that touches your daily life: spam filtering. Every day, millions of spam emails try to trick people with fake prizes and fake job offers. An AI spam filter looks at words in the email, the sender's address, and patterns of past spam. It gives the email a spam score. If the score is high, the email goes to the spam folder. The system learns from your actions too. When you mark an email as spam, the model updates its knowledge. Simple idea, huge impact. That is AI in action.

PART 9 - WALKTHROUGH: BUILDING A SIMPLE AI SPAM FILTER

Let us go a little deeper on that spam filter, because it shows the full AI workflow in miniature. Follow the steps with me.

Step one: define the problem. We want to separate emails into two groups: normal emails and spam emails. This is called a classification problem in machine learning.

Step two: collect data. We gather thousands of emails that are already labeled as spam or not spam. Maybe we have ten thousand emails: two thousand spam and eight thousand not spam.

Step three: prepare the data. We clean the text. We remove strange characters. We convert every word to lowercase so "FREE" and "free" count the same. Then we turn words into numbers, because models need numbers. One common trick is to count how often each word appears.

Step four: choose features. Features are the clues the model uses. For a spam filter, clues include words like "winner," "free," "prize," and "urgent," the number of links in the email, and whether the sender is known.

Step five: train the model. We use a machine learning algorithm. The algorithm studies the labeled emails and learns which words and patterns point to spam. It creates a model, which we can think of as a pattern detector.

Step six: test the model. We keep some emails aside for testing, emails the model has never seen. We check how many spam emails the model catches and how many normal emails it wrongly calls spam. The first measure is recall. The second mistake is called a false positive. In email, false positives are annoying because important emails get lost.

Step seven: improve and deploy. We tune the model, maybe add more data, and then put it into the email system where it works on new emails every day.

Step eight: keep learning. People change how they write spam, so we keep updating the model with new examples.

Notice the pattern: define, collect, prepare, train, test, improve, deploy, and keep learning. Almost every AI project in the world follows this same journey, from a small spam filter to a self-driving car. Remember this workflow. It will serve you for your whole career.

PART 10 - CHALLENGES AND LIMITATIONS OF AI

AI is powerful, but it is not magic. Let us be honest about its challenges.

First, data problems. AI learns from data, so bad data gives bad models. If your training data is incomplete or biased, the model will be biased too. For example, if a face recognition system is trained mostly on light-skinned faces, it may make more mistakes on dark-skinned faces. As engineers, we must collect diverse and fair data.

Second, lack of common sense. Today's AI can be very smart in narrow ways but very foolish in obvious ways. A system might identify a cow on a beach in a photo, but it may not understand that cows usually live on farms. Humans learn common sense from living in the world. Machines do not have that experience yet.

Third, explainability. Some models, especially deep neural networks, are like black boxes. They give an answer, but they cannot easily explain why. In healthcare and banking, people need explanations. "Why was my loan refused?" is a fair question that deserves a clear answer.

Fourth, cost and resources. Training big AI models needs powerful computers, lots of electricity, and money. This is why access to AI is unequal. Good engineers find efficient solutions that can run on cheap hardware, especially in places with limited infrastructure.

Fifth, security risks. AI systems can be attacked. Hackers can feed strange inputs to trick a model, which we call adversarial attacks. For example, small stickers on a stop sign can confuse a self-driving car's vision system. We must build systems that are robust and safe.

Sixth, misuse. The same AI that can find tumors can be used for mass surveillance. The same voice cloning that helps people who lost their voices can be used for scams. Technology is a tool, and the character of the user matters. You, as future engineers, carry responsibility.

PART 11 - ETHICS, SOCIETY, AND RESPONSIBLE AI

This leads us to ethics. Ethics means doing what is right, fair, and honest. In AI, ethics is not an extra subject. It is part of good engineering.

Think about fairness. An AI system that sorts job applicants should not discriminate by gender, ethnicity, or religion. But if the training data comes from a biased past, the model may copy those biases. We must check our models for fairness and fix problems before deployment.

Think about privacy. AI systems often use personal data: your location, your face, your health records. We must collect only what we need, protect it carefully, and ask for permission. Laws like the Nigeria Data Protection Regulation and Europe's GDPR set rules for this. Learn these rules. They matter in industry.

Think about transparency. People should know when they are talking to a machine, not a human. People should be able to ask how a decision was made. Hidden AI that manipulates people, like fake reviews or deepfake videos, damages trust in society.

Think about jobs. AI will change the job market. Some jobs will shrink, some will grow, and many new jobs will appear, like AI trainers, data annotators, robot technicians, and AI ethics officers. The best protection is education: keep learning, and learn to work with AI rather than compete against it.

Think about safety. If an AI system controls a robot, a car, or a medical device, mistakes can hurt people. Safety testing, fail-safes, and human oversight are mandatory, not optional.

A good habit is to ask five questions before building any AI system: Is it fair? Is it private? Is it transparent? Is it safe? And who is responsible if something goes wrong? If you can answer these five questions well, you are on the path to becoming a responsible AI engineer.

PART 13 - GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS AND FINAL REVIEW

Before we close completely, let us build a small glossary together. Writing down definitions in your own words is one of the strongest ways to learn.

Artificial intelligence: the field of building machines and programs that can learn, reason, solve problems, and make decisions in ways that seem smart.

Data: the raw information used to teach and run AI systems, including numbers, text, images, audio, and video.

Model: the learned structure inside an AI system that captures patterns from data and makes predictions.

Training: the process of feeding data to a model so it can learn useful patterns.

Prediction: the output of a trained model when it faces new, unseen information.

Algorithm: a clear step-by-step recipe that a computer follows to solve a problem or compute a result.

Machine learning: a branch of AI where systems learn patterns from data instead of following only hand-written rules.

Deep learning: a branch of machine learning using layered neural networks to learn complex patterns, especially in images, speech, and text.

Neural network: a computing structure loosely inspired by the brain, made of connected units that process numbers in layers.

Narrow AI: AI built for one specific task, such as face recognition or spam filtering. All AI today is narrow AI.

General AI: a hypothetical AI that could perform any intellectual task a human can perform. It does not exist yet.

Agent: anything that senses its environment and acts upon it, from a robot vacuum to a trading program.

Knowledge representation: methods for storing facts and rules so that computers can reason with them.

Expert system: a program that applies human expert knowledge and rules to give advice in a specific domain.

Turing test: a test proposed by Alan Turing in which a machine is considered intelligent if a human cannot tell it apart from another person in conversation.

Bias in AI: unfair patterns learned by a model when its training data over-represents or under-represents certain groups.

Explainability: the degree to which a human can understand why an AI system made a particular decision.

Adversarial attack: a carefully crafted input designed to trick an AI model into making a mistake.

Ethics in AI: the practice of building and using AI in ways that are fair, private, transparent, safe, and accountable.

Hybrid AI: a system that combines hand-written rules with machine learning to get the benefits of both.
