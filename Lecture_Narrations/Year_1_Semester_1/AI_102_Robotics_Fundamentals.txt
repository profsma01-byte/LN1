PART 1 - WELCOME TO THE WORLD OF ROBOTICS

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 102, Robotics Fundamentals. In the last lecture, we talked about artificial intelligence, the science of making computers act in smart ways. Today, we give that intelligence a body. We talk about robots.

A robot is a machine that can sense its surroundings, think about what to do, and then move or act in the real world. Robots are not just science fiction characters. They are already working beside us. They weld cars in factories. They vacuum floors in homes. They fly over farms to spray crops. They perform careful surgeries in hospitals. And yes, they even deliver food and parcels in some cities.

In this course, you will learn what robots are made of, how they move, how they sense the world, how we control them, and how we keep people safe around them. This is a fundamentals course, so we will build strong roots. Everything you learn here will support your later courses, like Robot Kinematics, Mobile Robotics, Robot Operating Systems, and Robot Design.

Let me say this clearly: you do not need to be an expert before you start. Robots look complex, but every robot is just a combination of simple parts working together. By the end of this lecture, you will see those parts clearly.

PART 2 - WHAT EXACTLY IS A ROBOT?

Let us define our main character. The word robot comes from a Czech word, robota, which means hard work or forced labor. A play in 1920 first used the word, and it has been popular ever since.

In engineering, a robot is a programmable machine capable of carrying out a series of actions automatically. Let us unpack that definition. Programmable means we can give it instructions, a program, and we can change those instructions. Machine means it is built from physical parts, motors, sensors, and computers. Automatically means it can do its work without a human controlling every single movement by hand.

Now, is a washing machine a robot? It does work automatically. But it only follows a fixed cycle, and it does not sense and adapt to a changing world in a flexible way. We usually call it an automated machine, not a robot. What about a modern robot vacuum cleaner? It senses furniture with its sensors, builds a map of the room, plans a path, and avoids obstacles. That is much closer to a true robot, because it senses, thinks, and acts.

A helpful way to remember the difference is this: automation repeats a fixed job; robotics adds sensing, decision, and movement in the real world. And when we add artificial intelligence, the robot can learn and adapt even more.

Let me introduce three words that describe every robot. First, perception: the robot senses the world. Second, cognition or decision: the robot decides what to do. Third, action: the robot moves and does the work. Sense, think, act. You heard this in AI 101, and it is the heartbeat of robotics.

PART 3 - THE ANATOMY OF A ROBOT

Now let us open up a robot and look inside. Every robot, from a tiny toy car to a giant industrial arm, has five main body systems.

First, the mechanical system. These are the parts you can touch: the frame or chassis, the arms, the wheels or legs, the gears, the joints, and the grippers. The mechanical system gives the robot its shape and its ability to move. Materials matter here. Engineers choose steel for strength, aluminum for light weight, and plastics for cheap and simple parts. In advanced robots, you also find carbon fiber and soft silicone materials.

Second, the actuator system. Actuators are the muscles of the robot. They make things move. Electric motors are the most common actuators. Servo motors give precise control of angles, which is perfect for robot arms. Stepper motors move in exact steps, useful for 3D printers and CNC machines. DC motors spin fast and drive wheels. Pneumatic actuators use compressed air, and hydraulic actuators use pressurized oil; you see hydraulics in excavators and heavy rescue robots. Artificial muscles are an exciting research area, copying real muscles.

Third, the sensor system. Sensors are the eyes, ears, and skin of the robot. Cameras act as eyes. Microphones act as ears. Ultrasonic sensors measure distance by sending sound waves, like a bat. Infrared sensors detect heat and nearby objects. LiDAR shoots laser beams to build a map, and it is a key sensor in self-driving cars. Touch sensors and force sensors act like skin, telling the robot when it touches something. IMU, which stands for inertial measurement unit, tells the robot if it is tilting or turning. GPS gives outdoor position.

Fourth, the controller system. This is the brain. It is a computer, often a small one, like a microcontroller board or an embedded processor. The controller reads the sensors, runs the program, and sends commands to the actuators. In many robots, the main brain is a small, powerful computer, sometimes with a graphics processor for AI tasks. Robot Operating Systems, which you will study later, help organize the software.

Fifth, the power system. Robots need energy. Small robots use batteries. Big industrial robots plug into the electrical grid. Power matters more than students think. A robot with heavy batteries moves slower and carries less useful weight. Power efficiency is a real design challenge.

Here is a practical exercise. Pick any robot you know, for example a delivery drone. Can you name its mechanical parts? Its actuators, the propeller motors? Its sensors, like camera and GPS? Its controller, the flight computer? And its power system, the lithium battery? Practice this with three different robots this week. It trains your engineering eye.

PART 4 - DEGREES OF FREEDOM AND ROBOT MOVEMENT

Let us talk about movement. When an engineer says degrees of freedom, they mean the number of independent ways a robot can move.

Think about your own arm. You can move your shoulder up and down, forward and backward, and rotate it. Your elbow bends. Your wrist turns and tilts. Your fingers grip. Your arm has many degrees of freedom, which is why your hand is so flexible.

Now think about a simple robot arm with three joints. It might bend at the base, bend at the shoulder, and bend at the elbow. That gives it three degrees of freedom. With three movements, it can reach many points in its workspace, which is the volume of space the robot can reach. Add a wrist that can rotate and tilt, and you get five or six degrees of freedom. A classic industrial arm has six degrees of freedom, because with six it can reach any position and any orientation within its workspace, like a human arm holding a tool at any angle.

Wheeled robots also have degrees of freedom. A car-like robot with Ackermann steering, like a real car, has fewer ways to move than a robot with mecanum wheels that can slide sideways. Understanding degrees of freedom helps you choose the right robot for the job.

Let me give you a real-life picture. A welding robot in a car factory needs six degrees of freedom, because the welding tool must meet the metal surface at exactly the right angle. A simple warehouse transport robot only needs to drive on the floor, so it has fewer degrees of freedom and is much cheaper. Matching the robot's movement ability to the task is one of the first and most important design decisions.

PART 5 - TYPES OF ROBOTS

Robots come in many families. Let me introduce the main ones.

Industrial robots. These are the strong arms you see in factories. They weld, paint, lift, drill, and assemble. They are fast, precise, and strong, but they usually stay inside safety fences because they are powerful and dangerous to people.

Mobile robots. These move around. They can be wheeled, like robot vacuums and warehouse robots. They can be legged, like robot dogs that climb stairs and cross rough ground. They can fly, like drones. They can swim, like underwater inspection robots.

Humanoid robots. These look like people, with a head, body, arms, and legs. They are useful research platforms because our world is designed for humans: door handles, stairs, and tools all fit the human shape. Building a humanoid is one of the hardest challenges in robotics.

Collaborative robots, called cobots. These are designed to work safely beside people without heavy fences. They have soft covers, force sensing, and slow, careful movements. You might see a cobot handing tools to a mechanic or packing boxes next to a human worker.

Medical and rehabilitation robots. Surgical robots help doctors make very small, precise cuts. Rehabilitation robots help patients move and recover after strokes. Exoskeletons are wearable robots that help paralyzed people stand and walk.

Service robots. These serve people in public places. You might meet a service robot in a hotel delivering towels, in a restaurant carrying food, in an airport guiding passengers, or in a hospital delivering medicines.

Aerial robots and drones. Drones are used for photography, delivery, search and rescue, mapping, and agriculture. A farmer can send a drone to spray pesticide only on the infected part of a field. This saves money and reduces chemical use.

Underground and space robots. Robots explore caves, mines, the deep sea, and other planets. The Mars rovers are famous examples. They are far from their engineers, so they must be very reliable and partly autonomous.

Soft robots. These are made of flexible materials, like silicone. They can squeeze through tight spaces and gently hold fragile objects, like fruit or even a human hand. This is a young and exciting field.

Each type solves a different problem. The engineering skill is choosing the right type, or inventing a new type, for the job at hand.

PART 6 - SENSORS: HOW ROBOTS FEEL THE WORLD

A robot that cannot sense is like a person walking blindfolded. Let us look deeper at sensors.

Distance sensors tell the robot how far away things are. Ultrasonic sensors work like bats: they send a sound pulse and measure the echo time. They are cheap and good for short ranges, like a robot vacuum avoiding chair legs. Infrared sensors are even smaller and cheaper. LiDAR, which stands for light detection and ranging, spins laser beams and builds a detailed map of the surroundings. It is more expensive but very powerful.

Vision sensors, cameras, give the richest information. With a camera and computer vision, a robot can read signs, recognize people, find objects, and inspect products. Stereo cameras use two lenses to see depth, similar to your two eyes.

Position and motion sensors tell the robot where it is. Encoders on motors measure how much the wheels or joints have turned. An IMU measures acceleration and rotation, helping the robot balance. GPS gives global position outdoors, but it does not work well indoors or in dense cities with tall buildings.

Force and touch sensors let the robot feel contact. A robot hand with force sensors can hold an egg without breaking it. This is essential for robots that work with people or handle fragile goods.

Environmental sensors measure temperature, humidity, gas, and sound. A robot inspecting a gas pipeline needs gas sensors. A robot working in a cold storage warehouse needs temperature sensors.

Now, a practical point. Sensors are never perfect. They have noise, small random errors. A distance sensor might read 1.02 meters when the truth is 1.00 meter. Good engineers use filtering, for example a simple moving average, to smooth noisy readings. And they often fuse several sensors together, which is called sensor fusion. A self-driving car combines camera, radar, and LiDAR, because each sensor has strengths and weaknesses. Cameras see color but struggle in darkness. Radar works in rain and fog but has poor resolution. LiDAR is precise but expensive. Together, they are stronger than any one alone. This idea, using many imperfect views to build one reliable picture, is a deep principle in robotics.

PART 7 - ACTUATORS AND MOTORS: THE MUSCLES

Let us look at muscles again. Choosing the right actuator is a real engineering decision.

DC motors spin continuously and are cheap. They are great for wheels. But they are hard to hold at an exact angle without help.

Servo motors contain a DC motor, gears, and a position sensor in one box. They can be commanded to turn to a specific angle and hold it. Robot arms and steering systems love servos.

Stepper motors move in exact steps. Send one pulse, and the motor turns exactly one step. This gives precise open-loop control. 3D printers and CNC machines use stepper motors.

Pneumatic actuators use compressed air. They are light, fast, and simple. Packaging machines often use them. But precise position control is harder.

Hydraulic actuators use oil under high pressure. They are incredibly strong for their size. Excavators, cranes, and heavy rescue robots use hydraulics. They are heavy and can leak, so they are less common in small robots.

There are also special actuators: shape memory alloys that change shape when heated, and artificial muscles made of special plastics that contract like real muscles. These are not yet common, but they may define future robots.

For every actuator, engineers ask four questions: How strong is it? How fast is it? How precise is it? And how much power does it need? Add cost and weight, and you have the full picture. When you design a robot later in this program, you will make these exact choices. Start noticing motors around you now: in fans, in printers, in car windows. The world is full of actuators.

PART 8 - CONTROL: FROM OPEN LOOP TO FEEDBACK

How do we make a robot move exactly as we want? This is the job of control systems. You will have a full course on control later, but let us build the basic idea now.

The simplest control is open-loop control. You send a command and trust the result. Imagine telling a motor to spin for ten seconds. If something blocks the motor, it will still try, and you will not know there is a problem. Open-loop is simple but fragile.

Closed-loop control, also called feedback control, is smarter. The robot measures what is actually happening, compares it to what we want, and corrects the difference. Imagine you want a robot arm at 45 degrees. The sensor says it is at 40 degrees. The controller sees the error of 5 degrees and pushes the motor. As the arm approaches 45 degrees, the push becomes gentler. This is feedback, and it is the soul of robotics.

The most famous feedback controller in industry is the PID controller. PID stands for Proportional, Integral, and Derivative. Let me explain in simple words. The proportional part reacts to the present error: the bigger the error, the bigger the push. The integral part remembers past errors: if small errors keep adding up over time, this part pushes harder to remove them completely. The derivative part watches how fast the error is changing and acts like a brake to prevent overshooting. Together, these three actions give smooth and accurate control.

Real-life example: cruise control in a car. You set 100 kilometers per hour. The car goes up a hill and slows to 95. The controller adds power. On the way down, the car speeds to 105, and the controller eases off. The car settles at your set speed. That is PID in your daily life. Another example: a drone hovering in the wind. The flight controller constantly measures tilt with the IMU and adjusts the four propeller speeds hundreds of times per second. If the control is bad, the drone wobbles or crashes. If the control is good, it hangs in the air like it is nailed to the sky.

Feedback control makes robots reliable in the real world, where floors are bumpy, loads are heavy, and wind blows. Always think in feedback loops.

PART 9 - PROGRAMMING ROBOTS

Let us talk about how we give robots instructions. Robot programming happens at different levels.

At the lowest level, we have embedded code, often written in C or C++, running directly on microcontrollers. This code reads sensors and drives motors with precise timing. It runs in milliseconds and controls the robot's reflexes.

At the middle level, we have robot middleware, like ROS, the Robot Operating System. ROS organizes the robot's software into modules that communicate. One module handles vision, one handles navigation, one handles the arm. This is the industry and research standard, and you will study it in detail later.

At the high level, we have task programming and AI planning. Here we say things like "pick the red block and place it on the blue tray," and the system figures out the steps. With modern AI, we can even teach robots by showing them.

One powerful idea is teaching by demonstration. Instead of writing code, you can move the robot arm by hand through the desired motion, and the robot records and repeats it. This is called kinesthetic teaching. Factory workers with no programming background can teach cobots this way.

Simulation is another essential practice. Before running code on an expensive real robot, engineers test in a simulator, a virtual robot in a virtual world. Simulators let you crash a thousand times for free. When the program works in simulation, you move carefully to the real robot. You will use tools like Gazebo, Webots, or Isaac Sim later in this program.

A practical tip: start small and simple. Make an LED blink. Then make a motor spin. Then make a robot follow a black line on the floor. Every big robot project is a stack of small, working steps. Celebrate each small step.

PART 10 - LEVELS OF AUTONOMY

Not all robots think for themselves. Let us look at levels of autonomy.

At level zero, there is full human control. A person drives every movement with a joystick. Think of a robot arm moved by a technician during repair.

At level one, the robot has simple automation. It repeats recorded motions, like a factory arm welding the same points in a loop.

At level two, the robot shares control with a human. The robot handles some parts, and the human handles others. Many drones work this way: the pilot commands direction, and the flight computer stabilizes the hover.

At level three, the robot works on its own in limited situations but may ask for human help. A warehouse robot plans its own route but calls a supervisor if it meets an unexpected blockage.

At level four, the robot is highly autonomous in a defined area. Delivery robots on a university campus can find their way from a cafeteria to a dormitory almost entirely alone.

At level five, full autonomy, the robot handles everything in all situations with no human help. This level is still rare and is the goal of self-driving car research.

Understanding levels helps in real projects. Never promise level five autonomy for a student project. Deliver reliable level two or three autonomy, and your system will be honest, safe, and useful. Overpromising is one of the most common failures in robotics projects.

PART 11 - ROBOT SAFETY AND HUMAN FACTORS

Safety is not boring paperwork. Safety is respect for human life.

Industrial robots are strong and fast. A mistake can seriously injure a person. That is why factories use safety fences, light curtains, and emergency stop buttons, the big red buttons that cut power instantly. A golden rule: never enter a robot's workspace while it is powered and active.

Cobots work differently. They use force sensing to detect a collision and stop immediately. They move slower near people. Their shapes are rounded, with no sharp edges or pinch points.

Risk assessment is the professional method. Before deploying any robot, engineers list possible hazards, judge how likely and how serious each one is, and then reduce the risks with design changes, guards, and procedures. This is part of international standards, and you will meet them in industry.

Human factors matter too. Robots that work with people must be predictable. If a robot's next move surprises people, accidents happen. Good design makes the robot's intention clear: lights, sounds, or body direction show what it will do next.

Real-life example: hospital delivery robots. They announce when they turn corners, they stop for people, and they play friendly sounds. This is not decoration. It is safety design for shared spaces. Remember this lesson: a robot is not safe just because it is smart. It is safe when it is designed to protect people, even when things go wrong.

PART 12 - ROBOTICS IN REAL LIFE: CASES AND CAREERS

Let us end with real-life stories and career paths.

Story one: agriculture. In many African countries, including Nigeria, drones and small robots help farmers. A drone maps a maize farm and shows where pests attack. A robot sprayer treats only those spots. The farmer saves money on chemicals, and the environment suffers less. Students in this program can build low-cost versions of such systems as final-year projects.

Story two: healthcare. Surgical robots do not replace doctors. They extend a surgeon's hands, filtering out tiny tremors and enabling operations through tiny cuts. Patients recover faster and lose less blood. But the engineers behind these robots must be extremely careful with reliability and testing.

Story three: logistics. Warehouse robots move shelves to human workers, cutting walking time dramatically. During online shopping peaks, fleets of thousands of robots work together, coordinated by central software. This is multi-robot coordination, a topic in your future courses.

Story four: disaster response. After earthquakes or building collapses, snake-like robots crawl through rubble to find survivors. They carry cameras and microphones where humans cannot go.

Now, careers. With this degree, you can become a robotics engineer, an automation engineer, a drone pilot and developer, a control systems engineer, a machine vision engineer, an AI robotics software developer, or a robotics technician and trainer. Industries include manufacturing, agriculture, healthcare, logistics, mining, oil and gas, entertainment, and security. The field is young, and there is room for you to create jobs, not just find them.

PART 14 - MINI PROJECT GUIDE: BUILD A SIMPLE LINE-FOLLOWING ROBOT

Let me walk you through a classic beginner project that ties everything together: a line-following robot. This project is practical, affordable, and deeply educational.

The mission is simple. The robot must follow a black line drawn on a white floor and stay on track. Shops, warehouses, and even some hospitals use guided robots that follow lines or magnetic strips. You are building a real industrial idea at hobby scale.

Parts you need: a small two-wheel robot chassis with a caster wheel for balance, two DC motors with wheels, a motor driver module, a microcontroller board such as an Arduino, two infrared reflectance sensors, a battery pack, jumper wires, and black tape to draw the line.

How it works. The infrared sensors sit under the robot, pointing at the floor. Each sensor sends out light and measures how much light bounces back. White floor reflects strongly; black tape absorbs light and reflects little. The microcontroller reads both sensors and knows where the line is relative to the robot. If the left sensor sees black, the robot is drifting right, so it turns left. If the right sensor sees black, it turns right. If both see white, the robot goes straight. If both see black, the robot has reached a cross line and can decide to stop or continue.

Step one: assemble the chassis and mount the motors and wheels. Keep the weight balanced between the wheels. Step two: wire the motor driver. Never connect motors directly to the microcontroller pins; motors need more current than the chip can give. The motor driver acts as a powerful middleman. Step three: mount the infrared sensors near the floor, about five to ten millimeters above the ground, and connect them to the analog input pins. Step four: write the program. Read both sensors, compare against a threshold, and decide the motor speeds. Start with simple rules, then try adding speed control: slow down in sharp turns and speed up on straight sections. Step five: test on a large loop of tape, debug patiently, and improve.

What you learn from this project is huge. You practice mechanics, sensors, actuators, embedded programming, and feedback thinking, all at once. When your robot wobbles and then finally completes a full loop cleanly, you will feel a joy that no exam can give. That feeling is what keeps engineers going.

A slightly harder extension: add an ultrasonic sensor so the robot stops when an obstacle blocks the line. Another extension: add a second line path and let the robot choose at a junction using a small switch or a remote command. Each extension teaches another robotics concept.

PART 15 - GLOSSARY AND CLOSING REVIEW

Let us lock in the vocabulary of this course.

Robot: a programmable machine that can sense, think, and act in the real world.

Actuator: a device that creates movement, such as a motor, a pneumatic cylinder, or a hydraulic ram.

Sensor: a device that measures the physical world and reports it to the controller, such as a camera, a distance sensor, or a force sensor.

Controller: the computing unit that reads sensors, runs programs, and commands the actuators.

Degrees of freedom: the number of independent ways a robot can move.

Workspace: the region of space a robot arm or end-effector can reach.

End-effector: the tool or hand at the end of a robot arm, such as a gripper, a welder, or a paint nozzle.

Servo motor: a motor with built-in gears and position feedback that can turn to an exact angle.

Feedback control: controlling a system by measuring its actual behavior and correcting the difference from the desired behavior.

PID controller: a widely used controller combining proportional, integral, and derivative actions for smooth, accurate control.

Sensor fusion: combining readings from several sensors to build a more reliable picture of the world.

Odometry: estimating a robot's position by counting wheel rotations.

Autonomy: the degree to which a robot works without human help.

Cobot: a collaborative robot designed to work safely beside people.

Kinesthetic teaching: teaching a robot by physically moving it through the desired motion.

Simulation: testing robots in a virtual world before trying them in reality.

Risk assessment: the professional process of finding hazards and reducing them before deployment.

Emergency stop: a control that immediately removes power or motion from a robot when safety is at risk.

Soft robot: a robot built from flexible materials that can squeeze and handle delicate objects.

Embedded system: a small dedicated computer inside a machine that controls its hardware in real time.

To close, let me remind you of the big picture. Robots are systems of systems. Mechanical design gives them bodies. Actuators give them muscles. Sensors give them senses. Controllers and software give them minds. Power gives them energy. Feedback control gives them reliability. Safety design gives them the right to work among us. And artificial intelligence, which you began studying in AI 101, gives them the ability to learn and adapt. When all of these parts come together with care and creativity, machines stop being tools and start becoming teammates.

Your journey in robotics begins with curiosity and continues with practice. Build something small every month. Read datasheets. Watch robots work in factories and hospitals and farms, and ask how each part works. The questions you ask now will become the projects you build tomorrow, and the projects you build tomorrow may one day change how people in your community farm, travel, heal, and live.

One last set of study tips. Keep a robotics notebook. In it, paste pictures of robots you find in news articles and label their body systems with your own notes. Watch slow-motion videos of robot grippers and joints and try to guess how many degrees of freedom each one has. When a delivery drone hums over your street, look up and ask yourself where its sensors are, what its actuators are doing, and how its controller is keeping it stable in the wind. Habits like these turn the whole world into your robotics laboratory, and they will make every later course in this program easier and more exciting.
