PART 1 - WELCOME: THE INVISIBLE SCIENCE THAT RUNS THE WORLD

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 106, Control Systems. Control is everywhere, yet most people never notice it. When your water heater keeps water at exactly the temperature you like, when a drone holds steady in the wind, when your car's cruise control maintains speed on a hill, when a hospital keeps an incubator at the perfect warmth for newborn babies, control systems are doing the work.

Control engineering is the science of making systems behave the way we want. In simple words, it is the art of making machines stay on target despite disturbances. This course gives you the foundation. Later, you will use these ideas in robotics, autonomous systems, intelligent control, and every project that involves movement, temperature, speed, or any physical quantity.

Here is a promise: control theory has a reputation for heavy mathematics, and yes, mathematics lives here. But the core ideas are simple and human. You already control things every day. When you adjust a shower tap to keep water warm while someone else opens a kitchen tap, you are doing feedback control. Let us now do it like engineers.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand open-loop and closed-loop control, feedback, transfer functions and block diagrams at a basic level, stability, and the famous PID controller. You will see real-life cases from homes, cars, drones, and industry, and you will know how to start tuning a controller yourself.

PART 2 - WHAT IS A CONTROL SYSTEM? KEY VOCABULARY

Let us build the vocabulary carefully.

A system is anything that changes with time: a motor's speed, a room's temperature, a drone's height, a robot arm's angle.

The output is the quantity we care about and want to control: the water temperature, the speed, the height.

The reference or setpoint is the value we want the output to have: 40 degrees Celsius, 100 kilometers per hour, 10 meters of altitude.

The input or control signal is what we adjust to influence the output: the heater power, the throttle, the propeller speed.

A disturbance is anything that pushes the output away from the setpoint without our permission: cold water entering the tank, a hill slowing the car, a gust of wind hitting the drone.

The plant is the system being controlled, like the water heater or the drone. The controller is the device or algorithm that decides what to do: a thermostat, a cruise control computer, a flight controller.

A sensor measures the output: a thermometer, a speed sensor, an altimeter. An actuator executes the control action: a heating element, a throttle valve, a motor.

Now the definitions. Open-loop control means we act without measuring the result. Closed-loop control, also called feedback control, means we measure the output, compare it to the setpoint, and correct the error. The error is simply the difference between what we want and what we have.

One more key word: dynamics. Dynamics is how the system behaves over time: how fast it responds, whether it overshoots, whether it settles. Every plant has its own personality. A small electric heater heats fast; a big water tank heats slowly. Understanding the plant's dynamics is the first job of a control engineer.

PART 3 - OPEN LOOP VERSUS CLOSED LOOP: A REAL-LIFE FEEL

Let us compare the two control styles with everyday examples.

Open-loop examples. A toaster: you set the timer to three minutes, and it heats for three minutes regardless of how brown the bread actually is. Wet bread burns; dry bread stays pale. A manual shower tap: you set a position and hope the temperature is right. A basic electric iron without thermostat: it gets hotter and hotter until you unplug it.

Closed-loop examples. A modern iron with a thermostat: a sensor measures temperature and switches the heater on and off to hold the setpoint. A car's cruise control: a speed sensor closes the loop. A drone's altitude hold: an IMU and barometer measure height and adjust motor speed constantly. Your own body: when you feel cold, you shiver and seek a sweater, closing the loop of thermal comfort.

Why prefer closed loop? Because the real world is full of disturbances and uncertainties. Components age. Loads change. Weather changes. An open-loop system cannot react; a closed-loop system senses and corrects. This is why almost every reliable machine uses feedback somewhere.

But closed loop has a cost and a danger. The cost: more parts, sensors, and complexity. The danger: poor feedback design can make things worse. If your controller overreacts, the system can oscillate wildly: a heater that overshoots and swings between hot and cold, or a drone that wobbles and crashes. Designing feedback well is the craft you are learning.

PART 4 - BLOCK DIAGRAMS AND THE FEEDBACK LOOP

Engineers draw control ideas as block diagrams, simple pictures of how signals flow.

Start with the setpoint, the target. A circle called the comparator subtracts the measured output from the setpoint to produce the error signal. The error goes into the controller block. The controller produces a control signal that drives the actuator. The actuator drives the plant. The plant produces the output. A sensor measures the output and feeds it back to the comparator. That closed circle is the feedback loop.

Read that again slowly, because this diagram appears in almost every control document you will ever see: setpoint minus measured output gives error; error drives controller; controller drives plant; plant output is measured and fed back.

Real-life mapping. In cruise control: setpoint 100 kilometers per hour; error is how far the actual speed is from 100; the controller decides throttle increase or decrease; the plant is engine plus car; the sensor is the speedometer. In a water heater: setpoint 40 degrees; error drives heater power; the plant is the tank and water; the sensor is the thermometer.

We also connect blocks mathematically with transfer functions, which describe each block's behavior with algebra relating input to output. A common representation is using the Laplace variable s. You will do the full mathematics in class and later courses. Today, I want the intuition: each block is a rule for how output follows input, and connecting blocks is following the chain of cause and effect around the loop.

PART 5 - HOW SYSTEMS BEHAVE: SPEED, OVERSHOOT, STEADY STATE

Let us describe how well a control system behaves using three qualities every engineer watches.

Speed of response. How fast does the output reach the target after a change? A responsive system reaches the neighborhood of the setpoint quickly. A slow system lags and frustrates users.

Overshoot. Does the output go past the target and then come back? Some overshoot is often acceptable: a car accelerating to 100 may briefly touch 103. But too much overshoot is dangerous: an incubator overshooting temperature can harm a newborn; a robot arm overshooting position can break parts.

Steady-state error. After everything settles, does the output sit exactly at the setpoint, or is there a small permanent gap? Good controllers drive steady-state error to zero or very near zero.

Oscillation is another behavior to watch: if the output swings up and down repeatedly around the setpoint, the system is under-damped or marginally stable. With too much correction in the wrong phase, oscillations grow instead of shrinking, and the system becomes unstable, the most dangerous failure in control.

A practical walk-through. Imagine heating a room to 24 degrees. A poor controller turns a powerful heater fully on until 24, then fully off, causing waves between 21 and 27. A better controller eases power as the room approaches 24 and holds steady. Analyzing these behaviors before deployment is how control engineers prevent complaints and accidents.

Tools you will use later: step response plots that show how output rises and settles after a sudden input change, and frequency response that shows how the system handles fast and slow repeated changes. Today, remember the qualities: speed, overshoot, settling, steady-state error, and stability.

PART 6 - STABILITY: THE HEART OF CONTROL

Stability deserves its own part because it is the heart of control.

A stable system, when disturbed, returns to balance. Push a ball sitting at the bottom of a bowl, and it rolls back and settles. That is stability. An unstable system runs away from balance. A ball sitting on top of an inverted bowl: the smallest push sends it away forever. A marginally stable system neither returns nor runs away but keeps swinging, like a frictionless pendulum.

In engineering, instability can be spectacular and expensive. In 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge oscillated in wind and collapsed. Fighter jets can enter dangerous oscillations if control gains are wrong. Chemical plants can run away with temperature. Stability analysis is how we ensure our designs do not do these things.

Two famous stability tools. The Routh-Hurwitz criterion checks a system's characteristic equation with algebra to decide stability without solving everything fully. The Nyquist plot and Bode plot examine frequency response to judge stability margins: how much gain or delay the system can tolerate before going unstable. You will compute these in your coursework. The intuition: every feedback system has a phase relationship between correction and response; if corrections arrive at exactly the wrong moments, they amplify error instead of killing it.

Gain margin and phase margin are the safety buffers. Never deploy a controller with tiny margins in the real world, where modeling errors and delays always exist. Robustness, meaning good behavior despite imperfect models, is the mark of professional design.

PART 7 - THE PID CONTROLLER: YOUR NEW BEST FRIEND

Now the star of industrial control: the PID controller. More than ninety percent of industrial control loops use PID. Master it, and you can hold your own in any factory on earth.

PID means Proportional, Integral, Derivative. Three actions working together. Let me explain each with intuition and then with a practical example.

Proportional action, P. It reacts to the present error. The bigger the error, the bigger the push. If the water is 10 degrees too cold, P pushes hard; if it is 1 degree too cold, P pushes gently. P alone is fast but imperfect: it usually leaves a steady-state error, because as the error shrinks, the push shrinks too, and something must fight the disturbance continuously.

Integral action, I. It looks at the past: it adds up all the errors over time. Even a small error, if it persists, grows the integral term and increases the push until the error disappears. I kills steady-state error. But too much I causes overshoot and oscillation, because the accumulated past error keeps pushing after the target is already reached.

Derivative action, D. It looks at the future, in a sense: it reacts to how fast the error is changing. If the error is shrinking quickly, D eases the push to brake the approach and prevent overshoot. D acts like anticipation. But D is sensitive to noisy sensors: if the measurement jitters, D reacts to jitter. Use it with filtered signals and care.

Together: P fixes the present, I fixes the past, D anticipates the future.

Practical example, water heater. Setpoint 40 degrees. Water is at 20, so error is 20. P pushes heater power high. As water warms, error shrinks and P eases. But suppose cold water keeps entering and error stalls at 2 degrees. I notices the lingering error and adds power until it vanishes. As the temperature approaches 40 quickly, D damps the rise so we do not overshoot to 45. The result: fast, exact, smooth control.

Another example, cruise control on a hill. The car slows on the climb; P adds throttle; I ensures we return to exactly 100, not 98; D softens throttle changes to avoid jerky speed swings. Passengers feel a smooth ride. That is good tuning.

PART 8 - TUNING PID: PRACTICAL WISDOM

Tuning means choosing the strength of P, I, and D. Theory gives methods like Ziegler-Nichols, which uses experiments with the plant: first run P only until the system oscillates steadily, note that gain and period, then apply formulas for P, I, and D. It gives a starting point, often with some overshoot.

Manual tuning follows a simple ladder. Start with P only and raise it until the system responds reasonably fast. Add I to remove steady-state error, raising it until error vanishes but before oscillations grow ugly. Add a little D if overshoot needs damping, and filter the sensor signal. Then refine gently.

Practical wisdom from the field. First, know your plant. A slow thermal system and a fast robotic joint need very different gains. Second, prioritize safety: tune with small signals first and with protective limits in place. Third, use anti-windup protection for the integral term: if an actuator saturates at maximum power and the error persists, the integral can accumulate enormously and cause a huge overshoot later. Anti-windup caps this. Fourth, set output limits: never command negative heater power or more than 100 percent throttle. Fifth, test with realistic disturbances, not just perfect laboratory conditions.

Real-life tuning story. A team controls a slow conveyor belt carrying fragile bottles. The first tuning was aggressive; bottles toppled on speed changes. Slowing the P gain and reducing I while adding soft acceleration ramps solved it. The lesson: the best tuning is not the fastest; it is the one that fits the real task and protects the real product.

PART 9 - BEYOND PID: OTHER CONTROL IDEAS IN BRIEF

PID is the workhorse, but let me preview the wider world you will meet later in this program.

State-space control models the system with sets of first-order equations describing multiple variables at once, like position and velocity together. It enables modern multivariable control for complex machines like aircraft.

Optimal control finds the best control according to a cost, for example minimum fuel or minimum time. Linear quadratic regulators are a classic example.

Model predictive control, MPC, looks ahead into the future using a model of the plant, chooses a sequence of actions that minimizes cost over a horizon, executes only the first action, and repeats. It handles constraints beautifully and runs self-driving cars and chemical plants. You will study it in Intelligent Control Systems.

Adaptive control adjusts its own parameters when the plant changes, like a drone that must carry light or heavy packages. Robust control keeps performance despite uncertainty using worst-case design principles.

Nonlinear control deals with systems whose behavior changes with operating point, like aerodynamics at high speed. And fuzzy control uses linguistic rules, like if error is large and change is slow then push hard, which we will study fully in Fuzzy Logic.

And here is where your AI degree comes alive: neural network control and reinforcement learning control learn controllers from data and experience. Classic control gives guarantees of stability; AI gives flexibility for complexity. The future belongs to hybrid designs that combine both. You are uniquely positioned to build them.

PART 10 - SENSORS, ACTUATORS, AND REAL-WORLD NON-IDEALITIES

Control quality is limited by hardware. Sensors have noise, bias, and delay. A cheap thermocouple jitters; a worn speed sensor drifts. Filtering reduces noise but adds lag, and lag can destabilize a loop. Balance these trade-offs consciously.

Actuators have limits and nonlinearities. Motors saturate at maximum torque. Valves stick. Gears have backlash, small play that causes jumps. Hydraulic systems have dead zones. Good control respects these realities: use anti-windup, dead-zone compensation, and gentle ramps.

Delays and dead time are deadly for control. If your sensor measures a temperature that only reflects what happened ten seconds ago in the tank, aggressive control will overcorrect and oscillate. The rule: the larger the dead time, the gentler the controller must be.

Sampling and digital control. Modern controllers run on computers that sample measurements at intervals: maybe every millisecond, maybe every second. Sample too slowly and you miss dynamics; sample fast and you need stronger hardware. Aliasing, where fast oscillations masquerade as slow ones, appears here as in your future signal processing course.

Practical lab advice. When a loop misbehaves, follow this checklist: verify sensor readings against an independent instrument; check actuator health; measure dead time; lower gains; add filtering; re-tune methodically. Change one thing at a time, and keep a log of every change and its effect. Control debugging is disciplined experimentation.

PART 11 - CONTROL IN ROBOTICS AND EVERYDAY TECHNOLOGY

Let me connect this course to your degree and your life.

Robot joint control. Each joint of a robot arm runs position loops, often with velocity and current inner loops. PID is at the core. Well-tuned joints make smooth, precise motion; poorly tuned joints make buzzing robots.

Mobile robot speed and steering control. A delivery robot uses feedback to keep wheel speeds equal on straight paths and to follow curves. Cruise control logic appears again here.

Drone flight control. Quadcopters run multiple fast loops: attitude stabilization with IMU feedback, altitude with barometer and GPS, position with visual or GPS data. Cascaded loops, inner fast loops and outer slow loops, are the standard architecture. When you see a drone hover calmly, appreciate the thousands of corrections per second happening inside.

Self-driving vehicles. Longitudinal control manages speed and following distance with radar feedback; lateral control steers along planned paths. MPC shines here.

Industrial process control. Refineries, cement plants, and breweries run thousands of control loops for flow, pressure, level, and temperature. This hidden infrastructure produces almost everything you touch daily.

Smart homes and appliances. Air conditioners, washing machines, refrigerators, and electric cookers all embed control loops. Your water heater at home is a small control laboratory.

Healthcare devices. Infant incubators, dialysis machines, ventilators, and insulin pumps are control systems where human lives depend on stability and safety. Choose reliability over cleverness in such domains.

Notice the pattern: wherever something must stay at a target despite disturbances, control engineering is the answer.

PART 12 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: Your electric iron has no thermostat and stays hot always. Is this open or closed loop, and what is the risk? Model answer: It is open-loop control: the heating runs without measuring temperature. The risk is overheating, fire hazard, and burned clothes. Adding a thermostat closes the loop and holds safe temperature.

Question two: A water heater overshoots to 45 degrees then swings down to 38 repeatedly. What is wrong? Model answer: The loop oscillates, likely due to excessive proportional or integral gain, or large dead time. Reduce gains, add damping with derivative action carefully, insulate to reduce disturbances, and consider anti-windup on the integral.

Question three: Why does pure proportional control often leave steady-state error? Model answer: As the error shrinks, the proportional push shrinks too. At equilibrium, the push must balance the disturbance exactly, which requires a small persistent error to generate it. Integral action removes this by accumulating even small errors over time.

Question four: What is the role of derivative action and when is it dangerous? Model answer: D anticipates change and damps overshoot. It is dangerous with noisy sensors because it amplifies noise, causing actuator chatter. Use filtered measurement and small D gains.

Question five: Why is dead time bad for control? Model answer: Dead time delays feedback, so the controller reacts to old information and tends to overcorrect, causing oscillation. Control must be gentler when dead time is large, or predictive methods like MPC should be used.

Question six: Name three control loops in a quadcopter. Model answer: attitude stabilization from IMU feedback, altitude control from barometer and GPS, and horizontal position control from GPS or vision, usually cascaded with fast inner loops.

Try answering aloud in your own words first. This practice turns knowledge into engineering instinct.

PART 13 - GLOSSARY AND RECAP

Setpoint: the desired value of the output.

Output: the quantity being controlled.

Error: the difference between setpoint and measured output.

Open-loop control: acting without measuring the result.

Closed-loop or feedback control: correcting actions using measurements of the output.

Plant: the physical system being controlled.

Controller: the algorithm or device computing the control action.

Sensor: the device measuring the output.

Actuator: the device executing control, like a motor or heater.

Disturbance: an unwanted influence pushing the output away from the setpoint.

Dynamics: how the system behaves and evolves over time.

Block diagram: a picture of how signals flow through controller, plant, and feedback.

Transfer function: a mathematical relation between a block's input and output.

Stability: the property of returning to balance after a disturbance.

Oscillation: repeated swinging of the output around the setpoint.

Overshoot: going past the setpoint before settling.

Steady-state error: the small persistent gap left after settling.

PID controller: the standard controller combining proportional, integral, and derivative actions.

Tuning: choosing controller gains for desired behavior.

Anti-windup: protection that stops integral term from accumulating during saturation.

Dead time: delay between action and measured effect.

Gain margin and phase margin: safety buffers before instability.

Model predictive control: control that optimizes future actions using a model.

PART 14 - A DEEPER PRACTICAL WALKTHROUGH: CONTROLLING A WATER HEATER

Let me give you a complete practical walkthrough that you can simulate on paper or with an Arduino kit: designing and tuning a control loop for a small water heater.

The plant: a small tank of water with an electric heating element and a temperature sensor. The mission: hold water at 40 degrees Celsius for a laboratory experiment, despite cold water being drawn off from time to time.

Step one, characterize the plant. Before designing any controller, understand what you are controlling. Turn the heater fully on and record temperature every ten seconds. You will see the temperature rise quickly at first, then slow as it approaches a natural maximum where heat lost to the air balances heat added. Note two numbers: how fast the temperature rises, the time constant, and how long the effect of the heater takes to appear at the sensor, the dead time. If the sensor sits far from the heater, dead time is larger and control must be gentler.

Step two, design safety first. Add a hard thermal cut-out that physically disconnects power above 55 degrees, independent of software. Software fails; physics must still protect the tank. Add a current limit on the heater circuit. These layers are not optional in real engineering.

Step three, implement P control and observe. Choose a modest proportional gain: for example, heater power equals 5 percent per degree of error, capped at 100 percent. Set the setpoint to 40 and log the response. You will likely see the temperature approach something like 38 or 39 and settle there: steady-state error caused by heat loss that a shrinking proportional push cannot overcome. Write down the observed error; this is your baseline.

Step four, add integral action. Implement integral as a running sum of error multiplied by a small constant, with anti-windup capping the sum when the heater is already at full power. Raise the integral gain slowly until the steady-state error disappears and the temperature holds at 40. If the temperature starts oscillating in waves, you have too much integral; reduce it.

Step five, consider derivative. If the temperature overshoots noticeably when setpoint changes, add a small derivative term on the filtered measurement. If the sensor signal is noisy, skip derivative or filter heavily; jittery derivative action wears out relays and makes the heater buzz on and off.

Step six, test disturbances. Draw a cup of cold water from the tank while the loop runs. Watch the recovery: how long until 40 degrees returns, and how much it dips. Then insulate the tank with foam and repeat. Insulation reduces disturbance strength and makes the controller's life easier. This teaches a profound lesson: mechanical improvements sometimes beat clever control. The best control design often begins with better hardware.

Step seven, document gains and behavior. Record your final P, I, and D values, the response time, overshoot, and how the system handled disturbances. Future maintainers will bless your documentation. In industry, loop records like this live for decades.

This walkthrough mirrors what process engineers do in power plants, breweries, and hospitals. When you finally run the loop and watch the graph settle exactly on 40 degrees and stay there while you draw water, you will feel the quiet satisfaction of control engineering: the world, gently but firmly, made to behave.

PART 16 - ADDITIONAL PRACTICAL SCENARIOS FOR CONTROL

Let me add further practical scenarios to broaden your control intuition.

Scenario one: controlling a robot gripper holding fragile fruit. The setpoint is the gripping force. A force sensor closes the loop. Too little force drops the fruit; too much crushes it. A gentle integral action removes steady-state error in force while a soft ramp prevents sudden squeeze. Packaging lines and agricultural robots face exactly this challenge, and the tuning objective is not speed but gentle consistency.

Scenario two: automatic voltage stabilization for home electronics. In areas with unstable power supply, an automatic voltage regulator holds output voltage near 220 volts despite swings at the input. Sensors measure output voltage; a controller switches transformer taps or adjusts power electronics; disturbances come from the grid and from heavy appliances switching on. This is closed-loop control protecting your laptop and your television every day.

Scenario three: temperature control in a poultry brooder. Chicks need steady warmth that decreases as they grow. The setpoint follows a schedule; the heater and vents are actuators; disturbances include night cold and door openings. Farmers who use thermostats see better survival rates than those who rely on guesswork. Control engineering directly serves food security.

Scenario four: lane-keeping assistance in a modern car. A camera measures the car's offset from lane center; the controller gently adjusts steering torque; disturbances include wind and road camber. The system warns or corrects when the driver drifts. Here control meets computer vision and human factors: the assistance must feel natural, never startling.

Scenario five: cruise control on a convoy of delivery motorcycles. Platoon control keeps safe spacing between riders at constant speed. Each rider's vehicle senses distance to the one ahead, and controllers adjust throttle. Fuel savings and safety both improve. Logistics companies research exactly this for trucks on highways.

Scenario six: stabilizing a camera gimbal on a moving boat. Waves tilt the boat violently; the gimbal must hold the camera level for filming or inspection. IMU feedback and fast motor control cancel the motion. The tuning goal is rejection of periodic disturbances from waves, which connects to frequency response ideas you will study further.

In each scenario, ask the same four questions you learned: what is the setpoint, what is measured, what is actuated, and what disturbances threaten the target? With those questions, any unfamiliar machine becomes readable as a control loop. That readability is the professional skill this course truly builds. As we close, one reflection to carry with you. Control engineering teaches a life lesson beyond machines: feedback is how anything improves. A student who checks work against the standard and corrects the error is a closed-loop learner. A business that measures customer satisfaction and adjusts is a closed-loop business. A nation that monitors its power grid and repairs drift is a closed-loop system.

Practical habit: whenever a machine misbehaves around you, a dripping tap, a wobbling fan, a car that drifts, try to name the loop and guess the fault. Is the sensor broken? Is the gain too high? Is there dead time? This mental game builds engineering intuition faster than any exercise.

Practical habit two: simulate before you build. Even a simple spreadsheet can model a first-order thermal system and let you test PID gains safely. Simulation saves hardware, time, and tears.
