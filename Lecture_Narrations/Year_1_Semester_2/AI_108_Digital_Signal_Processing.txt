PART 1 - WELCOME: THE SCIENCE OF SIGNALS IN A DIGITAL WORLD

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 108, Digital Signal Processing. Let me open with a question. What do these things have in common: the song playing on your earpiece, the voice call with your family, the ECG machine in a hospital, the seismic sensor warning of earthquakes, the camera that records your face, and the flight controller keeping a drone steady in the wind?

The answer is signals. All of them deal with signals, and all of them rely on signal processing to work. A signal is any quantity that carries information and changes over time or space. Sound is a pressure signal. An image is a light signal spread over space. Your heartbeat is a biological signal. The radio waves carrying your mobile data are electromagnetic signals.

Signal processing is the art and science of extracting useful information from signals and cleaning, transforming, and compressing them. Digital signal processing, or DSP, does this with numbers inside computers and microchips instead of old analog circuits. Almost everything in your digital life runs on DSP: voice calls, music streaming, video conferencing, medical imaging, radar, Wi-Fi, and control systems in robots.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand how continuous signals become digital numbers, how filters clean noise, how the famous Fourier transform reveals hidden frequencies, and how DSP serves real-life systems from hospitals to farms to spacecraft. This course also feeds directly into your robotics, computer vision, and embedded systems work.

PART 2 - WHAT IS A SIGNAL? CONTINUOUS VERSUS DISCRETE

Let us build the foundation carefully.

A continuous-time signal exists at every instant. Think of the temperature outside your window right now. It has a value at every moment: 27.3 degrees at 10:00:00, 27.4 degrees at 10:00:01, and infinitely many values in between. Sound waves in the air are continuous. In mathematics, we write such signals as functions of time, x of t.

A discrete-time signal exists only at specific moments. Imagine checking the temperature only every hour. Now you have a sequence of numbers: 27, 28, 30, 29. This is discrete, written as x of n, where n counts the samples.

Analog signals are continuous in both time and amplitude: they can take any value. Digital signals are discrete in both time and amplitude: sampled at moments and rounded to levels.

Let me give real-life intuition. A vinyl record stores sound as a continuous groove wave. A CD or MP3 stores sound as a stream of numbers. Your phone's microphone captures continuous sound waves, but the instant the sound enters the phone's processor, it becomes numbers. Everything after that, compression, noise removal, echo cancellation, is DSP.

Why go digital at all? Because digital systems are powerful, flexible, and precise. You can copy digital music a thousand times without degradation. You can update a phone's camera quality with software. You can run complex algorithms on a cheap chip. You can store and transmit information efficiently. The analog world is the source, but the digital world is where we do the work.

One caution: the conversion from analog to digital is not magic. It has rules and costs. Sampling too coarsely loses information. Rounding too roughly adds noise. These are the problems of sampling and quantization, which we must now study carefully.

PART 3 - SAMPLING, QUANTIZATION, AND THE NYQUIST THEOREM

How do we turn continuous sound into numbers? Through two steps: sampling and quantization.

Sampling means measuring the signal at regular time intervals. Imagine a camera taking frames of a fast-moving football match. Too few frames and you miss the action. Sampling is like taking snapshots of a wave in time.

Quantization means rounding each measurement to the nearest level in a finite set. Imagine measuring temperature with a ruler marked only in whole degrees: 27.4 becomes 27. Rounding introduces small errors called quantization noise.

Now the golden rule of sampling, the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem. To capture a signal faithfully, the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency present in the signal. This minimum is called the Nyquist rate.

Example. Human hearing goes up to about 20,000 hertz. To record music accurately, we need to sample at least 40,000 times per second. That is why CDs use 44,100 samples per second. Voice calls need much less, because speech mostly lives below 4,000 hertz, so telephone systems sample at 8,000 times per second and save bandwidth.

What happens if we break the rule? Aliasing. High-frequency signals disguise themselves as fake low-frequency signals. Real-life demonstration: in old western movies, wagon wheels sometimes appear to spin backward. The camera samples the spinning wheel too slowly, and fast rotation looks like slow reverse rotation. That is aliasing in your eyes.

To prevent aliasing, we use an anti-aliasing filter: a low-pass filter that removes frequencies above half the sampling rate before sampling. Practical wisdom: every real analog-to-digital system begins with such a filter. Respect Nyquist, and your digital world stays faithful.

One more practical point: bit depth. With 16-bit quantization, we have 65,536 levels per sample. With 24-bit, over 16 million levels. More bits mean less quantization noise and more dynamic range, at the cost of bigger files. Audio engineers know this trade-off by heart.

PART 4 - DISCRETE SIGNALS IN PRACTICE: SEQUENCES AND SYSTEMS

Once a signal is a sequence of numbers, DSP manipulates it with operations and systems.

Basic operations. Scaling multiplies every sample by a constant: turning volume up doubles the samples. Shifting delays a sequence: your phone's echo cancellation aligns what you said with what comes back. Addition mixes two signals: your voice plus background music.

A discrete system transforms input sequences into output sequences. A filter is a system that changes the frequency content. An amplifier boosts. A smoother removes jitter. An equalizer shapes tone.

The workhorse of DSP is convolution. Convolution slides one signal over another, multiplying and summing at each position. Real-life analogy: a moving average over exam scores. To smooth a noisy score chart, you average each point with its neighbors. As this window slides across the data, jitter is reduced and trend becomes visible. That sliding, weighting, and summing is convolution.

Impulse response tells you everything about a linear system. Feed it a single sharp spike, and the output shows how the system responds. Any input can then be understood as a pile of spikes, and the system's total response is the sum of its responses to each spike. This beautiful idea is the superposition principle.

Time domain versus frequency domain. So far we look at signals in time: amplitude versus seconds. There is another equally true view: frequency. A musical chord is one wave in time, but in frequency it is three notes played together. Moving between these views is DSP's superpower, and the bridge is the Fourier transform.

PART 5 - THE FOURIER TRANSFORM: HEARING THE HIDDEN NOTES

Let me introduce the most famous idea in signal processing: the Fourier transform, named after the mathematician Joseph Fourier.

The core insight: any complicated signal can be built by adding simple sine waves of different frequencies, amplitudes, and phases. A square wave is an infinite sum of odd harmonics. Your voice is a mixture of many frequencies. Noise is yet another mixture.

The Fourier transform takes a signal in time and shows its frequency ingredients, like a prism splitting white light into a rainbow. Suddenly you can see: this sound has strong energy at 200 hertz and 400 hertz, and unwanted hiss at 8,000 hertz. Now you know exactly what to remove.

For digital signals, we use the discrete Fourier transform, computed efficiently by the fast Fourier transform algorithm, FFT. The FFT is one of the most important algorithms ever invented. It computes frequency analysis in a fraction of the time naive methods need, and it runs inside billions of devices daily.

Real-life applications of the Fourier idea. Music apps draw spectrum bars dancing with the beat: that is FFT in action. Noise-canceling earphones analyze incoming sound frequencies and generate anti-phase waves to cancel noise. Wi-Fi and 4G systems use related transforms, like OFDM, to pack data into many frequency channels. Seismographs analyze earthquake wave frequencies to estimate distance and strength. Hospitals analyze heart rate variability in frequency domain to assess patient health.

Practical experiment you can try. Record three seconds of sound on your phone: a clap, a hum, and your voice. Use any free audio analysis app to view their spectra. A clap spreads energy across many frequencies like white noise; a hum concentrates at one frequency, like 50 hertz from mains power; your voice shows a rich pattern with formants. Seeing these spectra once changes how you hear the world forever.

PART 6 - FILTERS: CLEANING AND SHAPING SIGNALS

Now the practical heroes: filters. A filter passes some frequencies and blocks others, like a gate for sound.

Low-pass filter: passes low frequencies, blocks highs. Use it to remove high-frequency hiss while keeping bass and voice. It is also the anti-aliasing filter before sampling.

High-pass filter: passes highs, blocks lows. Use it to remove rumble, wind noise, or slow drift in sensor readings.

Band-pass filter: passes a middle band. A radio tuner is a band-pass filter: it selects one station's frequency band and rejects others.

Band-stop or notch filter: blocks a narrow band. Removing 50-hertz mains hum from medical equipment while keeping the patient's tiny heartbeat signals is a classic notch application. In Nigeria and many countries mains hum is 50 hertz; in others, 60 hertz.

Two implementation families. Finite impulse response filters, FIR: the output depends on a finite window of past inputs. They are stable, easy to design, and give linear phase, preserving waveform shape, crucial for audio and ECG. Infinite impulse response filters, IIR: feedback gives sharp frequency responses with less computation, like analog filters such as Butterworth and Chebyshev, but they can be harder to control for phase.

Design tools exist everywhere: filter design toolboxes, code libraries, and even graphical apps where you drag the cutoff frequency. But design wisdom matters more than tools. Ask: what must pass? What must be blocked? What distortion is tolerable? How much computation can the hardware afford?

Real-life case: cleaning an ECG signal. The raw signal contains the heartbeat waveform plus mains hum plus muscle noise plus baseline wander from breathing. A pipeline of high-pass filter removes wander; notch filter removes mains hum; low-pass filter removes muscle noise. The result: a clean trace that a doctor can trust. In healthcare DSP, cleaning is saving.

PART 7 - CONVOLUTION, CORRELATION, AND PATTERN FINDING

Let me go deeper on two related operations that quietly power many systems.

Convolution, as we said, computes each output sample as a weighted combination of nearby input samples. It is how filters work. But convolution is more than filtering. In deep learning, convolutional neural networks learn their own filters by training. The convolution operation is the same mathematical heart, applied to images and sound.

Correlation measures similarity between two signals as one slides over the other. Real-life analogy: recognizing a friend's knock at the door. Your brain correlates the incoming knock pattern against the memory of their knock. High correlation means a match.

Correlation applications. Radar sends a pulse and correlates the echo with the sent pulse to measure distance precisely. GPS receivers correlate satellite codes to find position. Speech recognition correlates sound patterns with learned templates. Seismology correlates tremor patterns across stations to locate quake epicenters.

Auto-correlation examines a signal against delayed copies of itself to find repeating patterns: the pitch of a voice, the tempo of music, the rotation period of a machine from vibration data. Predictive maintenance engineers use exactly this to spot failing bearings before breakdown.

Cross-correlation between two microphones gives time difference of arrival, which locates sound sources, useful in smart rooms and in wildlife monitoring.

These operations feel abstract on paper but become vivid when applied. Take a noisy recording of a heartbeat and correlate it with a clean heartbeat template; watch the match peaks light up. That is pattern recognition at the signal level.

PART 8 - SAMPLING IN IMAGES AND OTHER MULTIDIMENSIONAL SIGNALS

Signals are not only about time. Images are signals over two spatial dimensions. Video adds time. Medical volumes add three dimensions. DSP ideas extend beautifully.

An image is sampled on a pixel grid and quantized to brightness levels. Resolution is spatial sampling rate; bit depth is quantization depth. Aliasing appears as moire patterns, strange wavy stripes on photographed striped shirts or screens. Camera systems fight aliasing with optical low-pass filters and careful sensor design.

Image filtering uses 2D convolution kernels: blurring smooths, sharpening emphasizes edges, denoising cleans grain. Gaussian blur is a low-pass filter in space. Edge detection is high-pass. You met these in computer vision; now you see their DSP roots.

Compression exploits signal structure. JPEG compression applies a frequency transform to blocks of the image, keeps important frequencies, and discards barely visible details. MP3 and AAC do the same for audio, using psychoacoustics: knowledge of what human ears cannot hear. The result: a song file shrinks to a tenth of its size without noticeable quality loss. Understanding DSP explains why streaming works at all.

Video compression like H.264 and H.265 goes further: it transmits only what changes between frames and represents motion as vectors. This is DSP on a massive scale, running in every video call.

Multidimensional DSP also serves radar maps, satellite imagery, and MRI scans, where 2D and 3D transforms extract structure from raw sensor data.

PART 9 - DSP IN AI, ROBOTICS, AND CONTROL SYSTEMS

Let me connect DSP to the rest of your degree, because these subjects are deeply intertwined.

In machine learning, features are often DSP products. Audio classifiers use spectrograms, images of sound in time and frequency. Time-series forecasting smooths and decomposes trends and seasonality, which are DSP ideas. Good features beat fancy models, and DSP helps craft good features.

In natural language and speech, DSP is the front door. Speech recognition begins with sampling audio at 16 kilohertz, pre-emphasis, windowing into frames of 25 milliseconds, and computing frequency features like MFCCs, Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients. Only then does machine learning begin. The quality of DSP decides the ceiling of recognition accuracy.

In computer vision, image filtering, pyramids, and frequency analysis underpin smoothing, enhancement, and texture analysis.

In robotics, sensor signals are noisy. Accelerometers, gyroscopes, and odometry all need filtering. A simple moving average removes jitter; a complementary filter or Kalman filter, which combines predictions with measurements, gives clean orientation estimates for drones and robots. Vibration monitoring predicts failures.

In control systems, frequency response, Bode plots, and stability margins are frequency-domain views of system behavior. Digital controllers implement difference equations derived from filter design. Anti-aliasing and sampling rates limit what control bandwidth is achievable.

In communications, DSP is everything: modulation, demodulation, error correction, echo cancellation, and channel equalization. Your mobile phone is a DSP supercomputer.

This web of connections shows why DSP is a core course. It is the language in which sensing, communication, and computation meet.

PART 10 - PRACTICAL LAB: REMOVING NOISE FROM A HEARTBEAT-LIKE SIGNAL

Let me guide a hands-on mini-project you can do with Python this month.

Goal: take a noisy signal that looks like a heartbeat, contaminated with 50-hertz mains hum and high-frequency hiss, and clean it.

Step one, generate or obtain the signal. Use a public ECG dataset, or simulate: create a simple periodic pulse waveform, add 50-hertz sine hum, and add random white noise. Save as a CSV of samples at 250 samples per second.

Step two, observe in time and frequency. Plot the signal over time; the heartbeat shape is buried. Then compute the FFT with NumPy and plot the magnitude spectrum. Notice the spike at 50 hertz. Seeing the enemy in frequency domain tells you exactly what to fight.

Step three, design the notch. Remove 50 hertz with a narrow band-stop filter. Many libraries provide simple functions; or implement averaging of samples spaced to cancel 50 hertz. Re-plot: hum reduced.

Step four, low-pass the hiss. Apply a moving average or a Butterworth low-pass filter at, say, 40 hertz, keeping heartbeat harmonics while suppressing high-frequency noise.

Step five, high-pass the drift. If baseline wanders, apply a high-pass at about 0.5 hertz to keep the heartbeat while removing slow drift.

Step six, evaluate honestly. Compare before and after. Does the heartbeat shape become visible? Are peaks detectable? Count beats per minute and compare with ground truth. Be careful not to over-filter and distort the very signal you must preserve. In medical DSP, distortion can hide pathology, so conservative design wins.

Step seven, extend. Try your filter chain on a real recording of your own finger on a phone camera, using the flash to detect pulse waves, a technique called photoplethysmography, which is how many wearables measure heart rate. This turns class theory into a personal, memorable experiment.

PART 11 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: Why must sampling rate be at least twice the highest frequency? Model answer: Below that rate, high frequencies masquerade as lower ones through aliasing, and information is irreversibly lost. The Nyquist rate sets the faithful minimum; practical systems sample slightly higher and filter first.

Question two: What is aliasing and give a real-world example. Model answer: Aliasing is a false low-frequency appearance of a faster signal sampled too slowly. The wagon wheel in old films appearing to spin backward is a classic example.

Question three: Difference between low-pass and high-pass filters with one use each. Model answer: Low-pass passes low frequencies and blocks highs, used to remove hiss. High-pass passes highs and blocks lows, used to remove rumble or slow drift.

Question four: Why is the FFT important? Model answer: The FFT computes frequency analysis quickly, making real-time spectrum analysis possible in music, communications, radar, and medical devices.

Question five: How does MP3 compression shrink files? Model answer: It transforms audio to frequency domain, discards components masked by louder sounds or inaudible to humans, and codes the rest efficiently using psychoacoustic models.

Question six: Name two DSP steps before machine learning on speech. Model answer: Sampling at a suitable rate and computing framed spectral features like MFCCs after pre-emphasis and windowing.

Question seven: What role does a notch filter play in ECG? Model answer: It removes narrow-band mains hum at 50 or 60 hertz while preserving heartbeat components, improving diagnostic quality.

PART 12 - GLOSSARY AND RECAP

Signal: a varying quantity carrying information.

Continuous-time signal: defined at every instant.

Discrete-time signal: defined only at sample points.

Sampling: measuring a signal at regular intervals.

Quantization: rounding measurements to discrete levels.

Nyquist rate: minimum sampling rate, twice the highest frequency, to avoid aliasing.

Aliasing: false frequencies from insufficient sampling.

Discrete Fourier transform: conversion from time samples to frequency content.

FFT: the fast algorithm to compute Fourier transforms.

Frequency domain: describing signals by their frequency components.

Spectrum: the display of energy across frequencies.

Filter: a system passing some frequencies and blocking others.

Low-pass filter: passes low frequencies.

High-pass filter: passes high frequencies.

Band-pass filter: passes a middle band.

Notch filter: blocks a narrow band, like mains hum.

FIR filter: finite impulse response filter without feedback in output.

IIR filter: infinite impulse response filter using feedback.

Convolution: weighted sliding combination of signals.

Correlation: sliding similarity measure between signals.

Spectrogram: a picture of frequency content changing over time.

MFCC: speech features summarizing spectral shape for recognition.

Compression: reducing data size while preserving perceived quality.

Quantization noise: error from rounding to finite levels.

Dynamic range: ratio between largest and smallest representable signals.

PART 13 - DSP CAREERS, FUTURE TRENDS, AND CLOSING REFLECTIONS

Let me sketch where DSP can take you professionally, then close with reflections.

DSP engineers design audio systems for phones, studios, and hearing aids. Communication engineers build the modems and networks that carry the internet. Biomedical engineers develop ECG, EEG, ultrasound, and MRI processing. Radar and sonar engineers serve defense, aviation, and marine industries. Control engineers shape loop behavior in frequency domain. And in the AI era, DSP engineers craft the features and front-ends that make machine learning shine.

Future trends worth watching. Edge DSP: processing signals directly on tiny chips in phones, earbuds, and sensors, saving battery and privacy. Neural DSP: learned filters that replace hand-crafted ones, like audio enhancement networks that clean calls in real time. Compressed sensing: reconstructing signals from fewer samples than Nyquist seems to allow, when signals are sparse, revolutionizing medical scanning speed. And event-based sensing, like event cameras that output only brightness changes at microsecond speed, redefining high-speed vision.

Reflections to carry. DSP teaches you to see the invisible structure of the world: the frequencies inside sounds, the noise inside measurements, the information inside waves. It teaches discipline: violating Nyquist has consequences, quantization has costs, filtering has trade-offs. And it teaches humility: nature is analog and rich; the digital world is our faithful translation, and fidelity is earned through careful engineering.

Habit to build this week: whenever you hear noise, a generator humming, a fan whirring, a call crackling, ask yourself what frequencies it occupies and what filter would remove it. This habit turns everyday sounds into a lifelong laboratory.

PART 14 - ADDITIONAL PRACTICAL SCENARIOS FOR DSP

Scenario one: echo cancellation in voice calls. When you call someone, your voice travels to their phone, plays from their speaker, and leaks into their microphone, returning to you as an echo. DSP models the echo path and subtracts a predicted echo from the microphone signal. Adaptive filters learn the changing room acoustics in real time. Without this, calls would be unusable; with it, conversations feel natural.

Scenario two: noise suppression in drone video. A drone filming a farm in wind carries motor hum and propeller noise in its audio. A low-pass and notch combination cleans the audio track; spectral subtraction removes steady hum while keeping birdsong and speech. Filmmakers rely on such processing in every production.

Scenario three: radar speed measurement for traffic safety. A radar gun emits radio waves that bounce off moving cars. The frequency of the echo shifts depending on speed, the Doppler effect. DSP measures this tiny frequency shift precisely and converts it to kilometers per hour. Traffic authorities use this daily.

Scenario four: vibration analysis for factory machines. Accelerometers on a motor produce vibration signals. FFT reveals characteristic frequencies of bearings and gears. New peaks appear weeks before failure, allowing planned maintenance instead of costly breakdowns. This is predictive maintenance's signal processing core.

Scenario five: audio fingerprinting for music recognition. Apps like song identification tools compute a compact spectral fingerprint of a few seconds of audio and match it against a huge database of fingerprints. Correlation and hashing make it fast even with background noise at a party.

Scenario six: ultrasound imaging for prenatal care. Transducers send sound pulses into the body and receive echoes from tissue boundaries. DSP delays, filters, and beamforms these echoes into real-time images. Expectant parents see their unborn child because of DSP.

PART 15 - COMMON MISTAKES AND FINAL EXAM TIPS

Let me close with the mistakes students most often make in DSP, so you can avoid them.

Mistake one: confusing sample rate with frequency. The sample rate is how often we measure; frequency is how fast the underlying signal wiggles. A 1,000-hertz tone sampled at 8,000 hertz is perfectly captured; the same tone sampled at 1,500 hertz becomes an alias. Keep these two ideas separate in your mind and on your answer sheet.

Mistake two: forgetting the anti-aliasing filter. Students jump straight from analog to sampling in exercises. In real systems, an analog low-pass filter always comes first. When you design or describe a system, mention it; examiners and engineers notice.

Mistake three: over-filtering. Enthusiasm leads to aggressive filters that remove the signal along with the noise. A doctor needs the subtle notch in the ECG waveform that indicates disease; smooth it away and you smooth away the diagnosis. Filter conservatively and always compare before and after.

Mistake four: ignoring phase. Two filters can have identical magnitude responses but very different phase behavior. For audio and medical signals, phase distortion can smear sharp events in time. FIR filters with linear phase are often chosen for exactly this reason.

Mistake five: treating dB as a mystery. The decibel is just a logarithmic way to express ratios: 20 dB is a factor of ten in amplitude, 6 dB is about a factor of two. Practice converting a few values until it feels natural. Frequency response plots use dB everywhere, and comfort with them is expected in industry.

Exam tip one: draw before you compute. Sketch the spectrum before and after each filter stage. Pictures organize your thinking and earn method marks.

Exam tip two: always state Nyquist explicitly when a sampling question appears. Write the highest frequency present, double it, and compare with the given rate. This small step wins easy marks.

Exam tip three: memorize the roles of the four filter types and one application each. These definitions appear in almost every exam.

Exam tip four: for FFT questions, remember the symmetry property and that frequency resolution equals sampling rate divided by number of points. Small formulas, big marks.

Study habit: build a one-page DSP formula sheet in your own handwriting, with one line of explanation per formula. The act of writing it teaches more than reading three pages of notes. Bring that understanding, not just the sheet, into the exam hall.

PART 16 - QUICK REFERENCE: DSP IN EVERYDAY DEVICES

Let me lock in understanding with a rapid tour of everyday devices and the DSP inside them.

Your smartphone. The microphone signal is sampled, filtered to remove noise, compressed for transmission, and decompressed at the other end. The camera applies image DSP: denoising in low light, sharpening, and JPEG compression. The GPS receiver correlates satellite signals. The accelerometer's readings are filtered for screen rotation and step counting. A single phone contains dozens of DSP pipelines working together.

Your earbuds. They capture your voice with microphones, apply beamforming to focus on your speech, run echo cancellation against the music playing, compress the audio for Bluetooth, and in noise-canceling models, generate anti-noise in real time. All of this happens in a chip smaller than your fingernail.

Your television. It upscales low-resolution video with spatial interpolation filters, reduces compression artifacts, adjusts contrast with local tone mapping, and smooths motion with frame interpolation. Broadcast signals are demodulated and error-corrected before a single pixel appears.

Your car. Engine management reads crank sensors and filters noise. The radio demodulates FM or digital audio. Parking sensors use ultrasonic pulses and correlation. Stability control filters accelerometer data. The modern car carries dozens of DSP applications.

A weather station. Wind, temperature, and humidity sensors produce noisy readings; averaging and calibration filters clean them; data is compressed and radioed to a central server where analysts apply spectral analysis to atmospheric signals.

A hospital ward. Monitors filter ECG, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure waveforms continuously; alarms trigger on filtered thresholds; records are compressed for storage. Every beep you hear has passed through DSP.

This rapid tour should leave you with one lasting impression: DSP is not a niche subject for specialists. It is the universal plumbing of the information age. Every engineer who understands it speaks the native language of sensors, communication, and modern machines.

PART 17 - FINAL GLOSSARY CHECK AND COURSE CLOSURE

Let me add a few more essential terms to complete your DSP toolkit.

Pre-emphasis: boosting higher frequencies before analysis to balance the spectrum, commonly used in speech processing before feature extraction.

Windowing: multiplying a slice of signal by a smooth window function, like Hamming or Hanning, before FFT, to reduce spectral leakage at the edges of the slice.

Spectral leakage: smearing of frequency energy caused by analyzing a finite slice of signal without a window.

Frequency resolution: the ability to distinguish close frequencies, equal to the sampling rate divided by the number of samples in the transform.

Amplitude modulation: encoding information by varying the height of a carrier wave, the classic idea behind AM radio.

Doppler shift: change in observed frequency when source and observer move relative to each other, used in radar speed guns and medical ultrasound.

Latency: the delay between input and output in a real-time DSP system; low latency matters for live calls, hearing aids, and control loops.

Real-time processing: handling samples fast enough to keep up with the live signal stream; for a 48 kilohertz audio stream, the system must finish each block of samples before the next arrives.

With these terms, your DSP vocabulary covers the essentials of the field. Carry them into your robotics, vision, and embedded systems work: signals are everywhere, and now you speak their language.

From the heartbeat of a patient to the radio of a drone, from the song on your phone to the radar of a police officer, DSP is the quiet science behind it all.
