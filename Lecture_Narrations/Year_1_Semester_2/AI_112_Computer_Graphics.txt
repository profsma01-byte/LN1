PART 1 - WELCOME: THE ART AND SCIENCE OF MAKING PICTURES WITH MATHEMATICS

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 112, Computer Graphics. Every video game you have played, every animated movie you have loved, every special effect that took your breath away, every map on your phone, and every 3D model of a product you viewed online is the work of computer graphics.

Computer graphics is the field that creates, manipulates, and displays images using computers. It blends mathematics, physics, art, and engineering. On one side sit vectors, matrices, and light equations. On the other sit color, composition, and the feeling of beauty. As engineers with an artistic edge, you will master both sides.

Why does computer graphics matter in an AI and robotics degree? Three big reasons. First, simulation: robots and autonomous systems are trained and tested in virtual worlds built with graphics. Second, visualization: engineers, doctors, and scientists need to see data, from brain scans to stress analyses to the robot's planned path. Third, the metaverse frontier: virtual and augmented reality are graphics at their core, and your program includes VR ahead.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand how pictures are represented, how 2D and 3D transformations move and shape objects, how a 3D scene becomes pixels on your screen through the rendering pipeline, how light and materials create realism, and how graphics serves games, film, design, and robotics.

Let us paint with numbers.

PART 2 - PIXELS, RESOLUTION, AND COLOR: HOW IMAGES LIVE IN COMPUTERS

Let us start from the smallest brick: the pixel.

A pixel, short for picture element, is one tiny dot of color. Your screen is a vast mosaic of pixels. A full HD screen has 1920 pixels across and 1080 down: more than two million dots, refreshed dozens of times per second. Your eyes blend the dots into smooth images.

Each pixel's color is described by numbers. The common RGB model uses red, green, and blue components, usually from 0 to 255 each. Pure red is 255, 0, 0. White is 255, 255, 255. Black is 0, 0, 0. Mixing gives millions of colors. Your phone camera captures RGB; your screen displays RGB.

But there are other color models. HSV stands for hue, saturation, and value; artists find it more intuitive: choose the color family, how pure it is, and how bright. CMYK serves printing with cyan, magenta, yellow, and black inks. Grayscale uses one brightness number per pixel. Graphics engineers convert between models constantly.

Resolution and anti-aliasing. Low resolution looks blocky because pixels are large relative to detail. Anti-aliasing smooths jagged edges by blending edge pixels with partial colors, like the soft edge of a brush instead of a stair step. Modern games offer anti-aliasing settings; the technique is the same blending mathematics.

Bit depth matters here too. Eight bits per channel is standard for display. High dynamic range imagery uses more bits to capture the dazzling brightness of real skies and the deep detail of shadows. Understanding bit depth helps you produce images that do not band or clip.

Practical intuition test. Zoom into any photo on your phone until you see the mosaic of colored dots. Then zoom out and watch the picture emerge. Graphics engineering is the craft of placing those dots with intent, at speed, billions of times per second.

PART 3 - COORDINATES AND TRANSFORMATIONS: MOVING THE VIRTUAL WORLD

Now the mathematical skeleton of graphics: coordinates and transformations.

In 2D graphics, every point has coordinates x and y, like a map. Shapes are lists of points connected by lines or filled with color. Drawing a triangle means placing three points and filling between them.

In 3D graphics, points have x, y, and z: width, height, and depth. A cube is eight points connected into six faces. A virtual world is a sea of such points, and everything you see in a 3D game is built from them.

Transformations move and shape objects. Translation slides an object: add ten to x and the object shifts right. Scaling enlarges or shrinks: multiply coordinates by two and the object doubles. Rotation spins an object around an axis; the mathematics uses sine and cosine. Shear slants. These four transformations, combined in sequence, position every object in every scene.

The beautiful engineering trick: represent transformations as matrices. Then combining moves is just multiplying matrices. One final matrix can encode a whole chain: rotate, then scale, then translate. The graphics card applies it to millions of points in parallel. Matrices are the engine under the hood of every 3D engine.

Homogeneous coordinates add a fourth number, w, to the standard three. This elegant extension lets translations be expressed as matrix multiplications like every other transformation, and it paves the way for perspective projection. You will practice the mechanics in coursework; today, hold the idea: every move is a matrix, and matrix multiplication chains moves.

A practical example. Designing a robot arm in simulation: each link is a set of points; the base sits at the origin; each joint rotates its child link by an angle. Applying transformation matrices down the chain places the whole arm in space. This is exactly how forward kinematics renders in robotics simulators. Graphics and robot kinematics share the same mathematical bones.

World, view, and camera coordinates. Objects live in world coordinates; the camera has its own position and orientation; rendering happens relative to the camera. Converting between these frames is transformation work again. When you look around a game world, you are transforming the world around a moving camera.

PART 4 - THE RENDERING PIPELINE: FROM SCENE TO SCREEN

How does a 3D scene become the flat image on your screen? Through the rendering pipeline, the assembly line of graphics.

Step one, modeling. Describe objects as geometry: vertices, edges, faces. Real models may have millions of vertices: a game character, a scanned building, a terrain.

Step two, transformation and projection. Move objects into place with matrices, then project the 3D scene onto a 2D view. Perspective projection makes distant objects smaller, as in real life, using the geometry of similar triangles and homogeneous coordinates. Orthographic projection keeps parallel lines parallel, useful for technical drawings and blueprints.

Step three, clipping. Cut away what is behind or beside the camera view. No need to draw what no one can see; clipping saves enormous work.

Step four, rasterization. Convert projected triangles into pixels. Each triangle is scanned and filled with fragments, the pixel candidates. This is the heart of real-time rendering; graphics cards rasterize billions of triangles per second.

Step five, shading and texturing. Compute each fragment's color from lighting, materials, and textures. Textures are images wrapped onto surfaces: brick patterns on walls, skin tones on faces. Shading models decide how light interacts; more on this in the next parts.

Step six, visibility. Decide which surfaces are in front. The z-buffer records each pixel's depth; the nearest surface wins. Without depth testing, back walls would draw over front walls.

Step seven, output and display. The finished frame buffer, the full image, is sent to your screen at 60, 120, or more frames per second. Each frame is a complete picture; speed creates the illusion of motion.

Real-time versus offline rendering. Games and VR need real-time rendering: 90 frames per second for smoothness and comfort. Animated films use offline rendering: hours per frame are acceptable for perfect quality. Same principles, different budgets.

For robotics, rendering simulates cameras and lidar views of virtual worlds where robots learn. The pipeline you just learned produces the images robot networks train on.

PART 5 - LIGHTING, SHADING, AND MATERIALS: THE SOUL OF REALISM

Geometry gives shape; light gives soul. Let us study illumination.

Light sources come in types. Ambient light is the general soft light everywhere, like daylight bouncing around a room. Directional light has parallel rays, like sunlight. Point light radiates from a spot, like a bulb. Spotlights cone in one direction, like a torch. Area lights have shape and size, producing soft shadows.

Materials describe how surfaces react. Matte surfaces, like chalk, scatter light in all directions. Glossy surfaces, like polished metal, reflect light like mirrors. Transparent surfaces, like glass, transmit light with bending. Shiny plastic sits between. The technical families: diffuse reflection for matte, specular reflection for highlights, and refraction for transparency.

The Phong shading model is a classic teaching tool. It combines ambient light, diffuse reflection depending on the angle between light and surface, and specular reflection for highlights. The bright spot on an apple is specular; the soft glow of its skin is diffuse. Modern physically based rendering, PBR, extends these ideas with measurable material properties and produces film-quality realism in real time.

Shading techniques. Flat shading colors each face one shade; faceted look. Gouraud shading interpolates colors across faces; smoother. Phong shading interpolates normals; smoother still. Normal vectors, arrows sticking out of surfaces, are the key data: lighting calculations live on their angles to light.

Shadows shape depth perception. Shadow mapping renders the scene from the light's viewpoint to compute what is lit and what is shadowed. Shadow volumes trace geometry. Soft shadows from area lights are harder and more beautiful. Without shadows, scenes look fake; with them, scenes gain weight and place.

Real-life application: architectural visualization. Before a building is built, clients walk through rendered rooms lit by simulated sun at different hours. Materials show how marble and wood will feel. Decisions worth millions are made from these images. Graphics engineers serve architecture, film, medicine, and design by making light computable.

PART 6 - MODELLING THE WORLD: GEOMETRY, CURVES, AND TERRAIN

Where do 3D objects come from? Let us tour modeling.

Polygonal modeling builds shapes from triangles and quads, the workhorse of games and simulation. Modeling software lets artists push and pull vertices like digital clay. Efficiency matters: game models balance detail with polygon budgets for speed.

Curves and surfaces. Bezier curves, named after engineer Pierre Bezier, and B-splines describe smooth curves with a few control points. Move a point, and the curve flows elegantly. Car bodies, fonts, and character outlines use these curves. NURBS extend the idea to smooth 3D surfaces for industrial design.

Procedural generation creates content with algorithms rather than by hand. Fractals generate mountains, coastlines, and clouds. Noise functions create natural-looking variation. Entire game worlds are grown procedurally; the algorithm is the artist's brush. This connects to recursion and mathematics you already know.

Scanning the real world. Photogrammetry builds 3D models from many photographs. Structured light and lidar scanners capture rooms and objects precisely. Museums scan artifacts; surgeons scan bodies; your robotics courses will use scanned environments for localization and mapping practice.

Terrain and vegetation. Height maps paint elevation; water, grass, and trees are special systems with their own rules. Wind animations sway trees with noise-driven motion. Realism lives in these details.

Practical tip: open a free 3D tool this month, Blender is excellent and free. Create a table from cubes, a cup from a cylinder, and render a small scene. The hands-on contact with vertices, materials, and lights builds intuition that no lecture can replace.

PART 7 - ANIMATION: BRINGING PICTURES TO LIFE

Static beauty is one art; movement is another. Animation creates the illusion of life.

Frame-by-frame basics. Animation at 24 frames per second feels smooth to cinema; games aim higher for responsiveness. Each frame is rendered fresh or updated cleverly.

Keyframing. Artists set key poses; the computer interpolates between them. The in-between frames, called tweens, are computed with easing: slow starts, natural arcs, gentle stops. Disney's principles of animation, squash and stretch, anticipation, follow-through, are translated into rules for curves.

Rigging and skinning. Characters have skeletons, rigs of virtual bones and joints. Skinning binds the mesh surface to bones so the surface bends naturally. Animators move bones; the character moves. Your robot kinematics course shares these bones and joints ideas; graphics and robotics are twins here.

Motion capture. Record real actors with sensors and cameras; their movements drive digital characters. Gollum in film, game athletes, and dancing avatars all come from motion capture. The data is cleaned and retargeted to fit digital skeletons.

Physics-based animation. Gravity, collisions, cloth, hair, water, and fire simulated with equations. Springs and masses make hair bounce; particle systems make fire and rain; fluid simulations make oceans. When physics drives motion, movement obeys the world.

Path and camera animation. Cameras fly through scenes with smooth splines. In robotics visualization, animated robot paths show planned trajectories before real machines move. This is how engineers review motions safely.

Practical: in Blender, rig a simple character with two bones and make it wave. That single exercise teaches more about animation pipelines than pages of theory.

PART 8 - GRAPHICS FOR VR, AR, AND ROBOTICS

Let me connect graphics to your wider degree.

Virtual reality demands graphics at extreme discipline. The headset shows one image per eye; together they create depth through stereoscopy. Frame rate must stay high and latency low; below 90 frames per second and under 20 milliseconds of motion-to-photon delay, or users feel motion sickness. Foveated rendering, detailed only where you look, saves computation. VR is graphics under medical-grade performance constraints.

Augmented reality overlays graphics on the real world. Your phone measures the scene with cameras and sensors, then places virtual furniture, arrows, or characters onto it convincingly. Occlusion, virtual objects behind real ones, and lighting matching make the illusion hold.

Simulation for robotics. Gazebo, Isaac Sim, Webots, and Unity render worlds where robots learn perception and control. Simulated cameras must look realistic enough that vision networks transfer to real sensors. Graphics fidelity directly shapes sim-to-real success.

Scientific and medical visualization. Engineers see airflow over wings; doctors navigate 3D reconstructions of organs; climate scientists fly through storm simulations. Graphics turns data into insight.

Game development and the metaverse. Real-time engines like Unity and Unreal Engine are platforms not only for games but for training simulators, digital twins, and collaborative virtual spaces. Learning one engine multiplies your opportunities across industries.

Digital twins. A living, rendered replica of a factory, a city, or a machine, fed by live sensor data. Engineers test changes in the twin before touching the real system. Graphics gives the twin its face.

PART 9 - PRACTICAL LAB: YOUR FIRST RENDERED SCENE

Let me build your first real graphics project: a small 3D scene of a robot room.

Step one, install Blender. It is free, cross-platform, and industry respected. Learn the viewport: orbit, pan, zoom.

Step two, block the scene. Add a floor plane, cube walls, and a simple robot from primitives: a cylinder body, sphere head, box arms. Arrange the room with a table and a lamp object.

Step three, materials and color. Give the floor a wood-like texture from built-in materials; make the robot metal and plastic. Notice how specular highlights sell the material story.

Step four, lighting. Add a sun light with an angle for dramatic shadows and an area light as a lamp glow. Render once and compare with one light only; lighting is half the image.

Step five, camera and composition. Place the camera at robot eye level. Apply the rule of thirds: place the robot off-center. Render again; composition is the other half.

Step six, render settings. Start with the real-time engine Eevee for speed; then try Cycles for physically based quality. Compare render times and quality; this teaches the real-time versus offline trade-off directly.

Step seven, export and present. Save renders as PNG. For extra credit, animate the robot arm with keyframes waving over five seconds and export a short video.

What you learn: modeling, materials, lighting, composition, rendering modes, and animation basics in one project. Document renders and settings in a one-page report; that habit serves every future visualization project.

PART 10 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: What is a pixel and how is its color typically represented? Model answer: A pixel is the smallest dot of a digital image. Its color is usually represented by three numbers: red, green, and blue components, each commonly from 0 to 255.

Question two: Why do we use matrices for transformations? Model answer: Matrices let us compose many moves, rotate, scale, translate, into a single multiplication applied uniformly to all points, which is fast on parallel hardware and mathematically clean.

Question three: Explain perspective projection in plain words. Model answer: It makes distant objects appear smaller, mimicking how human eyes see, by projecting 3D points onto a 2D plane through a viewpoint, using similar triangles and homogeneous coordinates.

Question four: What does the z-buffer do? Model answer: It stores the depth of the closest surface drawn at each pixel, so that nearer objects correctly cover farther ones during rendering.

Question five: Compare real-time and offline rendering. Model answer: Real-time rendering must produce frames in milliseconds for games and VR; offline rendering can spend hours per frame for film-quality results. Both use the same core principles with different quality and speed budgets.

Question six: Name two ways computer graphics serves robotics. Model answer: Simulation environments where robots train and test safely, and visualization of sensor data, plans, and digital twins for engineering review.

PART 11 - GLOSSARY AND CLOSING RECAP

Pixel: the smallest element of a digital image.

RGB: color model with red, green, and blue components.

Resolution: the number of pixels across and down in an image.

Anti-aliasing: smoothing jagged edges by blending colors.

Vertex: a point in 2D or 3D space.

Polygon mesh: a surface built from triangles or quads.

Transformation: moving or shaping objects via translation, rotation, scaling.

Matrix: the mathematical structure encoding transformations.

Homogeneous coordinates: extended coordinates enabling uniform transforms and perspective.

Perspective projection: making distant objects smaller as human eyes see.

Rasterization: converting geometric shapes into pixels.

Rendering pipeline: the assembly line from 3D scene to 2D image.

Shading: computing pixel colors from light and materials.

Texture: an image mapped onto a surface.

Normal vector: an arrow perpendicular to a surface used in lighting.

Z-buffer: depth storage deciding which surfaces are in front.

Shadow mapping: computing shadows by rendering from the light's view.

Physically based rendering: realistic shading with measured material properties.

Bezier curve: a smooth curve defined by control points.

Procedural generation: creating content with algorithms.

Keyframe: a defined pose; frames between are interpolated.

Rigging: building virtual bones and joints for characters.

Motion capture: recording real movement to drive digital characters.

Foveated rendering: full detail only where the eye looks, used in VR.

Digital twin: a live virtual replica of a real system.

PART 12 - CAREERS, FUTURE TRENDS, AND FINAL REFLECTIONS

Let me open the door to careers and the future of computer graphics.

Careers. Game developer building worlds players love. Technical artist bridging art and code: shaders, tools, and pipelines. Rendering engineer squeezing beauty from hardware budgets. Simulation engineer building virtual testbeds for robots, cars, and aircraft. Visualization engineer helping doctors, scientists, and designers see their data. AR and VR developer crafting immersive experiences for training, therapy, and commerce. Film and animation technical director shaping the impossible on screen. In every case, the mix of mathematics and artistry is your signature.

Future trends. Real-time ray tracing brings film-quality lighting to games and interactive design. Neural rendering blends machine learning with graphics: neural radiance fields and Gaussian splatting reconstruct photoreal 3D scenes from ordinary photos, and your AI background positions you perfectly for this fusion. Generative AI creates textures, models, and even entire scenes from text descriptions; the graphics engineer of tomorrow curates and controls these engines. Cloud rendering streams photoreal worlds to light devices. And haptics plus graphics will make virtual touch as convincing as virtual sight.

Reflections. Computer graphics taught you that engineering can be beautiful and beauty can be engineered. It taught you precision: matrices and light equations do not forgive sloppiness. And it taught you service: graphics exists to help people see, decide, learn, and dream. From a surgeon rehearsing on a 3D organ to a child marveling at an animated world, your pixels touch human lives.

Carry three habits. First, look at images analytically: ask where the light comes from, how the surface reflects, and how the geometry is built. Second, practice rendering regularly: one small scene a week builds craft faster than occasional marathons. Third, fuse your skills: graphics plus AI plus robotics is a rare and powerful combination that few engineers possess.

PART 13 - MORE REAL-LIFE CASE STUDIES IN COMPUTER GRAPHICS

Case study one: graphics for surgical planning. A patient needs a complex liver operation. CT scans of the liver are segmented to separate tumor from healthy tissue and blood vessels. The 3D reconstruction lets surgeons rotate the organ, measure tumor margins, and rehearse the cut strategy before the first incision. The graphics engineer's choices in color mapping, transparency, and depth cues directly support life-and-death decisions. This is visualization in its most human service.

Case study two: a driving simulator for autonomous vehicle training. The graphics engine renders roads, weather, traffic, and pedestrians from every angle while physics keeps vehicles realistic. Thousands of edge cases, a child chasing a ball into the road, a night-time rainstorm, a broken traffic light, are generated virtually so that AI systems learn safely before touching real streets. Every frame of that simulator is the rendering pipeline doing its work at scale.

Case study three: an architectural walkthrough for a new hospital in Ibadan. Before construction, stakeholders tour the building in VR: checking that corridors handle stretcher traffic, that natural light reaches wards, that signage is visible. Design flaws that would cost millions to fix in concrete are corrected at zero cost in pixels. Graphics engineers collaborate with architects to make better buildings before they exist.

Case study four: cultural heritage preservation. A museum scans ancient artifacts and palaces with photogrammetry and laser scanning. Graphics pipelines restore color, repair geometry virtually, and publish interactive 3D exhibits worldwide. A student in any country can examine a sculpture from Benin bronze collections up close. Graphics becomes memory and education.

Case study five: an engineering digital twin of a factory line. Live sensor data drives a rendered replica: machines animate their real states, bottlenecks glow in heat maps, and proposed changes are simulated before implementation. Managers make faster, surer decisions because they can see the system at work.

Case study six: weather visualization on the evening news. Those swirling storm maps are graphics: satellite and radar data mapped onto a rendered globe with color scales chosen for clarity. The graphics engineer behind the scenes decides how truth becomes visible to millions.

In each case, notice the same recipe: gather data or models, choose representations that clarify, light and compose for understanding, and deliver through the pipeline at the needed speed. Whether the audience is a surgeon, a mayor, or a child, the principles hold.

PART 14 - EXAM TIPS AND QUICK REFERENCE FOR GRAPHICS

Exam tip one: know the pipeline stages in order and what each does. Examiners love asking for the journey from vertex to pixel; practice drawing the flow from model to transformation to projection to rasterization to shading to display.

Exam tip two: be able to multiply a 2D point by a simple transformation matrix by hand. A rotation or translation of a triangle on paper is a classic question; practice two examples until smooth.

Exam tip three: explain perspective versus orthographic projection with one example each. Perspective for games and realism; orthographic for blueprints and technical views.

Exam tip four: define key terms crisply: vertex, polygon, texture, normal, z-buffer, anti-aliasing. One-sentence definitions earn marks quickly.

Exam tip five: compare flat, Gouraud, and Phong shading in one line each; know that normals are interpolated in Phong.

Exam tip six: know why frame rate and latency matter in VR; connect to motion sickness and human perception.

Quick reference of formulas and facts worth memorizing. RGB values from zero to 255 per channel. Translation adds to coordinates; scaling multiplies; rotation uses sine and cosine of the angle. Homogeneous coordinates add a w component. Perspective divides by depth to shrink distant objects. The z-buffer stores nearest depth per pixel. Anti-aliasing blends edge pixels. Keyframes define poses; interpolation fills between. Real-time needs milliseconds; offline accepts minutes or hours. Keep these on one handwritten sheet and the exam hall will feel familiar.

Finally, the study habit that wins graphics exams: sketch constantly. Sketch the pipeline. Sketch a scene with its light sources. Sketch a transformation sequence. Pictures are the language of this course; answer in pictures and words together.

PART 16 - FINAL GLOSSARY CHECK AND FAREWELL

A last glossary extension to complete your graphics vocabulary.

Fragment: a candidate pixel produced by rasterization before final color and depth tests.

Shader: a small program that runs per vertex or per fragment to compute position, color, and lighting on the GPU.

Frame buffer: the memory holding the complete image before display.

Depth of field: the artistic effect where near or far objects blur, mimicking real camera lenses.

Global illumination: light bouncing between surfaces, producing soft indirect lighting that sells realism.

Level of detail: showing simpler models when objects are far away to save computation.

UV mapping: unwrapping a 3D surface into a flat 2D layout so textures wrap correctly, like peeling an orange skin flat.

With these terms, your graphics vocabulary spans the pipeline from vertex to screen. Use it in reports and interviews with confidence.
