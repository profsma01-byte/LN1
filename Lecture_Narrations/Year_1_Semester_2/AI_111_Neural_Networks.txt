PART 1 - WELCOME: MACHINES INSPIRED BY THE BRAIN

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 111, Neural Networks. This is one of the most exciting courses in your program. Neural networks are the engine behind face recognition, voice assistants, machine translation, self-driving cars, medical image analysis, and the generative AI tools that write and draw. Understanding neural networks is understanding the modern AI revolution from the inside.

Let me set expectations honestly. Neural networks are inspired by the brain, but they are not brains. A biological neuron is a living cell of astonishing complexity. An artificial neuron is a small calculator that multiplies, adds, and passes a result forward. The power comes not from copying nature perfectly, but from connecting many simple units in layers and learning from data. Simple parts, vast networks, remarkable results.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand what an artificial neuron does, how neurons form networks, how networks learn through backpropagation and gradient descent, how to train them without falling into common traps, and how the main architectures apply to real problems in your robotics and AI degree. We will use simple numbers and plain words first; the deep mathematics will build on this intuition in later courses.

One promise: by the time we finish the walkthrough with real numbers, you will be able to explain neural networks to a friend without a single equation. That is true understanding.

PART 2 - THE ARTIFICIAL NEURON: A TINY DECISION MAKER

Let us build the smallest piece: one artificial neuron, sometimes called a perceptron.

A neuron receives several input numbers. Imagine a neuron deciding whether to approve a small loan. Inputs might be: income score, past repayment score, and business stability score. Each input has a weight, which says how important that input is. Maybe past repayment matters twice as much as business stability.

The neuron first computes a weighted sum: multiply each input by its weight and add everything up. Then it adds a bias, a small constant that shifts the threshold, like an approval rule saying "be slightly strict" or "be slightly generous." Finally, it passes the result through an activation function, which decides the output.

Let us use numbers. Inputs: income score 0.8, repayment score 0.9, stability score 0.5. Weights: 0.4, 0.5, 0.1. Bias: negative 0.2. Weighted sum: 0.8 times 0.4 is 0.32; 0.9 times 0.5 is 0.45; 0.5 times 0.1 is 0.05; total 0.82; minus 0.2 gives 0.62.

Now the activation function. The simplest is the step function: if the result is above zero, output one; otherwise output zero. Our result 0.62 is positive, so the neuron approves. But modern networks usually use smoother functions. The sigmoid function squashes any number into a range between zero and one, which we can read as a probability: our 0.62 would map to about 0.65, meaning sixty-five percent approval confidence.

Other activations you will meet. ReLU, the rectified linear unit: output the input if positive, otherwise zero. It is simple, fast, and the default favorite in deep networks. Tanh, which squeezes values between negative one and one, useful when centered data matters.

Why do we need nonlinear activations? Because without them, stacking layers collapses into one big linear equation, no matter how deep the network. Nonlinearity is what gives networks their expressive power to model curves, images, language, and the messy real world.

One neuron is humble. Its power emerges when we connect thousands and millions of them in layers. That is our next step.

PART 3 - FROM NEURONS TO NETWORKS: LAYERS AND LEARNING

Let us connect neurons into a network.

A neural network is arranged in layers. The input layer receives the raw data: pixel values, sound samples, or numbers like our loan features. Hidden layers sit between input and output; they transform the data step by step, extracting patterns. The output layer gives the final answer: a class, a number, or a probability.

A network with one hidden layer can approximate many functions. Networks with many hidden layers are called deep networks, and this depth is what deep learning means. Depth builds hierarchy. In image recognition, early layers detect edges, middle layers detect shapes like eyes and wheels, and deep layers detect whole objects like faces and cars. Each layer refines the understanding.

How does the network connect? Usually fully connected between adjacent layers: every neuron in one layer talks to every neuron in the next. Later in computer vision courses, you will meet specialized connections like convolutional layers that look at local patches of images.

Now the great question: how does the network learn? Not by rules we write, but by adjusting its weights and biases to reduce errors. The learning loop works like this. First, show the network an example, say a picture of a cat with the label cat. The network makes a guess, maybe thirty percent cat, seventy percent dog. Second, measure the error with a loss function: how wrong was the guess? Cross-entropy loss is common for classification; mean squared error is common for regression. Third, adjust every weight and bias slightly to reduce the error. Fourth, repeat with many examples, many times.

The adjustment method is gradient descent, which you met in machine learning: feel the slope of the error and step downhill. The clever part is computing how much each weight contributed to the error. That is the job of backpropagation.

Backpropagation is bookkeeping genius. It walks backward through the network, from output to input, computing each weight's share of the blame efficiently. Then gradient descent nudges each weight in the direction that helps. Repeat thousands of times, and the network's guesses become accurate. When people say a model is trained, they mean this loop ran many times until errors became small.

Memory trick: forward pass makes a guess; loss measures the miss; backprop assigns blame; gradient descent takes a step. Four moves, endlessly repeated. That is training.

PART 4 - TRAINING A NETWORK: A FULL WALKTHROUGH WITH NUMBERS

Let us make training concrete with a tiny example: teaching a network to predict whether a student passes based on hours studied.

Data. We have five students. One studied 1 hour and failed, 2 hours and failed, 3 hours and passed marginally, 4 hours and passed, 5 hours and passed. Encode fail as zero and pass as one.

The network is tiny: one input, hours; one hidden neuron; one output neuron. The output uses sigmoid to give a probability of passing.

Step one: initialize weights randomly, say weight one is 0.2 and weight two is 0.5, with small biases. Step two: forward pass for the first student, 1 hour. Hidden value is 1 times 0.2, through ReLU, giving 0.2. Output is 0.2 times 0.5 through sigmoid, giving about 0.55. The network says fifty-five percent chance of passing. The truth is fail, zero. Step three: compute loss. Using squared error, the error is 0.55 minus 0, squared, about 0.30. Step four: backpropagation computes how to change weight one and weight two to reduce this error. Suppose the gradients suggest lowering weight two. Step five: gradient descent with learning rate 0.1 adjusts weights slightly. Step six: move to the next student and repeat. Step seven: after all students, one epoch is complete. Repeat for many epochs.

Watch the loss over epochs. It should fall steadily. If the network eventually gives high probability to students with 4 and 5 hours and low probability to 1 and 2 hours, it has learned the pattern: study time predicts passing. That learning lives entirely in the final weights and biases.

Training vocabulary to lock in. Epoch: one full pass over the dataset. Batch: the subset of examples used for one update; using all at once is batch gradient descent, using small groups is mini-batch, and using one at a time is stochastic. Learning rate: step size, the single most important hyperparameter. Iteration: one weight update.

PART 5 - TRAINING WELL: LEARNING RATES, OVERFITTING, AND PRACTICAL WISDOM

Knowing the mechanics is half; training well is the craft. Let us collect the practical wisdom.

Learning rate selection. Too large: loss jumps around or explodes; the learner overshoots valleys. Too small: training crawls for hours or days. Start with common defaults, like 0.01 or 0.001, watch the loss curve, and adjust. Modern optimizers like Adam adapt per-weight step sizes automatically and are the practical default.

Batch size. Larger batches give smoother updates but need more memory. Smaller batches add noise that can help escape poor solutions. Typical values: 16, 32, 64. On limited hardware, small batches are friends.

Overfitting, your old enemy from machine learning, lives here too. Deep networks have millions of weights and can memorize training data. Defenses: more data; data augmentation, for images: flipping, rotating, cropping, changing brightness; dropout, randomly silencing a fraction of neurons during training so the network cannot rely on any one pathway; regularization, gently discouraging huge weights; and early stopping, halting when validation performance stops improving.

Underfitting appears when the network is too small or trained too briefly. The remedy: more capacity, longer training, better features.

Initialization matters. Starting weights too large or too small breaks early learning. Modern defaults like Xavier or He initialization set sensible scales; use them.

Shuffling data each epoch prevents the network from memorizing order. Normalizing inputs, scaling numbers to similar ranges like zero to one, speeds training dramatically. These small habits separate painful experiments from smooth ones.

Diagnosing from loss curves is essential. Training loss falling while validation loss rises means overfitting. Both flat and high means underfitting or a bug. Wild oscillation means learning rate too high. Plot curves always; your eyes catch what numbers hide.

Practical wisdom: train a small version first. Prove the pipeline on a tiny network with tiny data before scaling up. Debugging a huge model is archaeology; debugging a tiny model is conversation.

PART 6 - ARCHITECTURES: FEEDFORWARD, CONVOLUTIONAL, RECURRENT

Let us tour the main families of neural network architectures.

Feedforward networks, also called multilayer perceptrons. Information flows one way: input to output, no loops. They handle tabular data well: predicting prices, scoring risks, classifying customers. They are the foundation; every architecture builds on their ideas.

Convolutional neural networks, CNNs. The stars of computer vision. Instead of connecting everything to everything, convolutional layers slide small filters over images, detecting features regardless of position. A cat is a cat in the corner or the center. Pooling layers shrink size and keep strong signals. CNNs power face recognition, medical imaging, self-driving perception, and quality inspection. You saw their intuition in computer vision; here you understand their training.

Recurrent neural networks, RNNs. These have loops: their output feeds back into themselves, giving memory of what came before. They suit sequences: text, speech, time series. The problem: early RNNs forgot long passages. The fix: LSTM, long short-term memory, and GRU cells with gates that control what to remember and what to forget. For years, LSTMs led translation and speech recognition.

Attention and transformers. The modern revolution. Instead of processing strictly in order, attention lets every element look at every other element and weigh relevance. Transformers power modern language models and increasingly vision too. The core idea is simple to state: let the network decide what to focus on.

Generative adversarial networks, GANs. Two networks compete: a generator creates fakes, a discriminator detects fakes. Through competition, generators learn to create astonishing images and audio. Deepfakes and synthetic art come from this family.

Autoencoders and variational autoencoders. Networks that learn to compress data into a compact code and reconstruct it. They power denoising, anomaly detection, and generative models.

Graph neural networks. They operate on graphs of connected nodes: molecules, social networks, robot configurations. Emerging and powerful for your robotics interests.

Choose architectures by data shape. Tabular data: start with feedforward or tree models. Images: CNNs. Sequences: transformers or RNNs. Graphs: GNNs. Match the architecture to the structure of the problem.

PART 7 - NEURAL NETWORKS IN ROBOTICS AND EMBODIED AI

Let me connect this course to your robotics heart.

Robot perception runs on neural networks. Vision networks identify objects to grasp; depth networks estimate distances; segmentation networks separate drivable road from obstacles. The robot's understanding of the world increasingly passes through trained networks.

Learning robot control. Instead of hand-coding every motion, robots learn policies: networks mapping sensor readings to motor commands. Reinforcement learning trains these by trial and reward in simulation and reality. Neural network controllers have learned to walk, fly, and manipulate with superhuman agility.

Imitation learning, learning from demonstration. A human demonstrates a task; a network learns to copy the mapping from observation to action. This is how many industrial skills are transferred to cobots.

Sim-to-real transfer. Training in simulation is cheap and safe; neural networks, with techniques like domain randomization, can transfer skills to real robots despite differences between virtual and physical worlds.

Human-robot interaction benefits from networks too: speech understanding for voice commands, emotion recognition from faces and voices for social robots, and intent prediction so robots can anticipate human actions and help proactively.

Edge deployment. Trained networks run on robot onboard computers and embedded chips. Model compression, quantization, and efficient architectures make neural perception real-time and battery-friendly.

A practical project idea: train a small network to recognize hand gestures with a webcam and map them to robot commands, like open gripper and close gripper. It combines computer vision, neural networks, and robotics in one memorable build.

Caution from the field: networks fail in unexpected ways. A grasping network trained on clean lab images may fail on shiny parts under factory lights. Always test on the real distribution, quantify failure rates, and keep classical fallbacks for safety-critical actions.

PART 8 - PRACTICAL LAB: YOUR FIRST NEURAL NETWORK

Let me walk you through building your first neural network in practice: recognizing handwritten digits with the classic MNIST dataset.

Step one, set up. Use Python with an environment like Google Colab, free with GPU support. Import TensorFlow or PyTorch, and load MNIST: 70,000 small images of digits zero to nine, each 28 by 28 pixels.

Step two, prepare data. Scale pixel values from 0 to 255 down to the range zero to one by dividing by 255. Split into training and test sets. Shape each image into a flat vector of 784 numbers for a simple network.

Step three, build the model. A simple stack: input of 784 values, one hidden layer of 128 neurons with ReLU activation, and an output layer of 10 neurons with softmax activation, which turns scores into probabilities across the ten digits.

Step four, compile and train. Use the Adam optimizer, categorical cross-entropy loss, and accuracy as the metric. Train for ten epochs with batches of 32. Watch the accuracy climb: ninety-five percent is typical for this simple setup.

Step five, evaluate. Run on the test set. Look at the confusion matrix: which digits are confused with which? Ones and sevens, fours and nines often mix. This analysis teaches you where the model is weak.

Step six, improve. Add a second hidden layer. Add dropout. Train longer with early stopping. Try a small convolutional network and watch accuracy cross ninety-nine percent. Compare and write down what helped.

Step seven, misuse test. Draw your own digits in a paint program, resize to 28 by 28, and run predictions. See where it fails on your handwriting. This personal test teaches humility and the value of diverse training data.

Document your lab like a report: goal, data, model, results, lessons. This single project covers the entire neural network workflow and becomes a portfolio piece you can show in interviews.

PART 9 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: Why do we need nonlinear activation functions? Model answer: Without them, multiple layers collapse into a single linear transformation. Nonlinearity lets networks model curves, boundaries, and complex patterns in images, language, and control.

Question two: Explain backpropagation in plain words. Model answer: It is the process of walking backward through the network after a wrong guess, computing how much each weight contributed to the error, so gradient descent can adjust every weight in the helpful direction.

Question three: Your training accuracy is 99 percent but validation accuracy is 75 percent. What is happening and what are two fixes? Model answer: The network is overfitting, memorizing training data. Fixes include data augmentation, dropout, more training data, regularization, or early stopping.

Question four: What does the learning rate control and what happens if it is too large? Model answer: It controls the step size of each weight update. If too large, updates overshoot good values, loss oscillates or diverges, and training fails to settle.

Question five: Why are CNNs good for images? Model answer: Convolutional layers detect local features with shared filters, making them efficient and translation aware: a feature is recognized wherever it appears. Pooling adds robustness and reduces size.

Question six: Name two ways neural networks are used in robotics. Model answer: Perception, such as identifying objects and estimating depth, and learned control policies that map sensor readings to motor commands via reinforcement or imitation learning.

PART 10 - GLOSSARY AND CLOSING RECAP

Artificial neuron: a unit computing a weighted sum with bias and an activation.

Weight: the importance factor of an input, learned from data.

Bias: a threshold shift added to the weighted sum.

Activation function: the function deciding neuron output, like ReLU or sigmoid.

Layer: a row of neurons; input, hidden, and output layers.

Feedforward network: a network where information flows one way.

Deep learning: training networks with many hidden layers.

Loss function: measuring how wrong a prediction is.

Backpropagation: computing each weight's blame for the error efficiently.

Gradient descent: adjusting weights to reduce error step by step.

Learning rate: the size of each training step.

Epoch: one full pass over the training data.

Batch: the subset of examples used for one weight update.

Overfitting: memorizing training data at the cost of generalization.

Dropout: randomly silencing neurons during training to fight overfitting.

Data augmentation: expanding training data with transformations.

Softmax: turning output scores into probabilities across classes.

CNN: convolutional neural network, ideal for images.

RNN: recurrent neural network with memory for sequences.

LSTM: a recurrent cell with gates controlling memory.

Transformer: an architecture built on attention mechanisms.

Adam: a popular adaptive optimization algorithm.

Inference: using a trained network to make predictions.

Policy: a network mapping observations to actions in robotics.

PART 11 - DEEPER PRACTICAL: SOFTMAX, CROSS-ENTROPY, AND CAREERS

Let me deepen your practical understanding with details every neural network engineer uses daily.

Softmax in action. The output layer of a classifier usually ends with softmax. Given raw scores called logits, say 2.0 for cat, 1.0 for dog, 0.1 for bird, softmax converts them into probabilities that sum to one: about 0.66 for cat, 0.24 for dog, 0.10 for bird. It exponentiates each score and normalizes. Read the outputs as confidence; a peaky distribution means certainty, a flat one means confusion. When a model gives 0.51 to one class and 0.49 to another, it is genuinely uncertain, and downstream systems should treat it as such, especially in robots where actions carry risk.

Cross-entropy loss. For classification, cross-entropy measures how far predicted probabilities are from the truth. If the truth is cat and the model gives cat a probability of 0.9, loss is small. If the model gives cat only 0.1, loss is large, and gradients are correspondingly steep, teaching rapidly from confident mistakes. This is why cross-entropy is the default loss for classification tasks.

One training epoch, step by step. Take a mini-batch of 32 images. Run the forward pass: each image flows to a probability vector. Compute loss: average cross-entropy over the 32 examples. Backpropagate: compute gradients for every weight. Update: subtract learning rate times gradient from each weight. Shuffle the dataset and continue. Over many epochs, the loss descends and accuracy climbs. Watching this loop run in a training script, with the loss printing every few seconds, is one of the most satisfying sights in engineering.

Hardware realities. Neural networks train faster on GPUs, graphics processing units, because thousands of small cores do matrix math in parallel. Cloud platforms rent GPU time affordably. On small projects, a laptop CPU suffices. Choose hardware to match the model: do not rent a supercomputer for a logistic regression, and do not expect a phone to train a giant transformer.

Deploying networks. After training, models are exported and served: as APIs on servers, as apps on phones, as firmware on robots. Inference speed matters; techniques like quantization shrink models with little accuracy loss so they run on cheap hardware.

Careers in deep learning. Roles include machine learning engineer, deep learning researcher, computer vision engineer, NLP engineer, robotics learning engineer, and MLOps engineer who maintains training and deployment pipelines. Industries from fintech to agriculture to entertainment hire for these skills. Start now with small projects: digit recognition, gesture detection, plant disease classification. Document each; your portfolio will speak for you.

Study habits for this course. Implement one neuron by hand in a spreadsheet first; formulas become friends when you see them move numbers. Then implement a tiny network in NumPy before using big frameworks; frameworks hide mechanics that you should experience once. Read one famous paper in plain summary form each month: AlexNet, ResNet, Attention Is All You Need. Explain each to a classmate. That is how mastery grows.

PART 12 - WORKED EXAMPLE: GRADIENT DESCENT ON ONE NEURON WITH FULL NUMBERS

Let me walk through gradient descent on a single neuron with complete numbers, so the mechanics become tangible rather than magical.

Setup. One input x, one weight w, one bias b. The neuron computes y equals sigmoid of z, where z equals w times x plus b. The true label is t. We use squared error loss: L equals y minus t, all squared. Take x equals 2, true label t equals 1, and initial guesses w equals 0.4 and b equals negative 0.2.

Forward pass. z equals 0.4 times 2 plus negative 0.2, giving 0.6. Sigmoid of 0.6 is approximately 0.646. Loss is 0.646 minus 1, squared, which is about 0.125. The neuron is under-confident: it should say 1 but says 0.646.

Backward pass. We need how loss changes with respect to weight and bias. Chain rule gives the recipe. The derivative of squared loss with respect to y is 2 times y minus t, which is 2 times negative 0.354, about negative 0.708. The derivative of sigmoid at z is y times 1 minus y, which is 0.646 times 0.354, about 0.229. The derivative of z with respect to w is x, which is 2; with respect to b is 1.

Multiply along the chain. Gradient for w: negative 0.708 times 0.229 times 2, about negative 0.324. Gradient for b: negative 0.708 times 0.229 times 1, about negative 0.162.

Update step. With learning rate 0.5, new w equals 0.4 minus 0.5 times negative 0.324, which is 0.562. New b equals negative 0.2 minus 0.5 times negative 0.162, which is negative 0.119. Notice both weight and bias moved in the direction that raises y toward 1. The error will shrink.

Repeat. Second forward pass with new values: z equals 0.562 times 2 minus 0.119, about 1.005; sigmoid gives about 0.732; loss drops to about 0.072. One step cut the error almost in half. Continue for dozens of steps and y approaches 1 ever more closely.

This tiny computation contains the soul of every neural network training run ever executed: forward pass, loss, chain rule, update, repeat. The only differences at scale are millions of weights, matrix algebra for speed, and smarter optimizers like Adam. When you later watch a training script print falling loss, remember this hand calculation: it is the same dance.

PART 14 - COMMON MISTAKES, INTERVIEW QUESTIONS, AND COURSE CLOSURE

Let me finish with the mistakes that trip students in neural networks, plus interview preparation.

Mistake one: forgetting to normalize inputs. Raw pixel values from 0 to 255 or house prices in millions create gradient scales that slow or break training. Scale inputs to similar ranges, like zero to one or standardized values, and training flows.

Mistake two: using softmax with mean squared error for classification. The natural pairing is softmax with cross-entropy; mixing them weakens gradients and confuses learning. Know the standard pairings: sigmoid with binary cross-entropy, softmax with categorical cross-entropy.

Mistake three: ignoring the validation set and tuning on the test set. The test set is sacred: touch it once at the end. Tune on validation; report on test; keep honesty intact.

Mistake four: chasing accuracy blindly. In medical or safety tasks, a false negative can cost a life while a false positive costs only time. Choose metrics that match human consequences: recall for screening, precision for alerts.

Mistake five: training huge models on tiny data. A giant network memorizes small datasets instantly. Start small, prove the signal exists, then grow. Data first, scale second.

Mistake six: ignoring random seeds. Neural network training involves randomness in initialization and shuffling. Fix seeds for reproducibility so your results can be trusted and repeated.

Interview questions to expect. Explain backpropagation to a non-technical manager: use the blame-allocation story. Why does ReLU help: it keeps gradients alive for positive values and computes fast. What is transfer learning: reusing a pretrained network's features for a new task with less data. How do you handle imbalanced classes: reweighting, resampling, and choosing proper metrics. What is overfitting and how do you detect it: the train-validation gap, fixed with augmentation, dropout, and early stopping. Practice these answers aloud; interviews reward clarity.

PART 15 - FINAL GLOSSARY ADDITIONS AND FAREWELL

A few more terms to complete your neural network vocabulary.

Logit: the raw score a network outputs before softmax converts it to probabilities.

Hyperparameter: a setting chosen before training, like learning rate, batch size, or layer size, as opposed to weights which training learns.

Validation set: data used to tune hyperparameters and monitor training, kept separate from training and final test data.

Early stopping: halting training when validation performance stops improving, to prevent overfitting.

Transfer learning: reusing a network pretrained on a big dataset for a new, smaller task.

Quantization: shrinking a trained model by representing weights with fewer bits, enabling deployment on small devices.

Model capacity: the ability of a network to fit complex patterns, growing with more neurons and layers.

With these terms, your toolkit is complete for the road ahead. Learn steadily, code weekly, question results, and build with purpose. The networks you train in the years to come may help a doctor, a farmer, or a robot. Train them with care.
