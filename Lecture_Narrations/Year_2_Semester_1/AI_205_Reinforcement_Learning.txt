PART 1 - WELCOME: LEARNING FROM REWARD, LIKE LIFE ITSELF

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 205, Reinforcement Learning. Of all the branches of machine learning, reinforcement learning is the closest to how living creatures learn. A child touches a hot pot, feels pain, and learns to avoid it. A student studies hard, earns good grades, and learns that effort pays. A football team tries tactics, wins or loses, and adjusts. Learning from the consequences of actions, that is reinforcement learning.

In technical words, reinforcement learning is a type of machine learning where an agent learns to make decisions by interacting with an environment and receiving rewards or penalties. There is no teacher giving correct answers as in supervised learning. There is only experience: try, observe results, adjust strategy, and repeat.

This approach has achieved astonishing feats. Reinforcement learning agents have beaten world champions at the board game Go, mastered video games directly from pixels, learned to walk and run in simulation, optimized data center cooling, improved recommendation systems, and trained robots to grasp and manipulate objects. It is the learning method behind many of the most impressive AI achievements of our era.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand the agent-environment loop, the vocabulary of states, actions, policies, and rewards, the great dilemma of exploration versus exploitation, and the core algorithms from multi-armed bandits to Q-learning and deep reinforcement learning. You will also see practical applications in robotics, games, and industry, and design your first learning agent.

Reinforcement learning combines ideas from machine learning, control theory, and psychology. Your background in all three will serve you well. Let us begin.

PART 2 - THE REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FRAMEWORK: AGENTS AND ENVIRONMENTS

Let us build the framework with a clear mental picture.

The agent is the learner and decision maker: the robot, the game player, the trading program. The environment is everything the agent interacts with: the game board, the physical world, the market. At each step, the agent observes the state of the environment, chooses an action, and receives two things back: a new state and a reward signal.

The reward is a number: positive for good outcomes, negative for bad. The agent's goal is not to maximize the immediate reward but the total cumulative reward over time. This distinction is profound. In chess, sacrificing a piece now can win the game later. In farming irrigation, saving water now can save the crop in a drought. Farsightedness is the essence.

The policy is the agent's strategy: a mapping from states to actions. A good policy tells the agent what to do in every situation. The value function estimates how good a state is: the total reward we expect from that state onward under our policy. Value functions are the bridge between immediate rewards and long-term thinking.

The model of the environment predicts what happens next: which state follows an action and what reward comes. Some methods learn models; others skip models and learn from trial alone.

Markov decision processes, MDPs, give the mathematical foundation: states, actions, transition probabilities, and rewards. The Markov property means the future depends only on the present state, not the full history. Chess is Markovian given the board; a partially observed card game is not. Many real problems are partially observable, and extensions handle them.

Real-life picture: a delivery robot learning to navigate a building. States: locations and sensor readings. Actions: move forward, turn, stop. Rewards: progress toward destination positive, collisions negative, time slightly negative. The policy it learns is smart navigation by trial and reward. Everything else in this course grows from this simple loop.

PART 3 - THE CENTRAL DILEMMA: EXPLORATION VERSUS EXPLOITATION

Now the tension that defines reinforcement learning: exploration versus exploitation.

Exploitation means using current knowledge to grab the best known reward. Exploration means trying new actions to discover possibly better rewards. Neither pure strategy works. Always exploit, and you might stick with a mediocre restaurant forever. Always explore, and you waste your life never enjoying the best.

The multi-armed bandit problem makes this vivid. Imagine a casino with several slot machines, bandits, each paying out differently but unknown. You have limited coins. Which machines do you pull, and when do you switch? This simplified problem isolates the dilemma without the complexity of states.

Strategies. Epsilon-greedy: most of the time choose the best known action, but with small probability epsilon, try a random one. Simple and effective. Upper confidence bound: favor actions whose rewards are uncertain, optimism in the face of uncertainty. Thompson sampling: maintain beliefs about each action's reward and sample from them. Each strategy balances the dilemma differently.

Practical intuition from daily life. Choosing where to eat in a new city: try several places early, then settle on favorites while occasionally trying something new. Job hunting: explore opportunities early, then focus on promising leads. The mathematics of bandits formalizes wisdom people practice instinctively.

For robots, exploration has real costs. A robot exploring a dangerous environment can damage itself. Safe exploration adds constraints: never explore into states with catastrophic risk. This subfield, safe reinforcement learning, is critical for physical systems.

Real-life case: an e-commerce recommender testing new products. Mostly show proven favorites, but sometimes surface new items to learn their appeal. The exploration budget is a business decision as much as an algorithmic one. Balance is everywhere.

PART 4 - Q-LEARNING: LEARNING VALUES FROM EXPERIENCE

Let us learn the classic algorithm: Q-learning, the heart of model-free reinforcement learning.

The idea. Maintain a table, the Q-table, with one number for every state-action pair. Q of state s and action a estimates the total future reward of taking action a in state s and then behaving well. The agent uses the Q-table greedily: in each state, take the action with the highest Q value.

Learning rule, in words. After taking action a in state s, receiving reward r, and arriving at state s', update Q of s and a toward the target: r plus gamma times the best Q value in state s'. Gamma is the discount factor, valuing future reward less than immediate reward; it encodes patience. The learning rate decides how fast new experience overwrites old estimates.

Let us trace a gridworld example. A small grid: the agent starts at the top-left, the goal at the bottom-right with reward plus 10, a pit with reward minus 10, and each step costing minus 0.1 to encourage speed. Initially all Q values are zero: the agent wanders randomly. After falling into the pit once, Q values near the pit drop. After reaching the goal, Q values along the path rise. Over hundreds of episodes, the Q-table fills with a map of wisdom: near the goal, high values; near the pit, low ones. The greedy policy following the Q-table now walks the safe, fast path. That is learning from reward without any teacher.

Convergence. Under reasonable conditions, Q-learning converges to optimal Q values. But tabular Q-learning struggles with large spaces. A robot with camera inputs has states too numerous for any table: every image is a state. The remedy came from deep learning.

Deep Q-networks, DQNs, replace the table with a neural network that takes the state and outputs Q values for all actions. Experience replay stores past transitions and samples them for training, breaking dangerous correlations. Target networks stabilize learning. The DQN that learned Atari games from pixels, scoring above humans on many titles, ignited the modern deep reinforcement learning era in 2013 and 2014.

Practical advice: implement tabular Q-learning on a small gridworld first. Watching the Q-table fill with numbers that encode strategy is one of the great joys of this course. Do it this month.

PART 5 - POLICY METHODS AND ACTOR-CRITIC: LEARNING TO ACT DIRECTLY

Q-learning learns values and derives actions. Policy gradient methods learn the policy directly: a neural network mapping states to actions, adjusted to increase the reward.

The idea. Sample actions from the policy, play episodes, and nudge the policy toward actions that brought higher rewards. The REINFORCE algorithm is the classic. High-reward episodes make their chosen actions more likely; low-reward episodes make theirs less likely. Simple and general.

Strengths. Policy methods handle continuous actions naturally: a robot arm's torques are continuous, not a small menu. They can learn stochastic policies, useful when unpredictability helps, in games of bluff and in crowded navigation.

Weaknesses. High variance in learning: performance swings until enough episodes average out the noise.

Actor-critic methods combine both worlds. The actor is the policy network choosing actions. The critic is the value network judging states. The critic's judgment reduces variance, stabilizing learning. Modern algorithms like A2C, A3C, PPO, and SAC build on this pattern and dominate robotics applications.

Trust region ideas keep updates small and safe: policies change gradually, avoiding catastrophic collapse mid-training. PPO, proximal policy optimization, is famous for its simplicity and robustness; it is the default workhorse of many labs.

Real-life robotics case: teaching a quadruped robot to walk. The policy maps joint sensors to motor torques. Rewards encourage forward motion and penalize falling and energy use. Training in simulation with millions of steps, using PPO, produces walking gaits transferred to real hardware. Domain randomization during training, varying friction and weight, prepares the policy for reality.

Practical tip: when starting a reinforcement learning project, try PPO first for continuous control and DQN for discrete simple tasks. The toolset covers most problems, and understanding both lines is your foundation.

PART 6 - DEEP REINFORCEMENT LEARNING AND APPLICATIONS

Let us tour the achievements and applications of deep reinforcement learning.

Games. AlphaGo and AlphaZero mastered Go, chess, and shogi through self-play reinforcement learning combined with search. StarCraft and Dota agents handled real-time strategy with imperfect information. Game mastery proved the algorithms can discover superhuman strategies.

Robotics. Learning dexterous manipulation: opening doors, pouring water, assembling parts. Locomotion: walking, running, jumping in simulation and on real machines. Sim-to-real transfer makes practice cheap and safe.

Recommendation and personalization. Systems that learn what content or products to show users, balancing engagement with satisfaction and fairness. The exploration-exploitation dilemma lives here directly.

Operations research. Data center cooling optimization saved massive energy at Google. Job scheduling, logistics routing, and inventory management improve with learned policies.

Healthcare. Optimizing treatment strategies, like dosing schedules, carefully and with expert oversight. The stakes demand interpretability and safety constraints.

Autonomous driving. Learning driving policies and decision making in complex interactions, complementing classical stacks.

Resource management in developing contexts. Optimizing micro-grid power dispatch, water allocation, and dynamic pricing for shared resources. For African engineers, reinforcement learning offers tools for scarce-resource problems of great local importance.

Limits to respect. Reinforcement learning is data hungry and sensitive to reward design. Poorly designed rewards create perverse behavior: a cleaning robot rewarded for staying clean learns to hide in corners. Reward shaping and oversight are engineering arts. Safety in physical domains requires constraints and human review.

PART 7 - REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FOR ROBOTICS: LEARNING TO MOVE AND MANIPULATE

Let me connect reinforcement learning to your robotics heart specifically.

Robot locomotion. Quadrupeds and humanoids learn gaits in simulation. Rewards balance speed, stability, and energy. Trained policies adapt to pushes, slopes, and rough terrain with grace that hand-coded controllers struggle to match.

Manipulation. Grasping diverse objects, using tools, and reorienting parts. The action space is continuous torques or end-effector velocities; rewards measure task success and smoothness. Tactile sensing helps policies adjust grip force.

Learning from demonstration plus reinforcement. Pure reinforcement learning explores blindly; imitation learning seeds policies from human demonstrations; reinforcement learning then refines them. This combination trains faster and safer.

Inverse reinforcement learning flips the problem: observe expert behavior and infer the reward function that explains it. Useful when we can show what we want but cannot write the reward. Apprenticeship learning builds on this.

Model-based reinforcement learning learns the environment's dynamics and plans within the learned model. Data efficient and suited to robots where real trials are costly.

Sim-to-real transfer techniques. Domain randomization varies simulation properties so policies generalize. System identification estimates real parameters for accurate simulation. Progressive adaptation moves policies gradually toward reality. These methods are today's practical bridge.

Safety in robot reinforcement learning. Constrained optimization keeps actions within torque, speed, and workspace limits. Human-in-the-loop training allows corrections. And conservative deployment: test policies in caged areas before sharing space with people.

Practical project: train a simulated cart-pole to balance using tabular Q-learning or a small DQN. The pole balancing on a moving cart is the hello world of reinforcement learning. Watching it learn from wobbly failure to steady balance makes the theory alive.

PART 8 - PRACTICAL LAB: YOUR FIRST LEARNING AGENT

Let me guide a full hands-on lab: a Q-learning agent solving a simple grid maze.

Setup: Python with NumPy; a grid of five by five cells; start top-left; goal bottom-right plus 10; one pit minus 10; step cost minus 0.1; four actions up, down, left, right; walls on some edges.

Step one: implement the environment as a small class with methods reset and step returning state, reward, and done. Keep it simple and transparent.

Step two: initialize the Q-table as a NumPy array of zeros shaped states by actions.

Step three: implement epsilon-greedy action selection with epsilon starting at 0.2 and decaying slowly.

Step four: run training episodes, each starting at the start cell, updating Q values with the learning rule you learned. Use gamma 0.9 and learning rate 0.1.

Step five: plot total reward per episode. Watch the curve rise as the agent learns. This curve is the signature of learning.

Step six: test the greedy policy without exploration. Print the learned path. It should be safe and near-optimal.

Step seven: experiment. Change epsilon and observe exploration effects. Add a second pit and watch learning slow. Increase gamma and see more farsighted policies. Each experiment teaches a knob of the method.

Step eight: extend toward robotics. Replace the grid with a simple simulation of a robot moving toward a goal with obstacles; discretize positions and reuse the same Q-learning. The algorithm is unchanged; the states now represent physical space.

Document your lab: code, learning curve, experiments, and lessons. This single lab is your gateway to the whole field.

PART 9 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: How does reinforcement learning differ from supervised learning? Model answer: Supervised learning learns from labeled examples with correct answers. Reinforcement learning learns from interaction with rewards and penalties, with no correct action given, optimizing cumulative reward over time.

Question two: What is the exploration-exploitation dilemma? Model answer: The trade-off between using known good actions and trying new ones to discover better rewards. Pure exploitation misses better options; pure exploration wastes reward; strategies like epsilon-greedy balance both.

Question three: Explain Q-learning in plain words. Model answer: It maintains estimates of future reward for each state-action pair and updates them from experience, so the agent can act greedily according to values learned by trial and error.

Question four: Why do deep reinforcement learning methods use neural networks? Model answer: To handle huge or continuous state spaces where tables fail, like images from cameras. Networks generalize across similar states.

Question five: Name one application of reinforcement learning in robotics and describe the reward. Model answer: Teaching a quadruped to walk; reward encourages forward distance and penalizes falling and energy use, producing efficient stable gaits.

Question six: What is a reward function and why must it be designed carefully? Model answer: It encodes the goal as numbers. Poor rewards create unintended behaviors; careful design, shaping, and oversight align agent behavior with true objectives.

PART 10 - GLOSSARY AND CLOSING RECAP

Reinforcement learning: learning decision making from interaction with rewards.

Agent: the learner and decision maker.

Environment: everything the agent interacts with.

State: the situation the agent observes.

Action: a choice available to the agent.

Reward: the feedback signal, positive or negative.

Policy: the strategy mapping states to actions.

Value function: expected cumulative reward from a state.

Q value: expected reward for a state-action pair.

Discount factor gamma: weighting future rewards against present ones.

Exploration: trying new actions to discover rewards.

Exploitation: using current knowledge for best known reward.

Multi-armed bandit: the simplified exploration-exploitation problem.

Q-learning: model-free learning of Q values from experience.

Experience replay: reusing stored transitions for stable training.

Deep Q-network: neural network approximating Q values.

Policy gradient: adjusting policy parameters toward higher reward.

Actor-critic: combining policy and value networks for stable learning.

PPO: proximal policy optimization, a robust policy gradient method.

Markov decision process: the mathematical framework of states, actions, transitions, rewards.

Sim-to-real: transferring policies trained in simulation to physical robots.

Inverse reinforcement learning: inferring rewards from observed expert behavior.

Reward shaping: adding helpful reward signals to speed learning.

Safe reinforcement learning: learning under constraints to avoid harm.

PART 11 - MORE SCENARIOS AND DEEPER PRACTICAL WISDOM

Scenario one: reinforcement learning for a farming irrigation scheduler. The system decides daily water amounts across zones. States: soil moisture, weather forecast, crop stage. Actions: water amounts per zone. Rewards: crop health and yield at harvest minus water cost and energy cost. Training begins in simulation on historical weather, then adapts cautiously on one experimental plot before wider use. The engineer's caution: a wrong policy can destroy a season's harvest, so conservative exploration and agronomist oversight are mandatory.

Scenario two: reinforcement learning for traffic signal timing. Agents at intersections learn to reduce waiting times. States: vehicle queues detected by cameras. Actions: phase timings. Rewards: negative total waiting time. Multi-agent reinforcement learning coordinates neighboring junctions. Cities testing such systems report double-digit improvements in flow, but fairness matters: pedestrian crossings and emergency vehicles must be protected explicitly in the reward design.

Scenario three: reinforcement learning for battery management in a solar micro-grid. The agent decides when to store energy, when to sell, and when to use the generator. Rewards balance cost savings and battery longevity. Trained on a year of weather and price data, the policy outperforms simple rules. Communities gain cheaper, steadier power.

Scenario four: reinforcement learning tutoring. An educational system chooses which exercise to present next. Rewards come from long-term learning gains, not just immediate correctness. The design pitfall: rewarding only easy wins makes the tutor lazy; rewards must value growth over comfort.

Scenario five: warehouse robot task allocation. Robots learn to pick tasks in busy warehouses where congestion changes costs. Rewards penalize collisions and delays. Multi-agent coordination emerges from local learning. Watch for selfish equilibria where robots crowd the same aisle; shaping terms encourage spreading out.

Scenario six: robotic arm learning to sort recyclable waste. Actions are continuous joint torques; rewards encourage correct sorting and penalize crushing. Trained in simulation with millions of attempts, then transferred to hardware with force limits. The sorting rate improves over weeks as the policy fine-tunes on real objects.

Deeper practical wisdom. First, reward design is your most important task. Write the reward on paper, then actively imagine how a clever agent could exploit it without truly doing the job. That imagination is called reward hacking prevention. Second, start with simulation whenever the agent has a body; real-world trial and error on hardware is slow and can be destructive. Third, log everything: rewards, episode lengths, and videos of behavior; behavior videos reveal problems that scalar curves hide. Fourth, compare against simple baselines: a hand-coded rule or random policy; if your learned agent cannot beat them, something is wrong. Fifth, be patient: reinforcement learning is notoriously sensitive to seeds and hyperparameters; run several seeds and report honest variance.

Career skills for reinforcement learning engineers: strong probability and linear algebra, fluency in Python and deep learning frameworks, experience with simulators like OpenAI Gym, MuJoCo, Isaac Gym, and CARLA, and the engineering discipline to evaluate honestly. Researchers push theory; engineers make it work on real machines; both roles are needed and rewarded.

PART 13 - FINAL GLOSSARY ADDITIONS AND STUDY SCHEDULE

Let me complete your reinforcement learning vocabulary and leave you with a study schedule.

More terms. Episode: one complete run of the agent from start to finish. Horizon: how far into the future rewards are considered. Baseline: a reference policy or value used to reduce variance and to compare against. Off-policy learning: learning about one policy while following another, as Q-learning does. On-policy learning: learning only from the current policy's own experience. Model-free: learning without modeling environment dynamics. Model-based: learning or using a model of transitions and rewards. Curriculum learning: arranging training tasks from easy to hard to accelerate learning. Reward hacking: the agent exploiting flaws in the reward instead of truly solving the task. Generalization: performing well on situations not seen in training.
