PART 1 - WELCOME: ROBOTS THAT ROAM THE WORLD

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 207, Mobile Robotics. So far in your program, you have studied robots that mostly stay in place: robot arms in factories, robotic hands, and fixed installations. Today we free the robots. Mobile robotics is the science of machines that move through the world: wheeled rovers, legged walkers, flying drones, rolling delivery robots, and swimming explorers.

Movement changes everything. A robot that moves must answer three eternal questions: where am I, where am I going, and how do I get there safely? These three questions map to localization, mapping, and navigation, the holy trinity of mobile robotics. Along the way, the robot must manage power, handle rough ground, avoid obstacles, and often work among people.

Why does mobile robotics matter for your degree? Because most of the world's useful work happens far from a fixed position. Deliveries must travel. Farms must be surveyed. Warehouses span hectares. Disaster zones need exploration. Homes need service. The robots that will change daily life in Nigeria and around the world are mobile robots: delivery bots on campus, agricultural rovers on farms, inspection robots in pipelines, and logistics fleets in warehouses.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand how mobile robots move, how they know where they are, how they map their surroundings, how they choose and follow paths, and how they avoid obstacles and people. We will connect to your earlier courses in perception, control, and embedded systems, and you will design your own mobile robot concept.

PART 2 - WAYS OF MOVING: WHEELS, LEGS, TRACKS, AND WINGS

Let us study locomotion: how mobile robots move.

Wheeled robots are the most common. The differential drive uses two wheels on a common axis, spinning at different speeds: equal speeds go straight, differing speeds turn. It is simple, efficient, and the brain of most indoor robots, from robot vacuums to warehouse movers. The Ackermann steering mimics a car: front wheels steer, rear wheels drive; efficient at speed but with a larger turning radius. Omnidirectional wheels, like mecanum wheels, allow sideways sliding without turning; invaluable in tight spaces like crowded warehouses. Skid-steer robots turn by spinning tracks or wheels on each side at different speeds, rugged for rough ground.

Legged robots walk, climb stairs, and cross rubble that wheels cannot. Two-legged humanoids navigate human spaces designed for people: stairs, door handles, and corridors. Four-legged robot dogs traverse farms, construction sites, and disaster areas with uncanny balance. Six-legged insect-like robots offer stability and redundancy. The trade-off: legs are mechanically complex and energy hungry, but their terrain freedom is unmatched.

Tracked robots, like small tanks, grip soft and uneven ground: snow, mud, and rubble. Rescue robots often use tracks or hybrid designs that combine wheels and tracks.

Flying robots, drones, ignore terrain entirely. Quadcopters hover, inspect, and deliver. Fixed-wing drones cover vast areas efficiently: farmland surveys, coastline mapping. Flight demands constant control attention and careful energy management.

Swimming robots explore water: reservoirs, pipes, oceans, and inspection of dams. Underwater vehicles face currents, pressure, and communication limits.

Choosing locomotion is engineering judgment. A hospital delivery robot: wheels, smooth and quiet. A farm rover: high clearance wheels or tracks for crop rows. A rescue robot: tracks or legs for rubble. A warehouse: omnidirectional wheels for tight aisles. Match the body to the terrain and the mission.

Real-life case: a delivery robot on your campus. Differential drive for simplicity and efficiency on paved paths, castor wheels for balance, enough ground clearance for small bumps and rain puddles. Chosen because the terrain demands nothing more, and simplicity means reliability.

PART 3 - MOBILE ROBOT KINEMATICS: HOW MOVES BECOME MOTION

Let us open the mathematics of motion, the mobile cousin of robot kinematics.

The configuration of a mobile robot is its pose: position x, y and heading angle theta on the plane. The state is this pose plus velocities. Control inputs are wheel speeds or steering and throttle.

Differential drive kinematics. Two wheels, distance apart called the wheelbase or track width. If both wheels spin at the same speed, the robot goes straight; if the left is faster, the robot curves right; spinning them oppositely turns the robot in place. The forward kinematics computes robot speed and turn rate from wheel speeds: v equals the average of the two wheel speeds; omega, the rotation rate, equals the difference divided by track width. Inverse kinematics computes needed wheel speeds for desired v and omega. These simple equations govern the motion of millions of robots.

Ackermann kinematics relates steering angle and wheel speed to turning radius: the famous bicycle model. Understanding this model helps you design controllers for car-like robots.

Nonholonomic constraints. A car cannot move sideways instantly; it must steer first. This constraint, nonholonomy, shapes planning: paths must respect how the robot can actually move. The car parallel parking problem is a study in nonholonomic motion.

Odometry: estimating position by integrating wheel motions. Count wheel rotations, apply the kinematics, and estimate where the robot has moved. Simple and useful, but errors accumulate: wheels slip, diameters vary, floors tilt. Dead reckoning drifts, and that drift motivates the localization methods we will study.

Practical insight: calibrate your robot. Measure actual wheel diameter and track width; run known distances and turns; compare with odometry. A one percent wheel diameter error becomes meters of drift over a long run. Calibration is the cheapest accuracy you will ever buy.

PART 4 - SENSING FOR MOBILE ROBOTS: SEEING WHILE MOVING

Mobile robots need senses that work in motion, building on AI 202.

Laser scanners and LiDAR scan the plane or the space, building maps and detecting obstacles at range, the workhorse of indoor and outdoor navigation. Cameras provide rich appearance data for visual navigation and place recognition. Ultrasonic and infrared sensors handle close-range detection cheaply: docking, doorway passage, and collision prevention. IMUs track motion at high rates between slower sensor updates. Wheel encoders feed odometry. GPS serves outdoors. And contact and bump sensors provide last-resort collision detection.

Sensor placement matters enormously. Sensors on a moving robot suffer vibration and motion blur; mounting, damping, and exposure control are design decisions. Field of view must cover the direction of travel and the sides for turning. Height matters: a sensor at floor level misses table edges; one high up misses curbs.

Time synchronization is critical again: a LiDAR scan taken while turning must be compensated for motion, or the map smears. Modern systems deskew scans using IMU and odometry.

Real-life design: a warehouse robot carries a safety LiDAR at ankle height for legs and pallets, a navigation LiDAR higher for shelves, front cameras for dock alignment, and a bumper as final fallback. Layered sensing, each layer covering the others' weaknesses.

Power and compute constraints on mobile platforms. Sensors draw power that reduces mission time; processing heavy vision needs capable computers that also consume energy. The design balance: enough sensing for safety and mission, within the power budget. This is where your embedded systems discipline meets robotics.

PART 5 - LOCALIZATION: WHERE AM I?

Now the first great question: where am I?

Odometry-based localization: integrate wheel motion. Fast and cheap, but drift grows without bound. Walking in a straight line, the estimate bends; turning, it lags. Dead reckoning alone is never enough.

Landmark-based localization: detect known features and correct the estimate. Walls and corners for indoor robots using LiDAR. Fiducial markers like AprilTags for structured environments. GPS landmarks outdoors. Each measurement corrects drift; the art is fusing them.

Probabilistic localization. The Kalman filter fuses odometry predictions with landmark measurements optimally under noise. The particle filter handles harder ambiguities: when two hallways look identical, particles hold multiple hypotheses until evidence resolves them. These tools from AI 202 now serve the specific problem of robot pose.

Markov localization and Monte Carlo localization are names you will meet in textbooks; the ideas are exactly this: represent belief about position as probability distributions and update them with Bayes' rule as sensors report.

Visual place recognition: cameras recognize locations from appearance, a bag of words of visual features or learned descriptors. This powers loop closure in SLAM and re-localization after kidnapping: when a robot is picked up and moved, it must recognize where it landed.

Real-life case: robot vacuum cleaners in millions of homes. They localize with a combination of wall-following odometry, cheap LiDAR or optical sensors, and map matching. When lifted and replaced, they re-localize against their stored map. The humble vacuum is a mass-market localization triumph.

Practical exercise: simulate localization. In Python, model a robot moving on a grid with noisy odometry; add noisy GPS-like measurements; implement a simple Kalman filter or particle filter and watch the estimate beat either source alone. This exercise cements the ideas permanently.

PART 6 - MAPPING: DRAWING THE WORLD

The second great question: what does the world look like?

Occupancy grid maps divide space into cells, each marked free, occupied, or unknown. LiDAR beams mark free space along their path and occupied space at returns. Simple, robust, and the everyday currency of navigation. Grid resolution balances detail against memory and compute.

Feature-based maps store landmarks instead of cells: corners, lines, and reflectors. Compact and useful in structured environments, but fragile when features are sparse.

Topological maps represent places and connections: rooms connected by doors, like a subway map. Lightweight and natural for route planning at human scale.

Metric versus topological: metric maps measure distances precisely; topological maps capture connectivity. Modern systems often layer both.

3D maps for complex terrain: point clouds, voxel grids, and meshes for drones and outdoor rovers. Your computer graphics and perception courses supply the tools.

Map maintenance: the world changes. Furniture moves, construction appears, seasons alter outdoor appearance. Maps must be updated or marked stale; a robot navigating with outdated maps fails at the worst moment. Lifelong mapping is active research.

Real-life case: a delivery robot mapping a campus. It drives each path slowly with LiDAR, builds an occupancy grid layered with a topological graph of buildings and paths, and localizes against it during service. Map updates happen monthly or after construction notices.

PART 7 - NAVIGATION AND PATH FOLLOWING: HOW DO I GET THERE?

The third great question: how do I get there?

Global planning chooses a route from origin to goal on the map. Graph search algorithms, A-star, Dijkstra, plan over the grid or graph. Sampling-based planners, RRT and PRM, handle complex spaces. You will study planning deeply in later courses; here we focus on the mobile context.

Local planning and obstacle avoidance handle the dynamic world: people walking, boxes appearing, doors closing. Classic methods: the dynamic window approach evaluates achievable velocities and picks the safest that makes progress. Vector field histograms build cost maps around the robot and steer through low-cost regions. Potential fields treat the goal as attractive and obstacles as repulsive, simple but prone to local minima. Modern methods use optimization and learned policies.

Path following: once a path is chosen, controllers keep the robot on it. Pure pursuit steers by aiming at a lookahead point on the path; Stanley controller corrects cross-track error and heading. Both are elegant and widely used.

Velocity control and comfort: robots among people move smoothly. Sudden stops and sharp turns frighten people and spill loads. Trapezoidal velocity profiles, acceleration limits, and courteous yielding are part of good navigation.

Docking and precision maneuvers: charging docks, shelf pickup, and door passage need precise final positioning, usually with close-range sensors and fine control.

Real-life case: restaurant delivery robots. Global plan to the table area; local planner weaving between chairs; pure pursuit following the aisle; docking under the serving cart; yielding to people with generous margins and slow speeds. Each layer is a concept from this course in action.

PART 8 - WORKING AMONG PEOPLE: SOCIAL MOBILE ROBOTICS

Mobile robots increasingly share space with people, so navigation becomes social.

Predicting human motion: people walk with purpose and patterns. Predictors estimate trajectories so robots can yield early rather than brake late. In crowds, robots should behave legibly and predictably: clear paths, obvious intentions.

Personal space and comfort: keeping respectful distance, passing on the right or left consistent with local custom, slowing near seated people and children. The robot's manners are programmed values.

Communication while moving: lights, sounds, and displays announcing intent: stopping, turning, passing. A robot that glows amber before turning gets smiles; one that silently cuts in front gets complaints.

Crowd navigation: dense markets and lobbies challenge every planner. Conservative behavior, teleoperation assistance for stuck cases, and route design that avoids peak bottlenecks are practical solutions.

Accessibility: robots must not block wheelchair users or visually impaired people with white canes. Designing for the most vulnerable users makes robots better for everyone.

Real-life case: a hospital logistics robot. It announces before corners, waits for elevators with visible patience, yields to stretchers unconditionally, and stops when a patient steps into its path. Nurses trust it because it behaves like a careful colleague.

PART 9 - PRACTICAL LAB: BUILD A SIMPLE MOBILE ROBOT

Let me design your first mobile robot project: a line-following and obstacle-avoiding robot, extended from your AI 102 work.

Base: two-wheel differential drive chassis with a castor, microcontroller, line sensors, one ultrasonic distance sensor, and a motor driver.

Level one: follow a black line with the two reflectance sensors and PID control on the error between them. Tune the PID: start with proportional only, add derivative for damping, add integral sparingly. The robot should track curves smoothly.

Level two: add obstacle detection. When the ultrasonic reports something close on the line, stop and wait or turn to search for the line around the obstacle. This teaches state machines: line following state, obstacle state, search state.

Level three: add odometry. Count encoder ticks; estimate distance and heading; log the path. Compare with the actual course drawn on paper. Measure drift; discuss causes.

Level four: add simple mapping. As the robot runs, plot sensor readings in a 2D chart: the beginnings of occupancy mapping.

Level five: communication. Send telemetry over Bluetooth or serial: speed, error, distance readings. Build a small dashboard on a laptop.

What you learn: differential kinematics, PID path following, state machines, odometry drift, sensing in motion, and telemetry. This one project is mobile robotics in miniature.

PART 10 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: Why does odometry drift and how do we correct it? Model answer: Wheel slip, diameter errors, and slippage accumulate into position error over time. Corrections come from absolute references: landmarks, GPS, LiAR map matching, or vision.

Question two: Compare differential drive and Ackermann steering. Model answer: Differential drive turns in place and is simple, ideal for indoor robots. Ackermann steering is car-like, efficient at speed, but needs turning radius, better for outdoor vehicles.

Question three: What is an occupancy grid? Model answer: A map dividing space into cells marked free, occupied, or unknown, built from sensor readings and used by planners to find safe paths.

Question four: Name two local obstacle avoidance methods. Model answer: Dynamic window approach, evaluating safe achievable velocities; and vector field histograms, steering through low-cost directions in a local cost map.

Question five: Why should mobile robots among people move smoothly and predictably? Model answer: Sudden movements frighten people and cause accidents; predictable, legible motion builds trust and enables comfortable sharing of space.

Question six: What is the kidnapped robot problem? Model answer: A robot moved without its knowledge must re-localize in the map, usually solved with place recognition and global localization algorithms like particle filters.

PART 11 - GLOSSARY AND CLOSING RECAP

Mobile robot: a robot able to move through the environment.

Pose: the robot's position and orientation.

Differential drive: two-wheeled locomotion with speed difference turning.

Ackermann steering: car-like steering geometry.

Omnidirectional: able to move in any direction without turning.

Odometry: estimating motion from wheel rotation counts.

Dead reckoning: estimating position by integrating motion from a starting point.

Localization: estimating where the robot is.

Mapping: building a representation of the environment.

Occupancy grid: a cell map of free and occupied space.

Kalman filter: optimal fusion for dynamic state estimation.

Particle filter: multi-hypothesis estimation for ambiguous localization.

Path planning: finding a route from start to goal.

Obstacle avoidance: reacting to unexpected objects in the path.

Dynamic window approach: choosing safe velocities locally.

Pure pursuit: a path-following controller aiming at lookahead points.

SLAM: simultaneous localization and mapping.

Loop closure: recognizing revisited places to correct drift.

Social navigation: moving among people comfortably and legibly.

Docking: precise approach to a charging or pickup station.

PART 12 - MORE PRACTICAL SCENARIOS IN MOBILE ROBOTICS

Scenario one: mobile robots in warehouses. Fleets of hundreds of robots carry shelves to pickers in vast fulfillment centers. Each robot localizes against floor markers and fleet software coordinates traffic like an air traffic control system. The engineering challenges: congestion management, battery swapping schedules, and graceful handling of unexpected obstacles like fallen boxes. Students can experiment with multi-robot path planning in simulation using free tools.

Scenario two: agricultural rovers on African farms. A wheeled rover with high ground clearance drives between crop rows, cameras looking down to detect weeds and pests, GPS guiding straight lines, and a small tank spraying only where needed. The terrain is rough, dust is constant, and connectivity is limited, so edge processing and robust enclosures matter. The value: reduced chemical cost and healthier crops.

Scenario three: inspection robots in pipelines and tunnels. Small tracked robots crawl through pipes with cameras and sensors, detecting corrosion and blockages. Tethered or wireless, they face dark, wet, and tight conditions. Motion planning is short-range and reactive; safety means never getting stuck and always being retrievable.

Scenario four: cleaning robots in public buildings. After-hours cleaning robots navigate halls and rooms methodically. They dock to charge, report completed areas, and handle unexpected obstacles like chairs left in corridors. Predictable patterns and quiet operation build acceptance among building staff.

Scenario five: mobile robots in hospitals. Beyond delivery, robots transport lab samples and linens, riding elevators and reporting status. They must integrate with hospital workflows, respect infection control rules, and never delay urgent human traffic. The best designs involve nurses and porters in the process design.

Scenario six: personal mobility and assistive robots. Robotic wheelchairs and guide robots help people with mobility and vision challenges navigate complex indoor spaces. The stakes are dignity and independence; the design is person-centered, adjusting assistance level to user preference.

In each scenario, notice the recurring lessons: match locomotion to terrain, calibrate sensors, fuse for localization, plan in layers, avoid obstacles reactively, move courteously among people, and design for maintenance in the field. Mobile robotics mastery is the integration of these habits.

PART 14 - FINAL GLOSSARY ADDITIONS AND FAREWELL

A few more terms to complete your mobile robotics vocabulary. Waypoint navigation: moving through a sequence of planned points. Trajectory: a time-stamped path specifying where the robot should be when. Cost map: a grid assigning traversal costs used by planners. Cross-track error: the distance from the desired path. Heading: the direction the robot faces. Simultaneous localization and mapping: building maps while localizing. Visual odometry: estimating motion from camera images. Wheel slip: loss of traction corrupting odometry. Teleoperation: remote human control for stuck or tricky situations. Field of view: the angular coverage of a sensor. Deskewing: correcting scans distorted by motion. Traversal cost: how expensive it is to cross a cell in a map.

With these terms, your mobile robotics vocabulary spans wheels to fleets. Study steadily, build patiently, and let every robot you make move through the world with skill and courtesy.

PART 17 - ADDITIONAL STUDY NOTES FOR MOBILE ROBOTICS

Let me add a few more study notes to consolidate your learning. First, on coordinate frames: robots live among multiple frames: the world frame, the robot base frame, the sensor frames, and the goal frame. Transformations between them, the same matrix tools from computer graphics and kinematics, keep everything consistent. When something feels wrong in localization or planning, check your frames first; frame confusion is the most common bug in mobile robotics.

Second, on timing: mobile robots are real-time systems. Sensor readings arrive at different rates; control loops run faster than perception; planning runs slower still. Design your software so each layer runs at its natural rate and passes clean data upward. ROS 2, which you will study, organizes exactly this.

Third, on testing discipline: always test odometry first, then localization, then planning, then full autonomy. Layered testing isolates failures quickly. Keep a test course: tape loops, cardboard obstacles, and a marked docking spot; repeatable tests produce comparable results.

Fourth, on field conditions: rain makes floors slippery and sensors unreliable; sunlight blinds cameras; crowds change everything. Test in the messiest conditions your robot will meet. The lab is a hypothesis; the field is the exam.

Fifth, on documentation: keep a build log with photos, code versions, parameter values, and test results. When something works, capture the parameters that made it work. Future you, facing a regression at midnight, will be grateful.
