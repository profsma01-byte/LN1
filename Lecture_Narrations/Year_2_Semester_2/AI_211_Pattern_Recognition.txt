PART 1 - WELCOME: THE SCIENCE OF FINDING ORDER IN DATA

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 211, Pattern Recognition. Your brain is a pattern recognition machine. You recognize a friend's face in a crowd instantly, tell the sound of your mother's voice from others, read messy handwriting, and know when a machine sounds wrong. Pattern recognition is the science and engineering of building machines that do the same: finding regularities in data and classifying or describing what they observe.

Pattern recognition is the classical backbone of modern AI. Many ideas you met in machine learning and deep learning grew from this field: classifiers, feature extraction, decision boundaries, and evaluation methods. Here we build the classical foundations rigorously: Bayesian decision theory, statistical classifiers, clustering, dimensionality reduction, and evaluation methodology.

Why does this matter for your degree? Robots live on pattern recognition. A robot recognizes objects to grasp, gestures to follow, voices to obey, and anomalies to avoid. A self-driving car recognizes lanes, signs, and pedestrians. An inspection system recognizes defects. Behind every perception module you will ever build, pattern recognition principles are at work.

By the end of this lecture, you will understand the pattern recognition pipeline, the Bayesian view of decisions, classical classifiers from nearest neighbor to support vector machines, clustering with k-means, dimensionality reduction with PCA, evaluation with confusion matrices and ROC curves, and real applications from fingerprints to speech.

PART 2 - THE PATTERN RECOGNITION PIPELINE

Let us lay out the classic pipeline, step by step.

Step one: data acquisition. Collect observations: images, sounds, signals, measurements. Consider sensor quality and sampling, drawing on your DSP knowledge.

Step two: pre-processing. Clean and normalize: remove noise, correct lighting, standardize scales. Garbage in, garbage out; this step decides the ceiling of performance.

Step three: feature extraction. Convert raw data into informative numerical descriptors: edges and histograms for images, spectral coefficients for audio, statistical moments for signals. Good features make classes separable. Classical features like SIFT, HOG, and MFCCs were engineered by hand; modern deep learning learns features automatically, but understanding classical features builds engineering judgment.

Step four: feature selection and reduction. Choose the most informative features and reduce dimensionality, with PCA or feature importance methods. Fewer, better features mean faster and more robust classification.

Step five: classification or clustering. Assign class labels with a classifier, or discover groups with clustering. This is the decision stage.

Step six: post-processing and decision. Apply context, thresholds, or rejection options: when uncertain, say I do not know rather than guess. In safety-critical systems, rejection is wisdom.

Step seven: evaluation. Measure performance honestly with proper metrics and validation methodology.

A real-life example carries the pipeline. Fingerprint recognition: the finger is pressed on a sensor, acquisition; the image is enhanced, pre-processing; minutiae points, ridge endings and bifurcations, are extracted, features; the best subset is selected; matching compares against stored templates, classification; thresholds decide accept or reject; and error rates are measured, evaluation. Every security gate you have passed follows this pipeline.

PART 3 - BAYESIAN DECISION THEORY: DECISIONS UNDER UNCERTAINTY

Let us open the theoretical heart: Bayesian decision theory.

The core question: given evidence x, which class should we choose? Bayes' theorem answers it with beautiful clarity. The posterior probability of a class given the evidence is proportional to the likelihood of the evidence times the prior probability of the class. In words: update your prior belief by how well the evidence fits each class.

Let me make this concrete with a spam filter example. Prior: 20 percent of emails are spam. Likelihood: the word winner appears in 80 percent of spam but only 5 percent of normal mail. Evidence: an email contains winner. Posterior odds: spam becomes strongly favored. Compute with Bayes: posterior of spam given winner is high, so classify as spam.

The decision rule: choose the class with the highest posterior probability. This rule minimizes the overall probability of error. But some errors cost more than others. In medical diagnosis, missing a disease is far worse than a false alarm. Cost-sensitive Bayes adjusts the rule: choose the class that minimizes expected cost, not just error rate. This is the Bayesian view of your AI 203 fairness and safety lessons.

Likelihood functions describe how data is distributed within each class: Gaussian for measurements, multinomial for word counts. The discriminant function combines priors and likelihoods into a decision score. Linear discriminant analysis assumes equal Gaussian covariance, yielding linear decision boundaries; quadratic discriminant analysis allows different covariances, yielding curved boundaries.

Real-life application: medical testing. A disease affects 1 percent of people. A test is 90 percent sensitive and 95 percent specific. A patient tests positive. What is the actual probability of disease? Bayes' theorem shows it is far lower than 90 percent, because the disease is rare. Understanding this prevents mass panic and guides follow-up testing. Bayes is not just mathematics; it is clear thinking about evidence.

PART 4 - CLASSICAL CLASSIFIERS: THE ALGORITHM ZOO

Let us tour classical classifiers with practical guidance.

Nearest neighbor. Classify by majority vote of the closest training examples. Simple, intuitive, and powerful with enough data. The k in k-nearest neighbors balances noise and detail: small k is sensitive to noise; large k smooths too much. Distance metrics matter: Euclidean for continuous, cosine for text. The weakness: slow with large datasets and sensitive to irrelevant features.

Linear and quadratic discriminants. Bayesian classifiers with Gaussian assumptions. Fast and interpretable, good baselines for measurement data.

Naive Bayes. Assumes features are independent given the class. Despite this naive assumption, it works famously for text classification and spam filtering. Its probability estimates can be crude, but its decisions are often excellent.

Decision trees. Split data with feature tests along branches to leaves that hold class decisions. Interpretable as flowcharts and valuable where explanations matter. Pruning prevents overfitting. Random forests aggregate many trees for robustness.

Support vector machines, SVMs. Find the maximum margin boundary between classes: the widest street separating them. The kernel trick maps data into higher dimensions where classes separate cleanly: radial basis functions handle curved boundaries. SVMs shine on medium-sized datasets with clear margins and remain competitive against deep learning when data is limited.

Kernel methods broadly: any linear method can be kernelized to handle nonlinear structure. This elegant idea unifies much of classical pattern recognition.

Combining classifiers. Ensembles: voting, bagging, and boosting. AdaBoost focuses on hard examples; gradient boosting builds strong predictors from weak ones. Winning competitions and production systems for tabular data.

Choosing wisely. Simple data and limited samples: LDA or naive Bayes. Text: naive Bayes or linear SVM. Medium structured data: random forest or gradient boosting. Clear margin problems: SVM with RBF. Interpretability required: trees or linear models. Deep learning when data is abundant and features are raw.

PART 5 - UNSUPERVISED PATTERN RECOGNITION: CLUSTERING

Not all pattern recognition has labels. Clustering finds groups in data without supervision.

K-means clustering. Choose k cluster centers; assign each point to the nearest; recompute centers as means; repeat until stable. Simple, fast, and widely used. The challenges: choosing k, sensitivity to initialization, and assuming spherical clusters. The elbow method helps choose k: plot within-cluster variation against k and look for the bend.

Hierarchical clustering. Build a tree of clusters: agglomerative merges from the bottom up; divisive splits from the top down. Dendrograms visualize relationships. No need to pre-specify k; cut the tree where it suits.

DBSCAN. Density-based clustering: groups dense regions separated by sparse areas. It finds arbitrary shapes and marks noise points. Great for spatial data and anomaly detection.

Evaluation of clustering. Internal measures: silhouette score compares within-cluster cohesion to between-cluster separation. External measures compare to ground truth when available. Always visualize clusters in reduced dimensions to build intuition.

Applications. Customer segmentation in marketing: distinct shopper types for targeted services. Image segmentation: grouping pixels into regions. Bioinformatics: grouping genes with similar expression. Anomaly detection: rare points outside clusters are suspicious, like fraud in transactions or defects in manufacturing.

Practical exercise: cluster your own data. Record daily activities: study hours, sleep, exercise, screen time for two weeks. Run k-means and see what lifestyle clusters emerge. This personal experiment makes clustering tangible.

PART 6 - DIMENSIONALITY REDUCTION: SEEING THE ESSENCE

Real data often has too many features. Dimensionality reduction finds the essential structure.

Principal component analysis, PCA. Find directions of maximum variance: the principal components. Project data onto the top components to reduce dimensions while keeping most information. PCA is the workhorse of visualization and pre-processing. Face recognition classically uses eigenfaces, PCA of face images.

Independent component analysis separates mixed signals into independent sources: the cocktail party problem of unmixing voices from microphones. Your DSP course echoes here.

Linear discriminant analysis reduces dimensions while maximizing class separation, supervised unlike PCA.

Manifold learning: t-SNE and UMAP for visualization. They preserve local neighborhoods and reveal clusters in D embeddings. Modern practice: use UMAP for exploration and PCA for production pipelines.

Autoencoders, which you met in deep learning, learn nonlinear dimensionality reduction with neural networks. Powerful but less interpretable than PCA.

Why reduce dimensions? Faster computation, less overfitting, better visualization, and noise removal. The curse of dimensionality: in high dimensions, distances concentrate and data becomes sparse; reducing dimensions restores meaning to distance and density.

Real-life case: gene expression data with thousands of genes per sample. PCA reduces to a few components revealing patient subgroups; clustering those components guides treatment research. Dimensionality reduction turned overwhelming data into medical insight.

PART 7 - EVALUATION: MEASURING RECOGNITION HONESTLY

You met evaluation in machine learning; pattern recognition adds classical depth.

Confusion matrices summarize classification: true positives, true negatives, false positives, false negatives. From them: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1. In recognition systems, false accept rate and false reject rate are the classic pair: security systems tune the trade-off between convenience and safety.

ROC curves sweep the decision threshold, plotting true positive rate against false positive rate. The area under the curve, AUC, summarizes overall quality: 0.5 is chance; 1.0 is perfect. The equal error rate is where false accepts equal false rejects, a common summary in biometrics.

Validation methodology. Hold-out sets are simple but variable. K-fold cross-validation rotates the test set for stable estimates. Stratified folds preserve class balance. Leave-one-out for tiny datasets. Never tune on the test set; the discipline of train, validation, test preserves honesty.

Bias-variance in evaluation: repeated cross-validation quantifies variance; learning curves diagnose data sufficiency.

Real-life stakes. A fingerprint system with a 1 percent false accept rate lets one in a hundred impostors through. A cancer screening system with a 5 percent false negative rate misses one in twenty cancers. Metrics are not abstract; they are lives and losses. Choose and report them with the gravity they deserve.

Practical habit: always report confusion matrices and class-wise metrics, not only overall accuracy. And include confidence intervals from multiple runs; single numbers hide variability.

PART 10 - STUDY QUESTUES WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: What is Bayes' theorem and why does it matter in classification? Model answer: It updates prior class beliefs with evidence likelihoods to give posterior probabilities. Classification chooses the class with highest posterior or lowest expected cost, grounding decisions in probability.

Question two: Explain the kernel trick in SVMs. Model answer: Kernels compute similarity in higher-dimensional spaces without explicitly transforming data, allowing linear separators to solve nonlinear problems efficiently.

Question three: When would you use k-means over hierarchical clustering? Model answer: K-means for large datasets with roughly spherical clusters and known k; hierarchical for smaller data where relationships and dendrograms are valuable.

Question four: What is the curse of dimensionality? Model answer: In high dimensions, data becomes sparse and distances lose meaning, hurting distance-based methods. Dimensionality reduction restores statistical meaning and efficiency.

Question five: Define false accept rate and false reject rate. Model answer: False accept rate is the fraction of impostors wrongly accepted; false reject rate is the fraction of genuine users wrongly rejected. Systems tune thresholds trading one against the other.

Question six: Name two applications of pattern recognition in robotics. Model answer: Object recognition for grasping and gesture recognition for human-robot interaction.

PART 11 - GLOSSARY AND CLOSING RECAP

Pattern recognition: identifying regularities and classes in data.

Feature: a numerical descriptor of an observation.

Decision boundary: the surface separating classes in feature space.

Bayes' theorem: updating beliefs with evidence via likelihoods and priors.

Posterior probability: the updated probability of a class given evidence.

Likelihood: how probable the evidence is under a class.

Prior: the initial probability of a class.

Discriminant function: the scoring function for classification decisions.

k-nearest neighbors: classification by majority vote of closest examples.

Support vector machine: maximum margin classifier with kernel extensions.

Decision tree: hierarchical feature-test classifier.

Random forest: ensemble of decision trees.

Naive Bayes: classifier assuming feature independence.

K-means: clustering by iterative centroid assignment.

Hierarchical clustering: tree-structured grouping.

DBSCAN: density-based clustering.

Principal component analysis: variance-maximizing dimensionality reduction.

Eigenfaces: PCA applied to face images.

Manifold learning: nonlinear dimensionality reduction like t-SNE and UMAP.

Confusion matrix: table of correct and incorrect predictions.

ROC curve: threshold sweep of true versus false positive rates.

AUC: area under the ROC curve summarizing discrimination quality.

False accept rate: impostors wrongly accepted.

False reject rate: genuine users wrongly rejected.

Cross-validation: rotating test splits for stable evaluation.

PART 14 - WORKED EXAMPLES FOR EXAM PREPARATION

Let me walk through worked examples to cement the mathematics.

Worked example one: Bayes for a security gate. A face recognition gate: genuine users are 99 percent of attempts; impostors 1 percent. The system falsely accepts 0.1 percent of impostors and falsely rejects 1 percent of genuine users. Question: if an attempt is accepted, what is the probability it is genuine? Solution by Bayes: accepted genuine attempts are 0.99 times 0.99, about 0.98. Accepted impostors are 0.01 times 0.001, about 0.00001. Posterior of genuine given accept is 0.98 divided by 0.98001, essentially 0.99999. The gate is extremely secure for genuine traffic. Practice computing both posteriors: accept and reject.

Worked example two: k-means by hand. Five points on a line at positions 1, 2, 8, 9, 10. Choose k equals 2, initial centers at 1 and 2. First assignment: points near 1 join center 1; points near 8 join center 2. Recompute means: center 1 becomes 1.5; center 2 becomes 9. Reassign: same grouping. Converged. Two clusters emerge: 1,2 and 8,9,10. Now change initial centers to 1 and 10 and confirm the same result. Try k equals 3 and describe the clusters. These small hand computations build intuition for larger data.

Worked example three: PCA intuition. Three measurements: height, arm span, and leg length, all correlated with overall size. PCA finds the first component capturing general size, explaining most variance; the second capturing proportions. Reducing to one component keeps most information. Compute correlations on a small table of student measurements to see this live.

Worked example four: ROC reading. Given a classifier tested at four thresholds, with true positive rates 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4 and false positive rates 0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, plot the ROC points and connect them. The area under this curve estimates discrimination quality. Choose the threshold matching context costs: screening favors high true positive rate; security favors low false positive rate.

Exam tip six: always state assumptions in Bayes problems: priors and likelihoods. Exam tip seven: in clustering questions, mention initialization sensitivity of k-means and how multiple restarts help. Exam tip eight: define discrimination and calibration as separate qualities: a model can rank well yet give poor probabilities.

PART 16 - FINAL GLOSSARY COMPLETION AND FAREWELL

A last glossary completion for your pattern recognition toolkit. Observation: one measured instance to classify. Class: a category an observation belongs to. Class-conditional density: how features distribute within a class. Decision region: the feature space area assigned to a class. Margin: the distance between classes or to the boundary. Support vector: the critical points defining an SVM boundary. Kernel: a function computing similarity in transformed space. Centroid: the mean point of a cluster. Silhouette score: a cluster quality measure comparing cohesion and separation. Dendrogram: the tree diagram of hierarchical clustering. Scree plot: a PCA plot of variance per component. Reconstruction error: how well a reduced representation rebuilds data. Threshold: the decision cutoff tuning error trade-offs. Calibration: the agreement between predicted probabilities and outcomes. Overfitting: fitting noise at the cost of generalization. Feature scaling: standardizing ranges for fair comparison. Whitening: decorrelating and normalizing features. Ensemble: combining multiple models for better decisions. Bias: systematic error from wrong assumptions. Variance: sensitivity to training data fluctuations. Rejection option: declining to classify when uncertain.

With these terms, your pattern recognition vocabulary spans theory to deployment. Use it with precision and confidence.
