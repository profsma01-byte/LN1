PART 1 - WELCOME: THE MATHEMATICS OF ROBOT MOTION

Good morning, everyone, and welcome to AI 212, Robot Kinematics. If control systems are the muscles of robotics and perception is the senses, kinematics is the geometry of motion. Kinematics describes where a robot's body parts are and how joint movements translate into the motion of hands, tools, and wheels. It is pure geometry: no forces, no masses, just shapes and angles and positions.

Why does kinematics matter? Every robot task begins with geometry. To pick up a cup, the robot must know where its gripper is and how to move it to the cup. To weld a seam, the tool must follow a precise curve at the right angle. To navigate, a mobile robot must understand how wheel rotations translate into travel. Kinematics is the language that connects a robot's joints to its actions.

This course is the mathematical heart of your robotics training, and it builds directly on the transformations you met in computer graphics and the mobile kinematics from AI 207. Here we go deeper: full three-dimensional rotations, homogeneous transformations, forward and inverse kinematics of robot arms, the Jacobian, velocity kinematics, and the analysis of workspace and singularities.

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to describe robot poses with mathematics, compute where a robot's hand is given its joint angles, that is forward kinematics, compute what joint angles are needed to reach a desired position, that is inverse kinematics, and understand why some positions are impossible or awkward, that is workspace and singularity analysis.

Do not fear the mathematics. We will use simple examples with numbers you can follow by hand. The power of kinematics is that it turns the physical movement of robots into calculations, and calculations are something engineers can trust.

PART 2 - DESCRIBING POSITION AND ORIENTATION

Let us build the foundations: how to describe where things are.

Position in space. A point in three dimensions has three coordinates: x, y, and z, relative to a reference frame. A frame is an origin plus three perpendicular axes. Everything in robotics is described relative to some frame: the world frame, the robot base frame, the gripper frame.

Orientation is subtler. How do we describe how something is turned? Several tools exist. Rotation matrices: three-by-three matrices whose columns are the directions of the rotated frame's axes. They are orthonormal, columns perpendicular and unit length, and they rotate points by multiplication. Euler angles: three successive rotations about chosen axes, like roll, pitch, and yaw for aircraft and ships. Intuitive but with a famous trap: gimbal lock, where two axes align and a degree of freedom vanishes. Axis-angle representation: one vector giving the rotation axis and one angle. Quaternions: four-number representations free of gimbal lock, used heavily in flight controllers and graphics.

Let me give intuition. Imagine holding a book. Its position is where it sits on the table. Its orientation is how it is turned: flat, tilted toward you, rotated sideways. Position moves the book; orientation turns it. Every robot hand pose is a position plus an orientation: where is the hand, and how is the palm facing?

Homogeneous transformation matrices, four-by-four, combine rotation and translation in one object. They are the workhorses of kinematics: multiplying transformations chains frames together. You met them in computer graphics; here they become your daily tool.

Composition of transformations. If you know the base-to-shoulder transform and the shoulder-to-elbow transform, multiplying them gives base-to-elbow. This chaining is exactly how robot arms are analyzed: link by link from base to tip.

Real-life example: a camera on a robot arm. The camera-to-gripper transform is fixed by mounting. The gripper-to-base transform comes from kinematics. Multiplying gives camera-to-base, letting the robot use camera observations in its own coordinate frame. This is the mathematics behind visual servoing and hand-eye coordination.

PART 3 - FORWARD KINEMATICS: WHERE IS THE HAND?

Now the first great calculation: forward kinematics. Given the joint angles, where is the robot's hand?

Let us work a two-link planar arm by hand. A simple arm with two links: link one of length one meter from the shoulder, link two of length one meter from the elbow. Joint one turns the shoulder by angle theta one; joint two turns the elbow by angle theta two.

Compute the elbow position. From the shoulder at the origin: elbow x equals one times cosine of theta one; elbow y equals one times sine of theta one. With theta one equals zero, the elbow sits at one, zero.

Compute the hand position. The second link adds its angle to the first: the hand angle is theta one plus theta two. Hand x equals cosine theta one plus cosine of theta one plus theta two. Hand y equals sine theta one plus sine of theta one plus theta two.

Try numbers. Theta one equals zero, theta two equals zero: hand at two, zero, arm stretched straight forward. Theta one equals ninety degrees, theta two equals zero: hand at zero, two, arm straight up. Theta one equals ninety degrees, theta two equals minus ninety degrees: hand at one, one, elbow bent at a right angle. Work these through on paper until the geometry feels natural.

Generalizing. Each link has parameters: length, twist, offset, and joint angle. The famous Denavit-Hartenberg convention, DH parameters, standardizes these four numbers per joint. Multiplying the joint transforms from base to tip gives the complete forward kinematics. Every industrial robot's datasheet contains its DH parameters or equivalent.

Real-life use. Forward kinematics answers: if I command these joint angles, where will my tool be? It is used constantly: in simulation to render robot poses, in control to compare desired and actual positions, and in calibration to verify robot accuracy.

Practical tip: implement forward kinematics in Python or MATLAB for a two-link arm and plot the hand position for various angles. Seeing the geometry in code cements the mathematics.

PART 4 - INVERSE KINEMATICS: HOW DO I REACH THERE?

Now the second great calculation, and the harder one: inverse kinematics. Given where I want the hand to be, what joint angles do I need?

Why is it hard? Forward kinematics is one calculation: angles in, position out. Inverse kinematics can have many answers, one answer, or none. A two-link arm can reach a point with elbow up or elbow down: two solutions. Some points are beyond reach: no solution. And as complexity grows with six joints, the equations become a system of nonlinear trigonometric equations.

Geometric approach for the two-link arm. Given a target x, y within reach, compute the distance from shoulder to target: r equals square root of x squared plus y squared. Use the law of cosines to find the elbow angle: cosine of the interior angle equals one squared plus one squared minus r squared, all divided by two times one times one. The shoulder angle follows from the direction to the target minus half the elbow adjustment. Work through this on paper with r equals one: the arm forms an equilateral triangle; interior angles are all sixty degrees.

Multiple solutions. Elbow up and elbow down both reach the same point. Robot controllers choose based on context: avoid obstacles, stay far from joint limits, keep the elbow clear of the table. Choosing among solutions is engineering judgment encoded in code.

Analytical versus numerical methods. For simple arms, closed-form solutions exist and are fast: preferred for real-time control. For general arms, numerical methods iterate: guess angles, compute forward kinematics, measure error, adjust angles with the Jacobian, repeat. Newton-Raphson style methods converge quickly near solutions.

Special cases. When the target is exactly at the workspace boundary, the arm fully extends and precision degrades: near singularities, as we will see. When targets are outside the workspace, the best the controller can do is reach as close as possible along the direction of the target.

Real-life case: a welding robot following a car body seam. The seam is a 3D curve; for each point and tool orientation along the curve, inverse kinematics computes joint angles. The controller interpolates smoothly between points. Any failure to solve would stop the line; robust inverse kinematics is industrial gold.

Practical project: implement inverse kinematics for the two-link arm in Python, including elbow-up and elbow-down branches, and animate the arm reaching for moving targets.

PART 5 - THE JACOBIAN: VELOCITY AND FORCE RELATIONSHIPS

Let us meet one of the most useful objects in robotics: the Jacobian.

The Jacobian relates joint velocities to hand velocities. If you know how fast each joint is turning, the Jacobian computes how fast the hand moves and in what direction. It is the matrix of partial derivatives, but the intuition is simple: it is the gearbox ratio between joints and hand.

Why does this matter? Motion planning needs smooth hand trajectories; the Jacobian converts them into joint commands. Force control needs the reverse mapping: what joint torques produce a desired hand force? The transpose of the Jacobian does that. Singularities appear where the Jacobian loses rank: directions where the hand cannot move however fast joints turn.

Computing the Jacobian for the two-link arm. The first column is the partial derivative of hand position with respect to theta one; the second with respect to theta two. For our planar arm, these are cross products of joint axes with hand offsets: classic results you will derive in coursework.

Velocity kinematics in practice. A robot tracking a moving conveyor: vision measures the target velocity; the Jacobian converts desired hand velocity into joint velocity commands; the controller executes. This is the core of visual servoing.

Force kinematics. In assembly tasks, the robot must push with controlled force. The Jacobian transpose maps desired hand forces to joint torques. Impedance control shapes the relationship between hand motion and force, letting robots behave like soft springs when touching parts.

Singularity awareness. When the arm stretches fully straight, it cannot move outward any further; the Jacobian becomes singular in that direction. In practice, controllers slow near singularities, and planners avoid them. Understanding the Jacobian is understanding where robot motion loses freedom.

Real-life case: a surgical robot arm translating a surgeon's hand motions. Hand velocity commands are multiplied by the inverse Jacobian to drive joints; force feedback goes the other way through the transpose. The mathematics you learn here literally operates inside operating rooms.

PART 6 - WORKSPACE, SINGULARITIES, AND JOINT LIMITS

Let us study where robots can and cannot reach.

The workspace is the volume of all points the hand can reach. For a two-link arm, it is an annulus, a ring between radii zero and two meters for our example arm. With six joints, workspaces become complex volumes. Designers shape workspaces to fit tasks: a painting robot needs a wide shell; an assembly robot needs a compact, dexterous zone.

Workspace boundaries matter practically. At full extension, accuracy drops and torques rise. Near the inner boundary, the arm folds and self-collision threatens. Planners keep tasks comfortably inside boundaries.

Singularities. Configurations where the Jacobian loses rank: the arm loses one or more degrees of freedom. The classic stretched-arm singularity: all joints aligned, the hand cannot move along the arm direction. The wrist singularity: wrist axes align, losing orientation freedom. Near singularities, joint speeds can explode for small hand motions; controllers detect and avoid them.

Joint limits. Every physical joint has a range: a shoulder might turn plus or minus one hundred eighty degrees. Inverse kinematics must respect these; solutions outside limits are invalid. Path planning keeps joints within limits with margin.

Redundancy. Arms with more than six joints, like seven-axis robots, are redundant: infinitely many configurations reach the same pose. The extra freedom is used to avoid obstacles, stay far from singularities, or keep elbows clear of people. Redundancy resolution is optimization with constraints.

Real-life case: a robot working near a wall. The planner selects elbow-up configurations keeping the arm clear of the wall; singularity checks ensure smooth motion; joint limits guarantee no self-damage. Workspace analysis conducted before installation prevents expensive surprises.

Practical exercise: plot the workspace of a two-link arm in Python by sampling joint angles and scattering hand positions. Mark singular configurations. This visual builds intuition that tables cannot.

PART 7 - MOBILE ROBOT KINEMATICS REVISITED

Let us connect to mobile robotics with a brief deeper look.

Differential drive forward kinematics: given left and right wheel speeds and the track width, compute robot linear and angular velocity: v is the average; omega is the difference over track width. Inverse kinematics: given desired v and omega, compute wheel speeds: left equals v minus omega times half track width; right equals v plus omega times half track width.

Odometry integration: integrating velocities over time gives pose estimates, with the drift you learned to correct. These equations run in every robot vacuum and warehouse robot.

Nonholonomic constraints: car-like robots cannot move sideways; the bicycle model relates steering angle and speed to motion. Planning must respect these constraints; the mathematics of curvature and turning radius guides path design.

Mobile manipulators: robots with arms on moving bases combine both kinematics: base pose plus arm configuration. Coordination between them expands the effective workspace enormously.

Real-life case: a forklift-like autonomous vehicle positioning pallets. Base kinematics approach the pallet; arm or mast kinematics lift precisely. The same mathematics you learned for arms and wheels works together.

PART 9 - STUDY QUESTIONS WITH MODEL ANSWERS

Question one: What is the difference between forward and inverse kinematics? Model answer: Forward kinematics computes hand pose from joint angles. Inverse kinematics computes joint angles for a desired hand pose, often with multiple solutions or none.

Question two: Why can inverse kinematics have multiple solutions? Model answer: Different arm configurations, like elbow up and elbow down, can place the hand at the same point. The controller chooses based on context and constraints.

Question three: What is a singularity and why does it matter? Model answer: A configuration where the Jacobian loses rank and the hand loses a degree of freedom. Near singularities, joint speeds can explode and control degrades, so planners avoid them.

Question four: What does the Jacobian relate? Model answer: Joint velocities to hand velocities, and via its transpose, hand forces to joint torques. It is central to motion and force control.

Question five: What are DH parameters? Model answer: A convention using four parameters per joint, length, twist, offset, angle, to standardize kinematic chains and derive forward kinematics systematically.

Question six: Name one use of kinematics in mobile robots. Model answer: Differential drive kinematics converts wheel speeds to robot motion for odometry and control.

PART 10 - GLOSSARY AND CLOSING RECAP

Kinematics: the geometry of motion without forces.

Pose: position plus orientation of a body.

Frame: a coordinate system with origin and axes.

Rotation matrix: a three-by-three matrix describing orientation.

Euler angles: three rotations describing orientation.

Quaternion: a four-number orientation representation without gimbal lock.

Homogeneous transformation: a four-by-four matrix combining rotation and translation.

Forward kinematics: computing end-effector pose from joint angles.

Inverse kinematics: computing joint angles for desired end-effector pose.

Denavit-Hartenberg parameters: a standard convention for kinematic chains.

Jacobian: the matrix relating joint and end-effector velocities.

Singularity: a configuration losing degrees of freedom.

Workspace: the volume reachable by the end-effector.

Joint limit: the allowed range of a joint's motion.

Redundancy: having more joints than needed for a task.

Elbow up and elbow down: alternative inverse kinematics solutions.

Odometry: pose estimation from wheel motions.

Nonholonomic constraint: motion restrictions like no sideways sliding.

Visual servoing: controlling motion using camera feedback through kinematics.

PART 11 - MORE PRACTICAL SCENARIOS IN ROBOT KINEMATICS

Scenario one: kinematics for a painting robot in an automotive plant. The robot must hold a spray gun at constant distance from curved car body panels. Forward kinematics computes the tool pose from joint angles; inverse kinematics computes the angles for each point along the surface; the Jacobian maintains constant tool speed for even coating. Singularity avoidance keeps motion smooth near the roof edges. The mathematics you learned literally paints cars.

Scenario two: kinematics for a fruit picking arm. The arm reaches into branches at awkward angles: joint limits must avoid self-collision with the trunk; elbow-down configurations keep the gripper clear of leaves; redundant joints help the arm snake between branches. Inverse kinematics solutions are chosen for clearance and gentleness.

Scenario three: kinematics for a camera on a robot head. The neck has pan and tilt joints; forward kinematics gives the camera pose in the head frame; composing with body and world transforms lets the robot point at objects it hears about. Simple two-joint kinematics, powerful capabilities.

Scenario four: kinematics for a wheelchair-mounted robotic arm. The combined system has mobile base plus arm; the workspace analysis must include base motion; nonholonomic constraints of the base combine with arm redundancy. Users need the arm to reach tables, shelves, and doors: workspace analysis shapes the whole design.

Scenario five: kinematics for a drone camera gimbal. The gimbal's pan, tilt, and roll joints keep the camera level and pointed at targets as the drone moves. Gimbal kinematics plus drone pose from inertial sensing gives stable video. Your computer graphics and control courses join kinematics here.

Scenario six: kinematics for a sewing or textile robot. Precise curved seams demand continuous path control: dense waypoints along the curve, smooth interpolation, and velocity control through the Jacobian. Fabric handling adds compliance, where force kinematics meets material physics.

In each scenario, notice how the same toolkit applies: describe poses, chain transforms, compute forward and inverse kinematics, check the Jacobian for smooth motion, respect limits and singularities, and choose among solutions with task wisdom. Kinematics is universal in robotics; practice makes it fluent.

PART 13 - FINAL GLOSSARY ADDITIONS AND FAREWELL

A few more terms to complete your kinematics vocabulary. End-effector: the tool or gripper at the arm's end. Link: a rigid segment between joints. Joint: a movable connection between links. Revolute joint: a rotating joint. Prismatic joint: a sliding joint. Degree of freedom: an independent motion of the mechanism. Configuration space: the space of all joint angle combinations. Manipulability: a measure of how dexterous a configuration is. Trajectory: a time sequence of configurations. Interpolation: computing intermediate points along a path. Calibration: measuring actual robot geometry versus assumed. Tool center point: the working point of the tool used as the reference. Base frame: the reference frame at the robot's foundation. End-effector frame: the frame at the tool. Transformation composition: chaining transforms by multiplication. Numerical inverse kinematics: iterative solutions to reach targets. Analytical inverse kinematics: closed-form solutions via geometry. Path: a geometric route without timing. Trajectory with timing: positions plus velocities and accelerations. Compliance: yielding under force, blending kinematics with dynamics.

With these terms, your kinematics vocabulary is complete for the road ahead. Study steadily, derive patiently, and let every robot you touch move with mathematical grace.

PART 16 - GUIDED REVIEW OF CORE FORMULAS AND PRINCIPLES

Let me guide you through a final review of the core formulas and principles of this course, spoken slowly so they settle into memory.

First, pose representation. A pose is a position and an orientation. Position is a point in space; orientation tells which way the object faces. Together they describe where a body is and how it is turned.

Second, rotation matrices. A three by three matrix with orthonormal columns rotates vectors between frames. Compose rotations by multiplying matrices; invert them by transposing them. Remember that rotation order matters: turning left then right is different from turning right then left.

Third, homogeneous transforms. A four by four matrix combines rotation and translation in one object. Compose transforms by multiplication, from the fixed base outward toward the tool. Invert them by block matrix inversion or by geometric reasoning: swap the roles of the frames.

Fourth, forward kinematics. Given joint angles, compute the end-effector pose. For the two-link planar arm, the position is link one times cosine of theta one plus link two times cosine of theta one plus theta two, and similarly with sines for the vertical component. This formula is the foundation of all arm motion; know it by heart.

Fifth, inverse kinematics. Given a target pose, find joint angles. For the two-link arm, use the law of cosines: the distance to the target squared equals link one squared plus link two squared plus two times link one times link two times cosine of the elbow angle. Two solutions exist: elbow up and elbow down. Choose based on obstacles, joint limits, and comfort.

Sixth, the Jacobian matrix. It maps joint velocities to end-effector velocities. Where the Jacobian loses rank, the arm hits a singularity: directions of motion are lost and joint speeds may explode. Detect singularities by checking the determinant or condition number.

Seventh, workspace analysis. The workspace is every point the tool can reach. It is shaped by link lengths, joint limits, and mechanical design. Plot it early when designing any robot.

Eighth, mobile robot kinematics. Differential drive robots turn by wheel speed differences; their motion is nonholonomic. Odometry integrates wheel motions to estimate pose, with drift that sensor fusion corrects.

Ninth, practical engineering. Verify every analytical formula with a numerical check. Test round trips: angles to pose to angles. Use proven libraries for complex robots, but understand the mathematics they implement.

Tenth, the mindset. Kinematics rewards patience, steady practice, and geometric imagination. Sketch constantly, derive steadily, verify rigorously. The engineers who master kinematics design robots that move with precision, reliability, and grace in every application they touch.

Carry these formulas in your mind and these principles in your habits. They will serve you in every robotics challenge that comes your way. And keep a small reference card of these formulas in your own words. When you face a new robot mechanism, start by naming its joints and links, sketch its frames, and write its forward kinematics before touching any code. This disciplined habit turns intimidating machines into familiar chains of geometry. Review the card weekly until recall becomes instant and effortless for you.
