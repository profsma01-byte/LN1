PART 1 - WELCOME TO COGNITIVE ROBOTICS

Hello everyone, and welcome to AI 305, Cognitive Robotics. This is a course about giving robots minds: the ability to perceive, remember, reason, plan, learn, and even understand something of human intentions. In Year Three you have built strong foundations in perception, control, kinematics, and software. Now we bring those threads together and ask the deepest question in our field: how do we build robots that think. I am delighted to guide you through this material, and as always we will ground everything in real life practicals and examples from Nigeria and Africa.

What is cognitive robotics? Cognitive robotics is the field that designs robots with cognitive capabilities: perception beyond raw sensing, memory of the past, reasoning about the world, planning toward goals, learning from experience, and interacting naturally with humans. A simple robot reacts: see obstacle, stop. A cognitive robot understands: there is a chair in my path, chairs can be moved, my goal is to clear the doorway, so I should move the chair politely. That gap from reaction to understanding is what this course is about.

Let me frame the difference with a clear example. Consider two robots asked to help set a dinner table. A reactive robot follows pre programmed routes to carry plates. A cognitive robot looks at the table, sees where plates and cups are already placed, infers what is missing, remembers where items were stored, plans its route to fetch a jug, notices a child in its path and waits politely, and adjusts when someone says put the cups near the sink. The cognitive robot perceives, remembers, reasons, plans, learns, and cooperates. Building such robots is the art and science of cognitive robotics.

Why does this matter for real life? Because the environments where robots are most needed, homes, hospitals, farms, workshops, streets, are unstructured and full of surprises. A factory robot can repeat one motion forever because the factory is designed around it. A robot that helps people must cope with clutter, change, and human intention. Cognitive skills make robots useful in the beautiful mess of real life.

Real life practicals make this vivid. Imagine a service robot in a Nigerian teaching hospital in Ibadan: it delivers medicines, remembers which room requested them, reasons about the best route when corridors are crowded, learns the daily routines of nurses, and takes spoken corrections gracefully. Imagine a farm assistant robot near Kano: it recognizes crop diseases from images, remembers where problems appeared last week, reasons about likely spread, and plans inspection routes. Imagine a household companion robot in Accra: it finds objects that have been moved, learns family schedules, and answers questions about where things are. These are cognitive robotics in action.

Cognitive robotics draws on all your studies. Neural networks and pattern recognition power perception and learning. Natural language processing enables human communication. Knowledge representation structures memory and reasoning. Search and planning, from algorithms courses, compute action sequences. Reinforcement learning improves behavior from experience. Control and kinematics execute plans in the physical world. And ethics matters profoundly, because robots that think and interact raise deep questions about trust, autonomy, and responsibility.

How will we study this course? We will begin with the architecture of cognitive systems: how perception, memory, reasoning, and action are organized. We will study knowledge representation and reasoning: how robots know things about the world. We will examine memory and learning architectures. We will study planning in detail, from classical search to task and motion planning. We will explore decision making under uncertainty. We will study natural human robot interaction, including language and gestures. We will look at learning from demonstration and from experience. We will study cognitive architectures that integrate everything. We will explore applications in service, healthcare, agriculture, and education. And we will end with futures and honest reflections on machine minds.

To succeed in this course, think in terms of models. A cognitive robot acts on its model of the world, not the world directly. Every perception updates the model; every plan runs against the model; every action compares model predictions with reality. Hold this loop in your mind, and cognitive robotics will be clear.

Let us begin the journey into robot minds.

PART 2 - ARCHITECTURES OF COGNITIVE SYSTEMS

Let me walk you through architectures: the grand designs that organize perception, memory, reasoning, and action into working cognitive robots.

A cognitive architecture is the blueprint of a thinking robot. It answers: what are the parts, how do they connect, and how does information flow. Just as a computer architecture organizes processor, memory, and input output, a cognitive architecture organizes sensing, world modeling, goal management, planning, and execution.

The classic sense plan act architecture. The robot senses the world, builds a model, makes a complete plan, then executes it. This architecture, dominant in early artificial intelligence, is clean and powerful for structured problems. Its weakness is the real world: while you plan, the world changes. Execution of long open loop plans fails in dynamic environments.

The behavior based architecture, championed by Rodney Brooks, reacted against sense plan act. Instead of a central model, the robot runs many simple behaviors in parallel: avoid obstacles, wander, explore, go home. Behaviors are layered and compete or combine through arbitration. Subsumption architecture lets higher behaviors subsume lower ones. These robots are robust and lively, but struggle with complex goals requiring deep deliberation.

Modern cognitive architectures blend both. Deliberative layers handle goals, knowledge, and planning at a slower pace. Reactive layers handle reflexes and safety at high speed. Executive layers bridge them, managing tasks, monitoring execution, and replanning when surprises occur. This three layer model is the workhorse of practical cognitive robotics.

Let me describe the information flow in such a system. Perception modules convert sensors into symbols and features: objects, people, locations. The world model stores these with uncertainty and time stamps. The goal manager holds tasks from users or mission logic. The planner searches the world model for action sequences achieving goals. The executive dispatches actions to controllers and monitors results. Execution feedback updates the world model, closing the loop.

Symbolic versus sub symbolic processing is a key design axis. Symbolic systems represent knowledge as explicit facts and rules: the cup is on the table; if grasped, then held. Sub symbolic systems, neural networks especially, represent knowledge in weights and activations: recognizing a cup from pixels. Cognitive robotics needs both. Symbols give reasoning and explanation; sub symbolic gives perception and pattern recognition. Modern systems integrate them: neural perception feeds symbolic reasoning.

Blackboard architectures let modules share a common knowledge repository, each contributing and reading, cooperating indirectly. This scales to many skills and is common in complex systems.

Standards and middleware support architecture: the Robot Operating System, which you are studying in parallel, provides the plumbing for modules to communicate. Ontologies give shared vocabularies. Behavior trees, which we will meet in planning, structure reactive and deliberative logic readably.

Real life practicals. A service robot team in Nigeria adopts a three layer architecture: reactive safety and reflexes at the base, a deliberative planner for tasks, and an executive managing interruptions like a person crossing the path. A student team debugging slow responses discovers their deliberative layer is blocking the reactive layer, and restructures to keep safety reflexes fast. Architecture is destiny: choose your organization deliberately, because it determines what your robot can do and how it fails.

A practical habit: draw your architecture diagram before coding, with data flows and timing marked. When behavior puzzles arise, the diagram guides your debugging.

PART 3 - KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION AND REASONING

Let me walk you through knowledge representation and reasoning: how robots know things, draw conclusions, and answer questions about their world.

Knowledge representation asks: how do we encode facts about the world in a form a computer can use. The choice of representation shapes what the robot can reason about and how efficiently.

Logical representations are the oldest and most rigorous. Propositional logic deals with true and false statements. First order logic adds objects, properties, and relations: cup is on table; red is a color. Rules expressed in logic enable deduction: if all humans are mortal and Socrates is human, then Socrates is mortal. Logical reasoning is precise and explainable, but can be computationally expensive and brittle with incomplete knowledge.

Semantic networks and graphs represent knowledge as nodes and edges: object nodes connected by relations like is a, part of, and located at. A cup is a vessel; a handle is part of a cup. Graph representations are intuitive, visualizable, and queryable with graph search.

Frames and object oriented representations bundle properties into structures: a chair frame has properties like color, position, and number of legs, with default values. This suits structured worlds and programming practice.

Ontologies are formal, shared vocabularies for a domain: defined classes, relations, and constraints. Ontologies let different programs and robots share understanding. In healthcare robotics, an ontology might define patients, wards, medicines, and procedures so all modules agree on meanings.

Rule based systems encode knowledge as if then rules and use inference engines to draw conclusions. Expert systems of the past, diagnosing diseases or configuring equipment, were rule based. Rule systems are transparent but struggle with uncertainty and large rule bases.

Reasoning methods forward chain from facts to conclusions, useful for monitoring and diagnosis; backward chain from goals to needed facts, useful for planning and question answering. Inference in first order logic uses resolution and unification. Description logics underpin modern ontologies and web standards.

Uncertainty pervades the real world, so knowledge systems represent and reason with it. Bayesian networks encode probabilistic dependencies: symptoms and diseases, sensors and states. Fuzzy logic, which you studied, handles vagueness: near, warm, almost full. Probabilistic reasoning answers questions like: given these observations, what is most likely true.

Common sense knowledge is the hidden challenge. Humans know that water is wet, that unsupported objects fall, that people do not like being blocked. Robots lack this vast background; projects attempt to encode common sense at scale. Cognitive robotics confronts this gap with hybrid approaches: learned models capture implicit common sense, symbolic systems capture explicit facts.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian hospital robot project builds an ontology of wards, patients, medications, and schedules so that perception, planning, and dialogue modules share vocabulary. A student implements a rule based diagnostic assistant for cassava diseases with forward chaining from observed symptoms. A service robot uses Bayesian reasoning to decide, from weak sensor evidence, which room a missing object is most likely in. Encode knowledge deliberately, and your robot's reasoning becomes reliable and explainable.

A practical habit: for every knowledge representation, write example queries and their expected answers first. The queries reveal whether the representation fits the questions you need answered.

PART 4 - MEMORY AND LEARNING IN COGNITIVE ROBOTS

Let me walk you through memory and learning: how cognitive robots remember the past and improve with experience.

Memory in cognitive systems comes in varieties, mirroring human memory. Short term working memory holds the current situation: what objects are visible, what task is active, what was just said. Long term memory stores durable knowledge: the map of the building, the location of tools, learned skills, and past episodes. Prospective memory holds intentions: remember to deliver the package at three o'clock.

Semantic memory stores general facts and concepts: cups are for drinking; kitchens contain plates. Episodic memory stores specific experiences: yesterday at ten, the meeting room was crowded. Both matter: semantics supports reasoning, episodes support learning and context.

Spatial memory is vital for mobile robots: cognitive maps, the internal representation of environments, support navigation, reference, and imagination. Cognitive maps in robots extend occupancy grids with objects, places, and topological relations: the lab is beside the corridor; the charger is in the corner.

Memory management faces real constraints. Storage is finite, retrieval must be fast, and relevance matters. Forgetting strategies, pruning outdated items, and indexing for fast retrieval are engineering concerns. Time stamps and confidence values let memory entries age and lose credibility gracefully.

Learning transforms experience into capability. Let me map the learning landscape for cognitive robots. Supervised learning, from labeled examples, powers perception: recognizing objects and faces. Unsupervised learning finds structure: clustering rooms by appearance. Reinforcement learning, from reward, improves decisions and control. Imitation learning, from demonstration, teaches skills by watching people.

Transfer learning lets knowledge in one setting help another: recognizing office objects helps in a home. Continual learning, learning over a lifetime without forgetting, is an active research frontier; catastrophic forgetting, where new learning erases old, is the classic problem.

Learning to learn and meta learning aim at faster adaptation: the robot that has learned many tasks picks up new ones with less experience. For household and service robots that must adapt to new homes and families, this matters greatly.

Skill learning composes motor knowledge: grasping styles, opening drawers, pouring liquids. Skills are learned from demonstration, refined by practice, and organized into libraries the planner can call.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian service robot remembers where the tea cups usually are, episodic memory of past locations, and semantic knowledge that cups live in the kitchen. A farm robot builds spatial memory of field zones and remembers which zones were treated. A student implements a simple episodic memory store logging events with time and place, then writes a query: what happened in the lab yesterday. An elderly care robot in Cape Town learns daily routines through observation and gently reminds about missed habits. Memory makes robots useful over time; learning makes them better at their jobs.

A practical habit: design memory with queries in mind. Before building memory structures, list the questions your robot must answer later, and shape storage to answer them quickly.

PART 5 - PLANNING: FROM SEARCH TO TASK AND MOTION

Let me walk you through planning, one of the crown jewels of artificial intelligence and the beating heart of cognitive robotics.

Planning is deciding on a sequence of actions to achieve goals. The planner takes the world model, the goal, and the available actions, and produces a plan: a recipe of steps from the current state to the goal state.

Classical planning uses formal models. The world is a set of states; actions have preconditions and effects; the planner searches the space of states for a path to the goal. The STRIPS formalism is the classic: each action lists what must be true before it and what becomes true after it. Planning algorithms search this space, often with heuristics that estimate distance to the goal and guide exploration.

Search algorithms are the engine. Breadth first search finds shortest plans in step count but explores widely. Depth first search dives deep but may find poor plans. Heuristic search like A star explores toward the goal using estimates and finds optimal plans when the heuristic is admissible. Planning tools like planners based on these ideas solve large problems efficiently.

Hierarchical planning tames complexity. Abstract tasks decompose into subtasks: make tea decomposes into boil water, warm teapot, add leaves, pour. Hierarchical task network planning performs this decomposition systematically, matching methods to tasks. Abstraction lets plans form at high level first, then fill in details, exactly how humans plan.

Task and motion planning bridges symbols and geometry. The symbolic plan says pick the cup and place it on the shelf. But the robot must also find a collision free arm trajectory, a feasible grasp, and a stable placement. Task and motion planning interleaves symbolic search with geometric feasibility checks, ensuring plans are executable by real bodies. This is one of the most important active research areas in robotics.

Planning under uncertainty responds to the real world. Classical plans assume deterministic outcomes; reality is noisy. Contingent planning branches on observations: if the drawer is open, take the tool; otherwise, open it first. Probabilistic planning with Markov decision processes optimizes expected reward over uncertain outcomes. Replanning strategies plan, execute, monitor, and replan when surprises occur. The sense plan act loop is now the plan monitor replan loop.

Behavior trees, popular in game AI and robotics, structure control logic as trees of tasks and conditions: sequences, fallbacks, and conditions compose readable, modular behaviors that blend reactivity and planning. Many modern robot executives use behavior trees.

Real life practicals. A hospital delivery robot plans routes and item pickups hierarchically: mission level tasks, then waypoints, then motion. A logistics robot uses task and motion planning to pick varied objects from cluttered shelves, checking grasp feasibility during planning. A student implements A star on a grid for a robot maze and then adds replanning when an obstacle appears. Plans are promises to the future; monitor them constantly and replan without shame.

A practical habit: validate plans against reality constraints early. Ask of every plan: is each step executable by this robot's body, in this world, with this time budget.

PART 6 - DECISION MAKING UNDER UNCERTAINTY

Let me walk you through decision making under uncertainty: how cognitive robots choose well when the world is noisy, ambiguous, and incomplete.

Real robots never have complete knowledge. Sensors are noisy, models are wrong, and the future is uncertain. Good decision making embraces this reality rather than pretending otherwise.

Probability is the language of uncertainty. Bayesian reasoning updates beliefs with evidence: prior belief times likelihood gives posterior belief. Belief states represent what the robot knows as probability distributions, not single facts. A robot tracking a person estimates a distribution over possible positions, narrowing with each observation.

Markov decision processes formalize sequential decisions with uncertainty and reward. States, actions, transition probabilities, and rewards define the problem; a policy prescribes the best action in each state; value functions measure long term expected reward. Value iteration and policy iteration compute optimal policies. Reinforcement learning, which you studied, learns policies from experience when models are unknown.

Partially observable problems extend this: the robot cannot see the true state directly, only noisy observations. Partially observable Markov decision processes model this hard but realistic case; solutions maintain belief states and plan over beliefs. Robotic tasks like finding objects or following people are naturally partially observable.

Risk and utility refine decisions. Not all outcomes are equal: failing safely may be better than succeeding dangerously. Utility functions capture preferences over outcomes; risk sensitive decision making avoids gambles with catastrophic downside. For medical and safety robots, risk awareness is ethical duty.

POMDP and reinforcement learning approaches combine with model predictive methods: rolling horizon optimization over predicted futures, replanning each step. Modern robots use learned world models to predict and plan.

Active perception and information gathering are decisions too: when uncertain, act to learn. Move the camera for a better view; ask the human to clarify; pick an object to inspect it. Value of information reasoning weighs whether gathering data is worth the cost.

Human aware decision making considers people as agents with intentions and preferences. Theory of mind, reasoning about what others believe and want, helps robots cooperate. A robot that infers a person intends to sit offers the chair; one that hears a question reasons about what the asker knows.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian logistics robot deciding which item to pick first under uncertain detection confidence uses expected utility considering error costs. A farm robot plans inspection routes that maximize information about crop disease, active perception for early warning. A service robot asks clarifying questions when uncertain between two similar requests. Decide with probabilities, weigh consequences, and gather information wisely.

A practical habit: when making a decision, list possible outcomes, their probabilities, and their costs and benefits. Even rough numbers beat intuition alone.

PART 7 - NATURAL HUMAN ROBOT INTERACTION

Let me walk you through human robot interaction: how cognitive robots communicate and cooperate with people naturally.

Cognitive robots live among people, so interaction is not an accessory; it is core. Natural interaction means communicating through the modalities people already use: language, gesture, gaze, and social cues.

Spoken language interaction. Robots understand speech through recognition, interpret meaning through natural language processing, and respond through speech synthesis. Beyond words, robots must handle reference and context: put it there requires resolving what it is and where there is. Dialogue management tracks conversation state, handles questions and corrections, and manages repair when understanding fails. For multilingual societies like Nigeria, support for English and local languages, code switching, and accents matters deeply.

Gesture and pointing. People point to refer to objects and space. Robots interpret pointing direction, integrate with vision to resolve targets, and use their own gestures to communicate: pointing to show, nodding to confirm. Gesture and language combine naturally: put the red one there.

Gaze and attention. Where a robot looks signals attention and intention. Shared attention, both robot and human attending to the same object, is foundational to cooperation. Robots that look at what they discuss feel more understandable and alive.

Social norms and proxemics. People stand at comfortable distances, approach from visible angles, and respect personal space. Robots that violate these norms feel threatening. Politeness strategies in language and motion make robots pleasant companions.

Explainability and transparency. Cognitive robots should explain their decisions in human terms: I stopped because a person crossed my path. Explanations build trust and help users correct errors. Trust calibration, users trusting appropriately neither too much nor too little, is the goal.

Interaction design methodology. Study users in context. Prototype interactions early. Test with real people, observing confusion and delight. Iterate. Wizard of Oz studies, where a human secretly controls the robot, explore interaction concepts before full automation.

Safety in interaction is paramount. Force limits, speed limits near people, predictable motions, and reliable emergency stops protect users. Standards for collaborative robots define safe forces and speeds.

Real life practicals. A hospital robot in Nigeria announces intentions politely as it moves through wards: passing behind you, please. A service robot interprets a pointing gesture from a nurse to deliver supplies to the indicated shelf. A student team tests dialogue with real users and discovers their robot's confirmation questions annoy; they redesign to confirm only when confidence is low. Design interaction as carefully as algorithms; the best algorithm fails if the interaction feels wrong.

A practical habit: watch real users interact with your robot and write down every moment of confusion. Each confusion is a design requirement for the next iteration.

PART 8 - LEARNING FROM DEMONSTRATION AND EXPERIENCE

Let me walk you through how cognitive robots learn skills and behaviors from people and from practice.

Learning from demonstration, also called imitation learning, teaches robots by showing rather than programming. A human demonstrates a task; the robot extracts the skill. This aligns with how people teach each other and lowers the programming barrier dramatically.

Demonstrations are collected through teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching where you move the robot arm by hand, or observation of human video. Each method has trade offs in data quality and effort. Kinesthetic teaching is intuitive for manipulation; teleoperation suits mobile robots; video observation is unobtrusive but hard to map to robot bodies.

From demonstrations, robots learn policies or skills. Behavior cloning learns a mapping from states to actions directly. Inverse reinforcement learning infers the reward the demonstrator was optimizing, capturing intent rather than just motion. Movement primitives encode skills as parameterized curves: a reaching motion with adjustable targets and speeds. Dynamic movement primitives add stability and flexibility, letting skills adapt to new targets.

Learning from experience refines and extends. Reinforcement learning improves policies through trial and reward. Robots practice grasping thousands of times in simulation and refine on hardware. Exploration balances trying new strategies with exploiting known good ones.

Skill libraries organize learned abilities: grasps, pours, opens, wipes. Planners compose skills into task solutions. Transfer learning moves skills across robots and settings; domain adaptation handles differences between simulation and reality or between kitchens.

Human feedback guides learning: corrections, preferences, and ratings. Interactive learning lets people shape behavior efficiently: thumbs up and thumbs down to reinforce good and discourage bad actions. Preference learning from comparisons is a practical way to teach nuanced behavior.

Lifelong learning is the vision: robots that accumulate skills over years, never stop improving, and avoid forgetting. Challenges include distribution shift as environments change, safety during learning, and knowing when to ask for help instead of guessing.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian robotics lab teaches a robot arm to sort tomatoes by kinesthetic demonstration, then refines grasps through reinforcement learning in simulation. A service robot learns household routines through demonstration and gentle correction from family members. An agricultural robot learns to identify ripe produce from farmer demonstrations and improves with field experience. Teach robots as you would teach a bright apprentice: show, correct, praise progress, and let practice perfect the skill.

A practical habit: evaluate learned skills against clear success metrics before deployment, and keep humans in the loop during early learning, with override always available.

PART 9 - COGNITIVE ARCHITECTURES, APPLICATIONS, AND ETHICS

Let me tour integrated cognitive architectures, application domains, and the ethical questions that accompany machine minds.

Cognitive architectures integrate the pieces we have studied. Soar is a classic architecture with working memory, long term memories, and decision procedures, used in agents and training systems. ACT R models human cognition with modules for perception, memory, and action. CLARION combines implicit and explicit knowledge. Modern robotic systems often use custom three layer architectures with behavior tree executives, probabilistic world models, and learned perception, integrated through middleware like ROS.

These architectures share elements: perception updating a world model, goals driving planning, memory supporting reasoning, learning improving over time, and interaction modules handling people. Integration is the grand challenge: making the parts work together robustly is harder than any part alone.

Applications of cognitive robotics are expanding rapidly. Service robots in homes and offices fetch, clean, and assist. Healthcare robots support surgery, rehabilitation, elderly care, and hospital logistics. Education robots tutor and engage students. Agricultural robots monitor and tend crops with judgment. Retail and hospitality robots greet, guide, and serve. Field robots explore and inspect in dangerous places. Each domain rewards perception, memory, reasoning, and natural interaction.

African contexts offer compelling opportunities. In healthcare, cognitive service robots can extend limited staff in hospitals, delivering supplies and guiding visitors. In education, tutoring robots can reach schools lacking specialist teachers, adapting to each learner. In agriculture, judgment enabled robots help smallholder farms with precise, timely interventions. In commerce, service robots in banks and malls assist customers. Design for local languages, power realities, and cultural norms of politeness and respect.

Ethics of cognitive robots deserves deep reflection. Autonomy and accountability: when a thinking robot errs, who is responsible, the maker, the owner, or the machine. Transparency: people deserve to understand why a robot acted. Privacy: cognitive robots perceive homes and people intimately; data protection is sacred. Manipulation: robots that understand human psychology could influence unduly; restraint is required. Human dignity: care robots must serve human dignity, not replace human connection. And fairness: cognitive robots must not encode or amplify social biases. Responsible cognitive robotics engineers treat these questions as design requirements, not afterthoughts.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian hospital pilot of a cognitive delivery robot publishes clear accountability policies: the hospital operates it, the vendor maintains it, staff can stop it anytime. An education robot project conducts privacy impact assessments before classroom deployment. A student team adds explanation capability to their robot: it states its reasons in simple words. Build minds with conscience.

PART 10 - FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND COURSE COMPLETION

Let me close with the future of cognitive robotics and a synthesis of your learning.

The future is converging and accelerating. Foundation models, large neural networks trained on broad data, are entering robotics as general perception and reasoning engines, enabling robots to understand scenes and instructions with unprecedented breadth. Language models allow robots to be programmed and commanded in natural language, dramatically lowering barriers. World models let robots imagine and predict, planning in learned simulations of reality. Neuro symbolic systems combine the learning of networks with the reasoning of symbols, promising both competence and explainability.

Embodied intelligence will advance as robots learn from interaction with the physical world at scale, through simulation, teleoperation data, and deployment. Home robots will gradually become more capable and affordable. Medical and care robotics will grow with aging populations. Agricultural and environmental robotics will support sustainability.

Open problems remain profound. Common sense understanding is incomplete. Long term autonomy in unstructured human environments is unsolved. Trustworthy and verifiable cognitive behavior is a research frontier. Efficient learning from few examples continues to challenge. And the integration of all cognitive skills into one robust system remains the grand challenge.

The societal vision is worth contemplating. Cognitive robots as patient assistants in hospitals, tireless helpers on farms, engaging tutors in schools, and respectful companions in homes. For Nigeria and Africa, cognitive robotics offers paths to leapfrog infrastructure gaps in healthcare, education, and agriculture through intelligent machines designed for local realities.

Glossary of Key Terms

Cognitive robotics: robots with perception, reasoning, learning, and interaction abilities. Cognitive architecture: blueprint organizing components of a thinking system. Sense plan act: architecture sensing, planning fully, then executing. Behavior based architecture: parallel behaviors producing robot actions. Subsumption architecture: layered behaviors where higher levels subsume lower. Three layer architecture: reactive, executive, and deliberative layers. Knowledge representation: encoding facts for computational reasoning. Ontology: formal shared vocabulary of a domain. Semantic network: graph of concepts and relations. Rule based system: knowledge as if then rules with inference engine. Forward chaining: reasoning from facts to conclusions. Backward chaining: reasoning from goals to needed facts. Bayesian network: probabilistic graphical model of dependencies. Common sense knowledge: vast background understanding humans share. Working memory: memory holding current situation. Episodic memory: memory of specific past experiences. Semantic memory: memory of general facts and concepts. Cognitive map: internal representation of spatial environment. Catastrophic forgetting: new learning erasing old knowledge. Transfer learning: applying knowledge from one setting to another. Classical planning: search over states with action models. STRIPS: classic action representation with preconditions and effects. Heuristic search: search guided by estimates to the goal. Hierarchical task network: planning by decomposing tasks into subtasks. Task and motion planning: combining symbolic plans with geometric feasibility. Behavior tree: tree structured control logic of tasks and conditions. Markov decision process: sequential decision model with uncertainty and reward. POMDP: decision model with partial observability. Policy: strategy prescribing actions in each state. Value function: expected long term reward from a state. Risk sensitivity: decision making accounting for downside risk. Theory of mind: reasoning about others beliefs and intentions. Shared attention: joint focus of human and robot on the same object. Proxemics: social norms of physical distance. Explainability: ability of a system to give reasons for decisions. Learning from demonstration: learning skills from human examples. Behavior cloning: learning state to action mappings from demonstrations. Movement primitives: parameterized reusable motor skills. Inverse reinforcement learning: inferring rewards behind observed behavior. Skill library: organized collection of learned abilities. Interactive learning: learning guided by human feedback.
