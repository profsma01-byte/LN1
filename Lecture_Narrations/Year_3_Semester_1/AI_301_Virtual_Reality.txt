PART 1 - WELCOME TO VIRTUAL REALITY

Hello everyone, and welcome to AI 301, Virtual Reality. I am delighted that you have reached Year Three. This is the year where your work starts to feel truly immersive and professional, and virtual reality is one of the most exciting subjects you will study. In this course you will learn how computer generated worlds are created, how they are displayed to human senses, and how people interact with them naturally. You will study the whole VR pipeline: geometry and rendering, tracking and input, human perception, interaction design, and practical development with modern tools. And as always in this programme, we will ground everything in real life practicals and examples from Nigeria and Africa.

What is virtual reality? Virtual reality, usually shortened to VR, is a computer generated environment that a person can experience and interact with in a way that feels real. Instead of looking at a flat screen from the outside, you are placed inside a synthetic world. When you turn your head, the world updates as if you were really standing there. When you reach out your hand, a virtual hand reaches out too. When you walk forward, the environment comes closer. The aim is presence: the psychological feeling of truly being there.

A simple definition to remember is this: virtual reality replaces the real world with a computer generated one, delivered through immersive displays and natural interaction. Augmented reality, its cousin, overlays computer graphics onto the real world rather than replacing it, and mixed reality blends the two so virtual objects appear to belong in the real room. In this course we focus on VR while appreciating these related fields.

Let me share some real life practicals to show why this matters. Surgeons rehearse complex operations on virtual patients before touching a real one. Architects walk clients through virtual buildings that have not yet been built. Engineers train on virtual oil platforms where practicing a dangerous procedure in real life would be too risky. In Nigeria, a medical student at the University of Lagos can practice anatomy in VR, exploring the human heart in three dimensions. An architect in Abuja can show a client a full walkthrough of a proposed estate. A safety trainer in Port Harcourt can rehearse oil spill response in a virtual simulation. These are not science fiction; they are working applications built with the principles you will learn this semester.

VR is deeply interdisciplinary. Computer graphics gives you the visual world. Signal processing and sensor fusion handle tracking data. Neural networks help with rendering optimization and gaze prediction. Control theory appears in motion and stabilization. Ethics and psychology matter because VR experiences strongly affect the human mind. Your whole robotics and artificial intelligence training converges here, in the craft of building convincing synthetic worlds.

How will you study this course? We will begin with the history and concepts of VR, the human perceptual system, and the hardware of headsets and displays. We will then study three dimensional geometry, rendering, and virtual world construction. We will examine tracking, input, and interaction, the techniques that make a user feel present and in control. We will study presence, cybersickness, and human factors, because a VR system that makes users ill has failed. We will practice building VR applications with game engines and toolkits. We will explore serious applications in medicine, education, engineering, and cultural heritage. And we will end with future directions and honest reflections on the promise and limits of the technology.

To succeed in VR you need three qualities. Curiosity, because the field is new and evolving fast. Care, because experiences must be designed responsibly. And craftsmanship, because a convincing VR world demands many details done well. Bring these qualities to every lecture and every practical, and you will build worlds that people remember.

Let us begin our journey into virtual reality.

PART 2 - HISTORY AND CONCEPTS OF VIRTUAL REALITY

Let me tell you the story of virtual reality, because understanding its history helps you see why the field is the way it is. The dream of artificial worlds is old. In the nineteen fifties and sixties, pioneers built the first sensory displays. Morton Heilig designed the Sensorama in the nineteen sixties, a booth that combined a three dimensional screen, stereo sound, vibrations, wind, and scent to immerse a viewer in a film of a motorcycle ride. It was never mass produced, but the vision was pure virtual reality.

In nineteen sixty eight, Ivan Sutherland, often called the father of computer graphics, built the Sword of Damocles, widely considered the first head mounted display. It was so heavy it had to be suspended from the ceiling. It showed simple wireframe graphics that updated as the user moved their head. From this moment, the core idea of VR was established: a head worn display with head tracking.

Through the nineteen seventies and eighties, simulation industries advanced the technology for pilots and military training. Flight simulators are among the oldest successful VR-like systems. In the late nineteen eighties and nineties, Jaron Lanier popularized the term virtual reality, and companies built gloves, suits, and headsets for a first wave of consumer excitement. That wave crashed, because the hardware was expensive, the graphics crude, and the dream oversold.

The modern era began around twenty twelve, when Palmer Luckey launched a Kickstarter for the Oculus Rift headset, promising wide field of view and low latency tracking at an affordable price. Facebook acquired Oculus in twenty fourteen, signaling mainstream interest. Since then, headsets from HTC, Sony, Valve, and others have brought high quality VR into homes and businesses. Standalone headsets like the Meta Quest series removed the need for cables and external computers, and VR has become a practical tool for training, design, therapy, and play.

The key concepts from this history are important. First, immersion. Immersion is a property of the technology: how completely the display replaces reality through wide field of view, high resolution, stereoscopy, head tracking, and spatial sound. A system with more of these features is more immersive. Second, presence. Presence is the human response to immersion: the feeling of being in the virtual place rather than in the lab wearing a helmet. You can have immersion without presence if the world is boring or broken, but strong presence requires immersion.

Third, the VR system is a loop. The computer generates a scene from a viewpoint. The user moves. Sensors track the movement. The computer updates the scene accordingly. The display shows the new scene. This loop must run fast and smoothly. If the delay between your movement and the screen update grows too long, the illusion breaks and you feel ill. Latency is therefore a central engineering concern.

Fourth, VR is more than vision. Sound, touch, and even smell can contribute. Spatial audio that places sounds around you in three dimensions is a powerful presence booster. Hand tracking and controllers extend the experience from watching to doing.

Real life practicals make these concepts concrete. Consider a virtual reality lab in Kano used to train welders. The headset provides immersion through its display and tracking. The students feel presence as they stand in a virtual workshop. The system runs a continuous loop as they move their head to inspect welds. Sound effects of the welding torch reinforce the illusion. The result is training that is safe, repeatable, and accessible.

VR is often compared to film and games, but it differs in two ways. First, agency: in VR you act, you do not merely watch. Second, embodiment: you have a virtual body or presence in the world. Designing for agency and embodiment is the special challenge of this field.

PART 3 - HUMAN PERCEPTION AND PRESENCE

To build convincing virtual worlds, you must understand the human perceptual system, because VR ultimately happens in the brain, not in the headset. Let me walk you through the senses that matter most.

Vision dominates VR. Your two eyes see slightly different images, a difference called binocular disparity, and your brain uses it to perceive depth. Stereoscopic displays show a separate image to each eye to reproduce this cue. Another depth cue is motion parallax: when you move your head, nearer objects shift more than distant ones. Head tracking provides this cue by updating the scene as you move. Other cues include perspective, occlusion, relative size, and shading. A good VR system delivers as many of these cues as possible.

Field of view matters enormously. Human peripheral vision spans roughly two hundred degrees horizontally. Many headsets offer around one hundred degrees. The wider the field of view, the stronger the sense of being enclosed in a world. Resolution matters too: if you can see individual pixels, called the screen door effect, the illusion weakens. Modern high resolution displays have reduced this problem dramatically.

Focus is an interesting frontier. In reality, your eyes focus on near objects differently than far ones, a process called accommodation. Most current headsets show a fixed focus distance, which can cause strain and slightly reduce realism. Research on varifocal displays aims to solve this.

Hearing is the second pillar of presence. Humans localize sound using timing and volume differences between the ears and the shape of the outer ear. Spatial audio systems compute these cues and deliver sound that seems to come from a specific point in space. A creaking door behind you in VR feels wonderfully real when the audio is spatialized well.

Touch, called haptics, adds further realism. Controllers vibrate when you touch virtual objects. Advanced gloves apply forces to your fingers. Even simple vibration patterns, properly timed, significantly increase presence.

Presence itself is the psychological state of being in the virtual environment. Researchers describe presence as composed of spatial presence, the sense of being in a place, and social presence, the sense of being with other beings. Presence is influenced by immersion, by the plausibility of the world, by your ability to interact, and by individual psychology.

Cybersickness is the shadow side of VR. Some users feel nausea, dizziness, or eye strain. The dominant theory is sensory conflict: your eyes report motion but your inner ear reports stillness, or vice versa. The cure is engineering discipline: keep latency low, track head motion accurately, avoid unexpected acceleration of the viewpoint, provide a stable frame rate, and give users control over their motion.

Real life practicals for perception are everywhere. A VR therapy clinic in Ibadan uses spatial audio to create calming environments for anxiety treatment; the sound design is as important as the visuals. A training company designs courses with careful frame rate targets to keep cybersickness near zero. An educator in Nairobi limits VR sessions to fifteen minutes for young children to prevent strain. Understanding perception turns VR development from guesswork into applied science.

PART 4 - VR HARDWARE: HEADSETS, DISPLAYS, AND TRACKING

Let me open the headset and look at the hardware that makes virtual reality possible. The main components are the display, the optics, the tracking system, the compute unit, and the input devices.

The display shows the images. Two common technologies are LCD and OLED. LCD screens are bright and affordable. OLED screens offer perfect blacks and fast response, valuable for dark scenes and reducing motion blur. The display must refresh at high rates, ideally ninety hertz or more, to keep motion smooth and reduce sickness.

The optics sit between the display and your eyes. Lenses magnify the small screens to fill your field of view and focus the image so your eyes can see it clearly. Most headsets use simple magnifying lenses, which cause some distortion at the edges. The software corrects this distortion by pre-warping the rendered image, so the image you perceive is geometrically correct. Fresnel lenses, thin and light, are common but can cause ring artifacts. Pancake lenses, newer and more compact, fold the light path to make headsets slimmer.

Tracking tells the system where your head is and where it is looking. There are two families of tracking. Outside-in tracking uses external base stations or cameras placed in the room to observe markers or features on the headset. Inside-out tracking uses cameras and sensors mounted on the headset itself to observe the room and compute motion from that data. Modern standalone headsets favor inside-out tracking because it needs no external setup.

Tracking has six degrees of freedom: three for position, forward and back, left and right, up and down, and three for rotation, pitch, yaw, and roll. Six degree of freedom tracking is essential for room scale VR where you can walk around. Three degree of freedom tracking, rotation only, is cheaper and suits seated experiences like watching three hundred and sixty degree video.

Latency in tracking is critical. Motion to photon latency, the delay from your movement to the updated photon leaving the display, should be under twenty milliseconds and ideally closer to ten. Prediction algorithms estimate your head pose slightly into the future to compensate for rendering delays.

The compute unit renders the world. It can be a powerful gaming PC, a console, or the mobile chip inside a standalone headset. PC connected headsets offer the best graphics but tether the user. Standalone headsets offer freedom and easy setup with modest graphics. The trade off between quality and convenience is a key design decision.

Input devices include handheld controllers with buttons and thumbsticks, tracked in space so you can point, grab, and throw. Hand tracking uses cameras to track your bare hands. Gloves and suits provide haptics. Eye tracking, increasingly built into headsets, measures where you look, enabling foveated rendering, a technique that renders sharply only where you are looking, saving enormous computation.

Real life practicals. A training center in Lagos buys standalone headsets for a mobile skills lab that travels to rural communities; inside out tracking and no cables make setup simple in any classroom. A design studio in Cape Town uses eye tracking to study how customers view products in VR. A hospital pilots haptic gloves for surgical rehearsal. Hardware choices shape what experiences are possible and affordable; choose with the user and context in mind.

PART 5 - THREE DIMENSIONAL GEOMETRY AND RENDERING

Now let us study the core computer science of VR: three dimensional geometry and rendering. This is where your mathematics from earlier courses pays off handsomely.

A virtual world is made of geometry. Objects are described by meshes, collections of vertices connected into triangles. Each vertex has a position in three dimensional space, and usually a normal vector describing which way its surface faces, and texture coordinates telling how images wrap onto it. A complex character might have hundreds of thousands of triangles; a simple chair might have a few dozen.

Coordinate systems frame everything. There is a world coordinate system for the whole scene, object local systems for each object, and the camera or eye coordinate system for the viewer. Transformations move points between these systems: translation moves, rotation turns, and scaling resizes. As you learned in graphics and kinematics, matrices compose these transformations efficiently. In VR there are two eyes, so you render the scene twice per frame, once from the left eye position and once from the right, separated by roughly the interpupillary distance.

Rendering is the process of computing the image. The standard approach is rasterization, which projects triangles onto the screen and fills the pixels they cover, shading each pixel with color and lighting. A rendering pipeline has stages: transform vertices, clip what is outside the view, rasterize triangles into pixels, shade pixels with lighting and materials, and write the result to the framebuffer.

Lighting brings scenes to life. Simple models compute how light reflects off surfaces based on the light direction and the surface normal. Ambient light is the general background light. Diffuse reflection scatters light in many directions, giving matte surfaces. Specular reflection creates shiny highlights. Shadows add grounding, making objects feel solidly placed. Modern techniques like physically based rendering approximate real physics of light for greater realism.

Virtual reality rendering has special demands. First, you must render for two eyes every frame, doubling the cost. Second, the frame rate must be high and steady; ninety frames per second is a common target, meaning eleven milliseconds per frame. Third, you must apply lens distortion correction per eye. Fourth, techniques like foveated rendering reduce cost by rendering peripheral vision at lower quality. Fifth, stereo rendering must be carefully calibrated so depth perception is comfortable.

Texture mapping applies images to surfaces for detail without more geometry. Normal mapping fakes small surface bumps by manipulating how light reflects. These techniques enrich scenes cheaply.

Real life practicals. A VR team building a virtual museum in Accra models artifacts as meshes, photographs them for textures, lights the galleries warmly, and optimizes for ninety frames per second on standalone headsets. A student building a virtual chemistry lab uses simple flat shaded geometry first to validate interaction, then adds lighting and textures once the experience works. Optimization is the practical craft: reduce polygon counts, reuse textures, and measure frame times constantly.

PART 6 - TRACKING, INPUT, AND INTERACTION DESIGN

Let me walk you through tracking and interaction, the art of making users feel present and in control.

Tracking determines where the user and their devices are in space. Beyond head tracking, systems track hands and controllers. Optical tracking uses cameras to observe markers or natural features. Inertial measurement units, combining accelerometers and gyroscopes, measure motion directly and are fused with optical data for robustness. Sensor fusion combines these signals, playing to the strengths of each sensor while compensating for weaknesses, exactly the principle you studied in robotic perception.

Inside out tracking on modern headsets maps the room using simultaneous localization and mapping ideas from robotics. The headset builds a sparse map of feature points and tracks its position within it. This is why headset tracking improves as you walk around a well textured room and struggles in empty white spaces.

Interaction design is how users act within the virtual world. The golden rule is natural interaction: the closer the mapping between your real action and the virtual response, the stronger the presence. Grabbing a virtual cup by closing your tracked hand around it feels natural. Pressing a button labeled grab does not.

Common interaction patterns include teleportation for moving across large spaces, avoiding the sickness of smooth artificial motion; ray casting to point at distant objects like a laser; direct manipulation of objects you can reach; and virtual hands that open and close with finger tracking or controller triggers.

Affordances guide users. A virtual door handle that looks graspable invites grasping. Visual feedback confirms actions: highlight objects on hover, animate them on grab, and provide audio cues for contact. Without feedback, users feel lost.

Input hardware evolves quickly. Hand tracking frees users from controllers but offers less precision and no physical buttons. Controllers offer precision and haptics. Combining both, switching between them contextually, often gives the best experience. Voice input adds hands free commands.

Latency in interaction is felt immediately. If I grab and the virtual hand lags, my brain rejects the illusion. Keep interaction response within a few tens of milliseconds.

Real life practicals. A VR training application for technicians in Enugu uses direct manipulation: trainees unscrew virtual bolts with hand tracking, each action confirmed by sound and highlight. A museum experience in Dakar uses teleportation so visitors explore galleries without motion sickness. A language learning app in Ghana uses voice input so students practice pronunciation hands free. Test every interaction with real users early and often; presence is confirmed only in experience, never in theory.

PART 7 - BUILDING VR APPLICATIONS: ENGINES, TOOLS, AND WORKFLOW

Let me show you how virtual reality applications are actually built in practice, because knowing concepts is not enough; you must be able to make things.

The heart of modern VR development is the game engine, software for three dimensional graphics, physics, input, and audio. The two dominant engines are Unity and Unreal Engine. Unity uses the C sharp language and is popular for its accessibility and huge asset store. Unreal uses C plus plus and visual scripting, and is famed for high end graphics. Both support major VR headsets and both are free to start with. Choose one and learn it deeply; the concepts transfer between them.

A typical VR development workflow has stages. First, define the experience goal: what should the user feel, learn, or accomplish. Second, prototype quickly with simple geometry and interactions. Do not start by modeling beautiful assets; prove the core loop first. Third, iterate with user testing, observing where people struggle and what delights them. Fourth, polish visuals, audio, and performance. Fifth, profile and optimize until frame rates are stable. Sixth, deploy to target hardware and test in the real environment where it will be used.

Software development kits and frameworks help. OpenXR is an open standard that lets your application run on many headsets with one code base. WebXR brings VR to browsers, lowering access barriers dramatically. Middleware handles hand tracking, avatars, and spatial audio.

The performance budget is a practical discipline. Decide your frame rate target, measure your frame times, and divide the budget among rendering, physics, logic, and audio. Profiling tools show where time is spent. Common optimizations include reducing polygon counts, simplifying shaders, culling objects out of view, baking lighting in advance, and using level of detail to show simpler models at a distance.

Asset creation is another craft. Models come from three dimensional modeling tools like Blender, a powerful free option. Photogrammetry converts photographs of real objects into three dimensional models, ideal for cultural heritage artifacts and realistic environments. Textures and materials make surfaces convincing.

Testing VR is different from testing ordinary software. You must test comfort: do users feel ill. You must test usability: can they figure out controls without help. You must test presence: do they feel there. And you must test performance: is the frame rate steady in the worst case.

Real life practicals. A student team in Ibadan builds a VR physics lab with Unity and OpenXR, prototyping interaction with simple cubes before modeling lab equipment. A media startup scans traditional Nigerian sculptures with photogrammetry into a virtual gallery built with Unreal. A training company standardizes on WebXR so their courses run on any headset through a browser. Start small, test early, and optimize constantly.

PART 8 - SERIOUS APPLICATIONS: MEDICINE, EDUCATION, AND ENGINEERING

Let me tour the serious applications of virtual reality, where the technology saves money, reduces risk, and improves lives.

Medicine and therapy are leading fields. Surgical simulation lets trainees practice procedures repeatedly on virtual anatomy with haptic feedback. Preoperative planning allows surgeons to explore a specific patient's scanned anatomy in VR before operating. Therapy applications treat phobias through controlled exposure to virtual triggers, treat post traumatic stress with guided scenarios, and help stroke patients regain movement through engaging virtual exercises. The clinical evidence is growing steadily.

Education transforms in VR. Abstract subjects become tangible: students walk inside a human cell, stand on the surface of Mars, or manipulate molecules with their hands. Laboratory training becomes accessible: schools without expensive equipment can simulate experiments. Language and culture learning benefits from virtual immersion in realistic settings.

Engineering and design embrace VR for visualization and collaboration. Architects walk clients through buildings before construction, catching design problems while changes are cheap. Automotive designers review full scale virtual models of cars. Engineers conduct virtual maintenance training on complex machinery. Distributed teams meet in shared virtual spaces to review three dimensional designs together.

Cultural heritage finds new life in VR. Endangered sites are preserved as virtual experiences. Museums offer virtual visits to global audiences. Traditional crafts and performances are recorded in three dimensions for future generations.

Training for dangerous situations is a natural fit. Firefighters rehearse building evacuations. Offshore oil crews practice emergency shutdowns. Military and peacekeeping personnel train for complex scenarios. In every case, VR lets people make mistakes safely and learn deeply.

Real life practicals from Africa are inspiring. In Nigeria, medical schools pilot VR anatomy to stretch limited cadaver resources. A Lagos architecture firm reports clients approving designs faster after VR walkthroughs, reducing costly revisions. In Kenya, wildlife rangers train for anti poaching scenarios in VR. In South Africa, mining safety training uses VR to rehearse emergency procedures. In Senegal, a virtual museum preserves heritage sites for schools worldwide. Notice how these applications map directly to your course modules: rendering makes the visuals, tracking makes the interaction, perception science makes them comfortable, and the workflow skills make them shippable.

To design any serious VR application, ask three questions. What is the learning or performance goal. Why is VR better than cheaper alternatives like video or desktop simulation. And how will you measure success. VR is powerful but expensive; justify it with clear value.

PART 9 - PYTHON AND PRACTICAL LABORATORY WORK

Let me ground this course in hands on laboratory work, because virtual reality is learned by building. I will describe a practical sequence you can follow with a modern headset or even on a desktop simulator before you have hardware.

Lab one: orientation and safety. Install Unity or Unreal, create a blank project, and run the built in VR sample scene on your headset or simulator. Learn the comfort guidelines: stable frame rate, no unexpected viewpoint motion, clear interaction feedback.

Lab two: geometry and rendering. Build a small scene: a room, a table, and a few colored cubes. Modify materials and lighting. Measure your frame rate as you add objects. Learn to profile.

Lab three: tracking and input. Add VR controllers to pick up and move the cubes. Implement grab, highlight on hover, and release with a throw. Feel how feedback changes the experience.

Lab four: teleportation and movement. Implement teleport locomotion with a visible arc and landing marker. Compare with smooth joystick motion and observe comfort differences among your classmates.

Lab five: spatial audio. Add sounds to objects: a cube that clicks when grabbed, ambient room tone. Use the engine spatial audio features and observe how sound increases presence.

Lab six: photogrammetry. Photograph an object from many angles and convert it into a three dimensional model using free photogrammetry software. Import it into your VR scene.

Lab seven: a serious application prototype. Choose a training or education goal, such as a virtual fire extinguisher trainer or a virtual chemistry mixing lab, and build a simple but complete prototype. Test it with two real users and write down what you observed.

Lab eight: performance and comfort tuning. Stress test your worst scene, measure frame times, and apply optimizations until the frame rate is stable. Have a classmate test for comfort and record their feedback.

For students without headsets, much can be learned with desktop simulation: run the scene in the editor game view, test interactions with mouse and keyboard mappings, and study code and documentation deeply. When headset time is available, prioritize labs three and seven.

Reflection exercise. After each lab, write three sentences: what you built, what confused you, and what you would improve. This reflective habit converts experience into skill.

PART 10 - FUTURE DIRECTIONS, ETHICS, AND COURSE COMPLETION

Let me close by looking forward honestly and completely.

The future of virtual reality is being written now. Hardware trends point toward lighter and more comfortable headsets, higher resolution displays, wider fields of view, varifocal optics for natural focus, and built in eye and face tracking for expressive avatars. Input will move toward bare hand tracking and more sophisticated haptics. Wireless streaming will blend the power of PCs with the freedom of standalone devices.

Software trends include better creation tools, shared persistent virtual worlds for collaboration, and artificial intelligence that generates environments, characters, and behaviors automatically. Imagine describing a training scenario in words and having the VR world generated for you; early versions of this are already appearing.

Ethics deserves serious attention. Privacy: headsets with cameras and eye tracking collect intimate data about your body, your gaze, and your home; this data must be protected fiercely. Psychological safety: VR experiences can be emotionally powerful and potentially manipulative; designers bear responsibility for user wellbeing. Accessibility: VR must be designed for people with disabilities, with options for seated use, subtitles, and alternative interactions. Addiction and overuse: immersive worlds can be compelling to a harmful degree. And equity: VR should serve education and development broadly, not only entertainment for the wealthy.

For Nigeria and Africa, VR is an opportunity with local character. Imagine virtual laboratories reaching schools across the country, from Lagos to Maiduguri, sharing scarce science equipment through simulation. Imagine cultural heritage sites experienced by the global diaspora. Imagine medical and technical training scaled dramatically through immersive simulation. The engineers who build these systems are you.

You learned the hardware of displays, optics, tracking, and input. You mastered three dimensional geometry and rendering pipelines. You explored tracking and natural interaction design. You practiced building applications with engines and tools. You toured serious applications in medicine, education, and engineering. And you considered the future and ethics of the field honestly.

Final reflections. Virtual reality is the art and science of synthetic experience. It asks not only can the computer draw a world, but can a human being believe in it, feel at home in it, and grow through it. Build VR with technical excellence, human kindness, and creative joy, and you will open new worlds for people.

Virtual reality replaces the flat screen with a living world around the user. Master it, and you can build experiences that teach, heal, and inspire. Thank you for your attention and your effort. I will see you in the next lecture.

Glossary of Key Terms

Virtual reality: computer generated environment experienced through immersive displays and interaction. Immersion: technical property of how completely a display replaces reality. Presence: psychological feeling of being in the virtual place. Head mounted display: headset worn on the head showing stereoscopic images. Field of view: the angular extent of the visible world through the headset. Stereoscopy: presenting separate images to each eye for depth perception. Motion parallax: depth cue from movement causing nearer objects to shift more. Spatial audio: sound rendered to appear from positions in three dimensional space. Haptics: touch feedback through vibration or force. Cybersickness: nausea or discomfort caused by VR, often from sensory conflict. Six degrees of freedom: tracking of three positional and three rotational axes. Inside out tracking: headset tracks itself using on board cameras. Outside in tracking: external sensors track the headset. Latency: delay between user motion and system response. Motion to photon latency: total delay from movement to updated light from display. Rasterization: rendering technique converting triangles to screen pixels. Mesh: collection of vertices and triangles describing three dimensional shape. Texture mapping: applying images to surfaces for detail. Physically based rendering: lighting model approximating real light physics. Foveated rendering: high quality rendering only where the eye looks. OpenXR: open standard for cross platform VR development. Game engine: software framework for building interactive three dimensional applications. Photogrammetry: creating three dimensional models from photographs. Teleportation: locomotion method jumping the user across space. Ray casting: pointing interaction projecting a virtual ray. Affordance: design feature suggesting how an object can be used. Sensor fusion: combining multiple sensor data for better estimates. Avatar: virtual representation of a user. Varifocal display: display that adjusts focus distance for realism. WebXR: VR and AR experiences delivered through web browsers.
