PART 1 - WELCOME TO ROBOT LOCALIZATION AND MAPPING

Hello everyone, and welcome to AI 310, Robot Localization and Mapping. This is one of the most important and beautiful subjects in robotics, because before a robot can act intelligently in the world, it must know where it is, and before it can navigate, it must know the world. Localization is the problem of determining where the robot is. Mapping is the problem of building a representation of the environment. Together, they form the foundation of autonomous navigation, and their combination, simultaneous localization and mapping, is one of the crown achievements of modern robotics. I am delighted to guide you through this material, and as always we will ground everything in real life practicals and examples from Nigeria and Africa.

Let me define the problems clearly. Localization: given a map and sensor observations, estimate the robot's position and orientation, its pose. Mapping: given the robot's poses and sensor observations, build a map of the environment. Simultaneous localization and mapping, SLAM: the robot does both at once, building a map while localizing within it, when neither a map nor external positioning is available. These problems sound simple but are deep, involving probability, geometry, estimation, and algorithm design at their best.

Why is this so fundamental? Imagine a delivery robot in a Lagos office building. It must know its position in the building to navigate to the fifth floor meeting room. It must know where walls, doors, and elevators are to plan routes. If it does not know where it is, every other capability is useless. A robot arm needs to know the positions of parts to grasp them. An autonomous vehicle needs to know its lane position precisely. A drone needs its position to follow a route. Localization and mapping are the ground truth of robotic action.

The challenges are real. Robots have noisy sensors: wheel odometry drifts, GPS is unavailable indoors, cameras see textureless walls, and lidar gets confused in glass corridors. The world is ambiguous: corridors look alike, rooms repeat, and dynamic objects, people and doors, move and confuse maps. Computation is limited: estimates must be real time. And errors compound: small pose errors grow into wrong maps and wrong plans.

Real life practicals make the stakes concrete. A service robot at a teaching hospital in Ibadan uses a pre built map and localizes with laser scans to deliver medicines reliably. A warehouse fleet maps its floor once and shares the map across robots. An agricultural robot maps a field to know where it has sprayed. A drone maps a construction site in Abuja to monitor progress. Each of these is a system you will understand how to design by the end of this course.

Localization and mapping connect to your whole education. Probability and Bayesian reasoning are the language of estimation. Kalman filtering, from perception and control, is a core tool. Graph theory models SLAM as optimization on graphs. Neural networks increasingly serve place recognition and learned features. Control uses pose estimates in feedback. Multi robot systems share maps. And ethics matters: maps of private spaces and tracking of robots and people raise privacy questions.

How will we study this course? We will start with robot pose and coordinate frames. We will study dead reckoning and odometry and their drift. We will cover sensors for localization: odometry, inertial, GPS, laser, and vision. We will study Bayesian estimation and Kalman filtering in depth. We will learn particle filters and their use in localization. We will study map representations: occupancy grids, feature maps, and topological maps. We will build up to SLAM: the problem, its structure, and its main solution families. We will study visual place recognition and loop closure. We will examine robustness and dynamic environments. We will explore applications and practical systems. And we will end with trends and reflections.

To succeed in this course, hold the probabilistic mindset: every estimate is a belief with uncertainty, every measurement updates belief, and every prediction spreads it. Embrace uncertainty rather than fearing it, and localization becomes clear.

Let us begin our journey into knowing where we are.

PART 2 - POSE, FRAMES, AND DEAD RECKONING

Let me walk you through pose, coordinate frames, and dead reckoning, the foundations everything else rests on.

A robot's pose is its position and orientation in space. In a planar world, pose has three values: x position, y position, and heading angle. In three dimensions, pose has six values: three position and three orientation. Pose is expressed relative to a reference frame: coordinates only have meaning relative to some origin.

Coordinate frames govern the vocabulary. The world frame or map frame is the fixed global reference. The odom frame is a locally consistent frame from odometry that drifts slowly. The robot base frame moves with the robot. Sensor frames are attached to each sensor: laser, camera, and inertial unit. Transformations between frames, studied in kinematics, connect them; tools like transform trees manage these relationships in practice.

Dead reckoning is estimating pose by integrating motion commands or measured motions over time: from wheel rotations, infer how far and how the robot turned, and accumulate. Dead reckoning is the oldest navigation art: sailors used it, and robots still do.

Odometry is dead reckoning with wheel encoders or visual motion. Differential drive odometry computes distance from wheel rotations and heading change from wheel speed differences. Visual odometry estimates motion from camera images by tracking features between frames. Inertial odometry integrates accelerations and angular rates from inertial measurement units.

The fatal weakness of dead reckoning is drift. Small errors in each step accumulate: a wheel diameter slightly wrong, a slight misalignment, a moment of slip, and the estimate wanders further from truth over time. Inertial integration drifts fastest; wheel odometry drifts with every meter. Drift is unbounded; dead reckoning alone cannot sustain accuracy over long missions.

Let me quantify error growth intuitively. Position error from odometry grows roughly with distance traveled: one percent scale error means one meter error per hundred meters. Heading error is worse: a small heading error creates a growing lateral position error like a diverging arrow. Therefore, absolute references are essential.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian warehouse robot's odometry drifts across a shift; without correction, it misidentifies aisles. A student robot crosses a room and finds its odometry pose error of ten centimeters, mostly from wheel slip on smooth tiles. A delivery robot integrates inertial sensing between wheel updates and sees errors grow within seconds. Treat dead reckoning as the locally smooth but globally drifting foundation, and plan to correct it with absolute references.

A practical habit: calibrate odometry carefully: measure effective wheel diameters and track width with systematic tests; calibration cuts drift substantially.

PART 3 - SENSORS FOR LOCALIZATION

Let me walk you through the sensor families that enable localization, each with strengths and honest weaknesses.

Odometry, which we just covered, gives smooth relative motion with drift. Wheel encoders measure rotation; visual odometry tracks motion from images; both are relative references.

Inertial measurement units measure accelerations and angular rates. Integration gives motion estimates. Modern micro electrical mechanical systems are small and cheap, but drift with integration, especially position. Inertial sensing excels at fast motion and orientation over short times. Sensor fusion corrects its drift.

Global navigation satellite systems like GPS give absolute position outdoors with meter level accuracy, improved by differential and real time kinematic corrections to centimeters. Indoors and under canopy, GPS fails. Urban canyons with tall buildings degrade accuracy. GPS is the absolute reference outdoors; other references serve indoors.

Laser rangefinders, including two dimensional and three dimensional lidar, measure distances to surroundings with high accuracy. A laser scan is a fan of range measurements; matching scans to maps or to previous scans yields pose. Laser localization is robust and precise in structured environments with distinctive geometry. Lidar is the workhorse of indoor and many outdoor localization.

Cameras enable visual localization: recognizing places from appearance, tracking features, and estimating pose from images. Cameras are cheap and rich in information but sensitive to lighting and texture. Visual place recognition identifies previously visited locations, key to loop closure in SLAM.

Ultra wideband radios provide ranging between anchors and tag, giving absolute position estimates in GPS denied environments, increasingly used in warehouses and mines.

Ultra sound sensors measure short ranges cheaply, useful for close proximity and simple localization beacons.

Wireless signal based localization uses WiFi fingerprints or signal strengths for coarse position, helpful in buildings.

Magnetic and barometric sensing add context: magnetic anomalies affect compasses; barometers estimate altitude changes.

Sensor fusion combines these strengths: inertial for fast smoothness, odometry for continuity, laser or vision for absolute correction, GPS outdoors. Kalman filters and particle filters implement fusion, which we will study next.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian hospital robot fuses wheel odometry with laser scan matching for precise indoor localization. A drone survey project fuses GPS, inertial, and visual odometry for stable positioning over a construction site. A warehouse adopts ultra wideband anchors for localization in aisles where lidar sees repetitive geometry. Choose sensors to environment and budget; fuse for robustness.

A practical habit: characterize every sensor's failure modes in your target environment: glass corridors for lasers, textureless walls for cameras, metal interference for inertial units.

PART 4 - BAYESIAN ESTIMATION AND KALMAN FILTERING

Let me walk you through Bayesian estimation and Kalman filtering, the mathematical heart of modern localization.

The probabilistic framing: the robot maintains a belief about its pose, expressed as a probability distribution. Motion prediction spreads the belief as the robot moves and uncertainty grows. Measurement update sharpens the belief as observations arrive. This predict update cycle is Bayesian filtering, and it is the engine of localization.

Bayes theorem governs the update: the posterior belief is proportional to the likelihood of the measurement times the prior belief. Intuitively: combine what you expected with what you observed, weighted by their uncertainties.

The Kalman filter is the optimal recursive estimator for linear systems with Gaussian noise. Let me describe its intuition without drowning in matrices. The filter keeps a state estimate and a covariance matrix describing uncertainty. Prediction step: propagate the state through the motion model and grow the covariance by process noise. Update step: compare the predicted measurement with the actual measurement; the difference is called innovation; the Kalman gain decides how much to trust the measurement versus the prediction; the estimate is corrected and the covariance shrinks. Trust more the more precise the source: if sensors are very precise, measurements dominate; if sensors are noisy but motion model is good, prediction dominates.

The extended Kalman filter handles nonlinear systems by linearizing around the current estimate at each step. This is the practical workhorse for robot localization: nonlinear motion models and nonlinear measurement models are the norm. The unscented Kalman filter improves on linearization by sampling sigma points through the true nonlinearities, more accurate for strongly nonlinear systems.

The extended Kalman filter for robot localization: state is pose and sometimes velocities; motion model predicts pose from odometry or inertial inputs; measurement model predicts observations from pose and map. With known landmarks or scan matching corrections, the filter maintains a smooth, drifting corrected pose estimate.

Filter consistency and tuning matter practically. Process noise and measurement noise parameters must reflect reality; bad tuning gives overconfidence or sluggishness. Innovation sequences should be zero mean and white for consistent filters.

Multi sensor fusion uses filter variants fusing asynchronous measurements from different sensors at different rates.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian robotics student implements an extended Kalman filter fusing wheel odometry with landmark bearings, and watches her pose estimate stop wandering. A drone flight stack uses an unscented Kalman filter fusing inertial, GPS, and visual odometry for robust state estimation in wind. A hospital robot fuses ultra wideband ranges with odometry in a Kalman filter. The filter is the quiet hero of every working robot.

A practical habit: log innovations and filter consistency during testing; they reveal sensor problems and tuning issues before users notice.

PART 5 - PARTICLE FILTERS AND MARKOV LOCALIZATION

Let me walk you through particle filters and Markov localization, the flexible workhorses for global localization and multi modal beliefs.

Sometimes pose beliefs are not Gaussian: the robot may be lost between two similar corridors, with belief concentrated at two distinct places. Kalman filters assume unimodal Gaussian beliefs and fail here. Particle filters represent beliefs as a set of weighted samples, particles, each a pose hypothesis with a weight. The cloud of particles can represent any distribution, including multiple hypotheses.

The particle filter cycle. First, prediction: move each particle by the motion command with added noise reflecting uncertainty. Second, update: weight each particle by how well its predicted sensor reading matches the actual reading. Third, resample: draw a new particle set with replacement according to weights, concentrating particles where belief is high. Resampling prevents weight collapse onto one particle and keeps computation focused.

Markov localization uses particle filters or grids to maintain pose belief over the whole map. Robot kidnapping problem: the robot is moved without odometry; particle filters recover by maintaining diverse particles and having enough of them land in the right place. Kidnapping is the acid test of global localization.

The number of particles trades accuracy against computation: thousands of particles for global localization, hundreds for tracking. Adaptive particle counts adjust to uncertainty.

Sensor models in particle filters: for lidar, likelihood of a scan given pose; for cameras, appearance similarity. Good sensor models matter enormously.

Monte Carlo localization is the celebrated name for particle filter localization with lidar, widely used in service robots and industrial platforms.

Convergence properties: particle filters converge with enough particles and informative measurements; degeneracy, where all weight goes to one particle, is managed with resampling and injection of random particles.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian service robot performs global localization at startup with a particle filter over its floor map, converging within seconds even when started in an ambiguous corridor. A student implements a simple one dimensional particle filter robot on a line with three landmarks and sees multi modal beliefs resolve. A logistics robot recovers from being carried to another floor by particle diversity and re localization. Particle filters are the robust answer when beliefs are complex.

A practical habit: visualize particle clouds during development; they tell the story of belief, convergence, and confusion better than any log.

PART 6 - MAP REPRESENTATIONS

Let me walk you through map representations: how robots store knowledge of the world.

The choice of map representation shapes what the robot can do and how computation scales.

Occupancy grids divide space into cells; each cell holds the probability of being occupied. Grid maps are intuitive, easy to build from range sensors, and support path planning directly. Resolution trades detail against memory and computation: five centimeter grids are common indoors. Grid maps handle arbitrary geometry well and are the practical default for indoor mobile robots.

Feature maps store geometric primitives: lines for walls, corners, points for landmarks. Feature maps are compact and support efficient localization by matching features. They assume structure; unstructured environments are harder.

Topological maps represent places and connections: rooms as nodes, doorways as edges, like a subway map. Topological maps are compact, robust to metric errors, and natural for high level planning and human communication. Hybrid metric topological maps combine detail where needed with global structure.

Three dimensional representations extend grids to voxels, volumetric pixels, for full spatial mapping; surfel maps store oriented surface patches; and mesh maps build connected surfaces. Three dimensional mapping supports aerial and complex environments.

Semantic maps attach meaning to geometry: this is a kitchen, that is a doorway, this table is a meeting table. Semantic mapping supports human level interaction and high level planning, and is a frontier of research.

Dynamic maps handle change: doors open and close, furniture moves, people walk. Maintaining maps with temporal models distinguishes permanent structure from transient clutter. Dynamic mapping is essential for long term autonomy.

Map quality metrics: coverage, accuracy against ground truth, consistency, size, and usefulness for planning and localization. Building maps that are correct and useful is a craft.

Map storage and sharing: maps are data products; saving, loading, and sharing maps across robots multiplies value. Multi robot systems share and merge maps.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian hospital robot maintains a hybrid map: metric grids on each floor, topological links between floors, semantic labels on rooms for patient friendly interaction. A student builds a grid map of a classroom from a laser scanner and uses it for both localization and planning. An agricultural mapping project uses three dimensional maps of orchards from drone data. Choose representations to your tasks: localization wants features or grids, planning wants traversability, humans want semantics.

A practical habit: keep maps versioned and dated; environments change, and stale maps cause mysterious failures.

PART 7 - SLAM: THE PROBLEM AND ITS STRUCTURE

Let me walk you through SLAM, simultaneous localization and mapping, the celebrated problem and its elegant structure.

SLAM asks: can a robot build a map of an unknown environment while simultaneously localizing within it. The chicken and egg: mapping needs pose, pose needs map. The breakthrough insight of the field is that errors in pose and map are correlated: solving them jointly is easier than solving either alone.

Let me explain the correlation intuition. As a robot moves and observes landmarks, its pose estimates drift; this drift creates consistent distortions in the map. The key observation: relative observations between landmarks constrain the distortion. When the robot revisits a place, loop closure, the loop constraints correct accumulated drift in both pose and map together. SLAM converts a drifting estimation problem into a globally consistent one through these loop constraints.

The SLAM process has stages. Front end: process raw sensor data into constraints: odometry constraints between consecutive poses, and observation constraints between poses and landmarks or map features. Back end: solve the global estimation problem: find poses and map that best satisfy all constraints. Loop closure detection: recognize revisited places and add loop constraints. Map building: render the final map from corrected poses.

The dominant modern formulation is graph based SLAM. Poses are nodes in a graph; constraints are edges; finding the best map is minimizing the total error across constraints, a nonlinear least squares optimization problem solved efficiently with sparse solvers. Graph SLAM is elegant, scalable, and the foundation of practical systems.

Filter based SLAM, the earlier approach, uses extended Kalman filters or particle filters to maintain joint pose landmark beliefs. EKF SLAM is simple but scales poorly with many landmarks; sparse extended information filters improved scaling. Particle filter SLAM handles nonlinearity but suffers in large maps. Graph based methods won the scalability battle for large environments.

Feature based SLAM extracts landmarks and tracks them; direct SLAM uses raw sensor intensities for matching without explicit features, gaining accuracy in low feature environments.

Real time SLAM runs online as the robot moves; offline SLAM optimizes after the fact with global smoothing. Practical systems run online with periodic global optimization.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian robotics lab runs graph based SLAM with a two dimensional laser on a wheeled robot mapping an entire building floor in forty minutes, with loop closures correcting hallway drift. A drone project runs three dimensional SLAM over a construction site with cameras and inertial sensing. A student watches a SLAM map visibly jump into alignment when a loop closure occurs, the thrill of seeing drift corrected. SLAM turns unknown worlds into navigable maps.

A practical habit: when a SLAM map looks wrong, inspect loop closures first: missed loops cause drift; false loops cause jumps; both are visible in the pose graph.

PART 8 - LOOP CLOSURE, PLACE RECOGNITION, AND ROBUSTNESS

Let me walk you through loop closure and robustness, the topics that separate lab demonstrations from dependable systems.

Loop closure detection decides when the robot has returned to a previously visited place. It is a recognition problem under severe challenges: viewpoint changes, lighting changes, seasons, and dynamic objects. A missed loop closure leaves drift uncorrected; a false loop closure corrupts the map, worse than none.

Place recognition methods. Appearance based methods compare visual descriptors of places: histograms of gradients, learned convolutional features, and bag of words models from image retrieval. Modern learned descriptors, like NetVLAD style representations, give robust place recognition. Lidar based methods match scan context descriptors or local geometric signatures.

Geometric verification follows candidate recognition: confirm the match by geometric consistency before accepting the loop. Rigorous verification prevents false loops.

Incremental mapping with pose graph optimization adds loop constraints and re optimizes efficiently. Robust cost functions, like Huber losses, reduce the influence of outlier constraints.

Robustness in SLAM addresses many threats. Dynamic environments: moving people and doors create false features and clutter; dynamic object filtering removes them. Degenerate environments: long corridors and glass tunnels provide weak constraints; prior knowledge and additional sensors help. Sensor failures: drifting inertial units, dirty lenses; monitoring and fallbacks matter. And computational constraints: real time optimization with bounded resources.

Long term autonomy adds revisiting and change detection: distinguishing permanent structure from transient change, updating maps with time indexed models.

Multi robot SLAM shares the robustness theme: merging maps from different robots requires place recognition across viewpoints and dealing with inconsistent data.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian warehouse mapping project struggles with glass walls that return no laser signal; they add reflective markers and visual fallback. A campus mapping robot manages day and night lighting changes with learned place descriptors. A rescue robot team handles dynamic rubble with robust optimization that down weights inconsistent observations. Design for the hard cases; easy cases take care of themselves.

A practical habit: log every loop closure candidate and its verification result; closure logs are invaluable for debugging and improving robustness.

PART 9 - VISUAL LOCALIZATION, INDOOR AND OUTDOOR SYSTEMS

Let me walk you through visual localization and practical localization systems in indoor and outdoor settings.

Visual localization uses cameras to estimate pose. Structure from motion builds three dimensional structure and camera poses from image collections. Visual odometry tracks motion frame to frame. Visual SLAM combines both online. Monocular systems are cheap but scale ambiguous; stereo and depth cameras recover metric scale directly.

Visual inertial odometry fuses camera and inertial data, the state of the art for small robots and phones: inertial sensing handles fast motion and scale, vision corrects drift. Modern drones and augmented reality devices use visual inertial systems.

Image retrieval localizes by recognizing places from appearance databases: capture an image, find the closest matches in a mapped database, and estimate pose from correspondences. This enables re localization when lost.

Indoor localization systems in practice. Laser based Monte Carlo localization with pre built grid maps is the reliable standard for structured indoor environments. Visual localization serves where laser struggles: texture rich but geometrically repetitive spaces. Ultra wideband beacons give absolute fixes in GPS denied buildings. WiFi fingerprinting gives coarse room level localization. Practical indoor systems fuse several of these.

Outdoor localization fuses GPS with odometry, inertial, and visual or laser corrections. In urban canyons and tunnels where GPS degrades, prior maps and place recognition maintain accuracy. Agricultural and mining robots rely on high precision GPS where available and SLAM elsewhere.

Localization in extreme environments: underwater with sonar and bathymetric maps; underground with lidar and inertial; in space with crater and feature matching. Each demands tailored sensing and estimation.

System design for localization services. Define accuracy and availability requirements. Select sensing to environment. Choose estimation architecture. Build and maintain maps. Monitor health with consistency metrics. Test across conditions: day and night, crowds, seasons.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian logistics hub deploys ultra wideband anchors plus wheel odometry for fleet localization with sub meter accuracy. A hospital robot relies on laser Monte Carlo localization with carefully built maps and daily self checks. An agricultural tractor uses precise GPS with inertial and visual row following. Match sensing to the environment, fuse for resilience, and monitor continuously.

A practical habit: maintain a localization performance log with accuracy estimates and anomalies; trends reveal degrading sensors before failures.

PART 10 - TRENDS, ETHICS, AND COURSE COMPLETION

Let me close with trends, ethics, and your course synthesis.

Trends shaping localization and mapping. Learned components transform the field: learned place recognition, learned feature matching, and end to end learned odometry compete with geometric methods and often combine with them. Neural scene representations, implicit maps like neural radiance style representations, compress environments richly. Event cameras promise high dynamic range and microsecond timing for challenging conditions. Multi modal fusion matures across lidar, radar, camera, and inertial. Cloud and collaborative mapping share maps across robot fleets. And foundation models bring semantic understanding to mapping: maps that know what things are.

Ethics and society. Mapping and localization enable tracking: privacy protections must govern whose spaces are mapped and who can access maps. Security: manipulated localization signals, like GPS spoofing, endanger systems; robustness is safety. Consent and data governance for mapped environments matter, especially in homes and public spaces. And equitable access: localization technology should serve broad needs, including African development in agriculture, logistics, and infrastructure.

For Nigeria and Africa, localization and mapping unlock practical autonomy: delivery robots in complex buildings, agricultural robots on farms without GPS reliability, inspection robots in industrial sites, and mapping for infrastructure planning. Local expertise ensures these systems suit local environments and needs.

Glossary of Key Terms

Localization: estimating a robot's pose in a map. Mapping: building a representation of the environment. SLAM: simultaneous localization and mapping. Pose: position and orientation of a robot. Dead reckoning: estimating pose by integrating motion. Odometry: measuring relative motion from wheels or vision. Drift: accumulating error in integrated estimates. Inertial measurement unit: sensor measuring acceleration and angular rate. Global navigation satellite system: satellite based positioning like GPS. Lidar: laser range scanning sensor. Ultra wideband: radio ranging for precise localization. Bayesian filtering: recursive belief updating with prediction and measurement. Belief: probability distribution over robot state. Kalman filter: optimal recursive estimator for linear Gaussian systems. Extended Kalman filter: Kalman filter for nonlinear systems via linearization. Unscented Kalman filter: filter using sigma points through nonlinearities. Innovation: difference between predicted and actual measurement. Kalman gain: weighting between prediction and measurement. Particle filter: belief represented by weighted samples. Markov localization: localization maintaining pose belief over maps. Monte Carlo localization: particle filter localization with range sensors. Resampling: drawing particles according to weights. Robot kidnapping: robot moved without odometry. Occupancy grid: cell based probabilistic map. Feature map: map of geometric landmarks. Topological map: map of places and connections. Semantic map: map with meanings attached. Graph based SLAM: SLAM as graph optimization problem. Loop closure: recognizing a revisited place. Place recognition: identifying locations from appearance. Pose graph optimization: solving poses from constraint graph. Structure from motion: recovering structure and poses from images. Visual odometry: motion estimation from camera images. Visual inertial odometry: fused camera and inertial motion estimation. Robust cost function: error metric reducing outlier influence. Dynamic environment: environment with moving and changing objects. Consistency: statistical property of well behaved estimators. Re localization: recovering pose after being lost. Surfel: oriented surface patch representation. Voxel: three dimensional pixel cell.
