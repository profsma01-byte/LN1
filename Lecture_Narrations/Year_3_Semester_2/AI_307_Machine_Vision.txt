PART 1 - WELCOME TO MACHINE VISION

Hello everyone, and welcome to AI 307, Machine Vision. In Year Three, Semester Two, we begin with one of the most practical and powerful subjects in your programme. Machine vision is the technology that gives machines the gift of sight: the ability to acquire images, understand what is in them, and make decisions from what they see. Factories inspect products with it, robots pick parts with it, doctors are aided by it, and autonomous vehicles depend on it. In this course you will learn the complete machine vision pipeline, from camera to decision, with strong emphasis on industrial and real world practice. As always, we will ground everything in real life practicals and examples from Nigeria and Africa.

What is machine vision? Machine vision, sometimes called industrial vision or computer vision in practice, is the use of imaging technology and processing algorithms to inspect, measure, identify, and guide. Let me distinguish it slightly from related terms. Computer vision is the broad science of making computers understand images. Machine vision often emphasizes the applied, engineered system, especially in industry: cameras on a production line checking parts thousands of times per hour. Robot vision emphasizes vision for guiding robot action: where is the part so the arm can grasp it. All share the same core ideas, and this course covers them all with an engineering flavor.

Let me show why machine vision matters with a simple comparison. Consider a factory producing bottle caps in Ogun State. Human inspectors examine caps for defects: flashes, short shots, color errors. People get tired, inconsistent, and slow; they miss subtle defects after hours of work. A machine vision system inspects every cap at line speed, at any hour, with consistent criteria, logging every result. The result is higher quality, less waste, and traceable production data. That is the value proposition of machine vision: fast, consistent, tireless, quantitative inspection and guidance.

The machine vision pipeline is the backbone of this course. First, image acquisition: lighting, optics, and cameras capture a good image. Second, preprocessing: enhance and clean the image. Third, segmentation: separate what matters from the background. Fourth, feature extraction: measure shapes, sizes, textures, and colors. Fifth, classification and decision: is this part good or defective, what is this object, where is it. Sixth, communication and action: send results to the machine, the robot, or the database. Every real system follows this flow, and we will master each stage.

Real life practicals across our context. In Nigerian manufacturing, machine vision inspects beverage fill levels, label placement, and packaging integrity. In agriculture, vision grades tomatoes by color and size for export markets. In banking and document processing, vision reads and verifies documents. In healthcare, vision analyzes microscope images and supports diagnostics. In logistics, vision reads barcodes and guides sorting robots. Each of these is a system you will be able to design by the end of this course.

Machine vision connects deeply to your studies. Digital signal processing covers filtering and transforms. Pattern recognition and machine learning cover classification. Neural networks and deep learning power modern detection. Control systems use vision measurements in feedback. Robot kinematics and calibration convert pixels into coordinates the robot can act on. And ethics matters: vision systems watch people and products; privacy and fairness must be designed in.

How will we study this course? We will begin with the physics of image formation and the engineering of lighting, optics, and cameras. We will study image representation and preprocessing. We will master segmentation and feature extraction. We will study geometric measurement and calibration, converting pixels to real world units. We will learn classification from classical methods to neural networks. We will study object detection, localization, and robot guidance. We will address color, texture, and three dimensional vision. We will cover system design, integration, and performance evaluation. We will explore applications in inspection, agriculture, logistics, and health. And we will end with trends and reflections.

To succeed in machine vision, remember one principle above all: a machine vision system is only as good as its image. Clever algorithms cannot rescue a poorly lit, poorly focused, poorly composed image. Engineer the image first. Let us begin.

PART 2 - IMAGE FORMATION, LIGHTING, OPTICS, AND CAMERAS

Let me walk you through image formation and the hardware that creates images: lighting, optics, and cameras. This is where machine vision engineering begins.

An image is formed when light from a scene passes through optics onto a sensor. Understanding this physical process is essential because it determines what information your algorithms can possibly extract.

Lighting is the first and most important design choice. Light interacts with surfaces by reflection, absorption, transmission, and scattering. Surface finish matters enormously: shiny metal specularly reflects, creating glare and hot spots; matte surfaces diffuse light evenly. Good machine vision lighting creates contrast between what you want to see and what you do not.

Lighting techniques are the craft of the field. Bright field illumination lights the surface evenly so defects show as dark spots on a bright background. Dark field illumination lights from low angles so scratches and edges glow against dark backgrounds, superb for surface defects on glass and metal. Back lighting places the object between light and camera, creating a crisp silhouette for measurement, ideal for dimensional gauging. Structured light projects patterns, lines or grids, onto surfaces for three dimensional measurement. Dome lighting diffuses from all directions for even illumination of curved shiny parts. Low angle ring lights emphasize texture and edges.

The choice of light wavelength matters too. Monochrome red or infrared light simplifies processing and can reveal features invisible in white light. Ultraviolet light excites fluorescence for certain inspections. The principle: choose light that maximizes the contrast of the feature of interest.

Optics form the image. Lenses focus light onto the sensor. Key lens parameters: focal length determines field of view and magnification; aperture controls light and depth of field; and lens quality determines sharpness and distortion. Depth of field is the range of distances in focus; smaller apertures increase it, valuable when parts vary in position. Machine vision lenses are designed for low distortion and precise focus, superior to ordinary camera lenses.

Optical filters select wavelengths: band pass filters pass one color, polarizing filters kill glare from shiny surfaces, neutral density filters reduce brightness. Filters are cheap, powerful tools.

Cameras convert light to digital signals. The main sensor technologies are charge coupled devices and, dominant today, complementary metal oxide semiconductor sensors. Sensor parameters matter: resolution in pixels determines detail; pixel size affects light sensitivity; frame rate determines speed; and dynamic range affects contrast handling.

Camera types. Area scan cameras capture two dimensional images, the standard. Line scan cameras capture one line at a time, building images as products move past, ideal for continuous webs like textiles and steel. Three dimensional cameras, including stereo, structured light, and time of flight cameras, capture depth directly. Smart cameras integrate sensor, processor, and I O in one package for simple standalone applications.

Calibration of camera settings: exposure time, gain, and white balance must be set for consistency. Fixed settings beat automatic settings in inspection, because automatic adaptation can hide defects.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian bottling plant adds dark field lighting to detect cap scratches invisible under general lighting; defect detection improves dramatically without any algorithm change. A tomato grading station uses diffuse dome lighting to measure color accurately without glare. A student team struggles with inconsistent images until they build a proper light shield and fixed exposure; the lesson lands hard. Design the imaging station as carefully as the algorithm.

A practical habit: write a lighting test plan before choosing algorithms: try at least three lighting geometries on sample parts and compare contrast quantitatively.

PART 3 - IMAGE REPRESENTATION AND PREPROCESSING

Let me walk you through how images are represented in the computer and how preprocessing turns raw images into clean data for analysis.

An image is a grid of pixels. In a grayscale image, each pixel has one value of brightness. In a color image, each pixel has multiple channels; the most common is red, green, blue. Understanding color spaces matters: red green blue is how cameras capture; hue saturation value separates color from brightness, useful for color inspection; and other spaces like Lab give perceptual uniformity.

Image resolution and bit depth define information content: more pixels mean more detail, more bits mean finer brightness levels. Trade offs with processing speed must be balanced against task needs.

Image quality problems are daily realities: noise from sensors in low light, blur from motion or poor focus, uneven illumination, low contrast, and lens distortion. Preprocessing addresses these.

Noise reduction filters smooth unwanted variation. A median filter is superb for salt and pepper noise while preserving edges, essential before edge detection. Gaussian blur smoothens noise but softens edges; use carefully. Bilateral filtering smoothens while preserving edges, elegant but slower. The rule: denoise just enough; excessive smoothing destroys detail.

Contrast enhancement spreads brightness values over the full range, helping segmentation. Histogram equalization improves global contrast; local methods handle uneven illumination. Homomorphic filtering corrects illumination gradients.

Geometric corrections fix lens distortion, straightening lines bent by optics. Undistortion maps pixels to an ideal pinhole camera model, essential for measurement work.

Image transforms provide alternative views. The Fourier transform reveals spatial frequencies, useful for texture analysis and periodic pattern inspection. Wavelet transforms give multi scale analysis. These tools, from your signal processing course, apply directly.

Morphological operations, on binary images, clean shapes: erosion shrinks, dilation grows, opening removes small noise, closing fills small holes. These simple operations dramatically improve segmentation results.

Real life practicals. An inspection line in Lagos applies median filtering to remove sensor noise before measuring part dimensions. A document processing system corrects uneven illumination from office lighting before reading text. A student preprocessing pipeline removes glare with polarizers and corrects lens distortion before measuring coin diameters, achieving sub millimeter accuracy. Preprocessing is unglamorous and decisive; budget time for it.

A practical habit: visualize before and after every preprocessing step. Seeing the effect prevents both under processing and destructive over processing.

PART 4 - SEGMENTATION: FINDING WHAT MATTERS

Let me walk you through segmentation: separating the objects and regions that matter from everything else.

Segmentation partitions an image into meaningful regions: object versus background, defect versus sound surface, one item versus another. Segmentation quality determines the ceiling of everything downstream.

Thresholding is the simplest and most used method: pixels above a brightness threshold become object, below become background. Global thresholding uses one value for the whole image, ideal when lighting is controlled and contrast is clear. Adaptive thresholding adjusts locally, handling uneven illumination. Otsu's method automatically finds a good threshold by maximizing between class variance. Color thresholding segments by color in chosen color spaces, powerful for grading produce.

Edge detection finds boundaries where brightness changes sharply. The Canny detector is the classic, with multi stage noise reduction, gradient computation, non maximum suppression, and hysteresis linking. Edges feed shape analysis and measurement. Practical tips: denoise before edge detection and tune thresholds to the contrast of real images.

Region based methods grow regions from seeds by adding neighboring pixels of similar properties. Watershed segmentation treats brightness like topography and floods basins to separate touching objects, valuable for counting clustered items like cells or grains.

Contour analysis extracts object boundaries as polygons and shapes for measurement: area, perimeter, circularity, and bounding boxes. Hierarchical contour relations, which contours are inside which, support structured scenes.

Color and texture segmentation uses color statistics or texture features to partition regions, useful for grading food and textiles.

Learning based segmentation is the modern frontier. Semantic segmentation assigns each pixel a class label with deep networks. Instance segmentation separates individual objects. These methods handle complex scenes where classical methods struggle, at the cost of training data and computation.

Practical segmentation advice. First, control the imaging to make segmentation easy: this is often the cheapest win. Second, start with the simplest method: thresholding handles a surprising share of industrial tasks. Third, combine methods: threshold then morphological cleanup then contour filtering. Fourth, validate on many images: segmentations that look good on one image often fail on the distribution.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian food processing company segments rice grains by color thresholding to remove discolored grains. A medical lab segments blood cells with watershed to count them accurately. A student counts coins on a table with adaptive thresholding and circularity filtering. Segmentation is the bridge from pixels to objects; build it on controlled imaging and simple methods first.

A practical habit: collect a validation set of at least fifty representative images before finalizing segmentation parameters; test on all of them, not just favorites.

PART 5 - FEATURE EXTRACTION AND MEASUREMENT

Let me walk you through feature extraction and measurement: turning segmented objects into numbers that capture what matters.

A feature is a measurable property used to characterize an object. Good features are discriminative, stable, and cheap to compute. Let me tour the feature families.

Shape features describe geometry: area counts pixels; perimeter measures boundary length; circularity compares area to the perfect circle; elongation and aspect ratio describe stretching; convexity and solidity describe shape regularity; and Hu moments give rotation invariant shape descriptors. Shape features power dimensional inspection and sorting.

Color features describe appearance: mean color values, histograms per channel, and color moments. Color spaces matter for stability under lighting changes; separating chromaticity from brightness helps.

Texture features describe surface patterns: co occurrence matrices quantify how pixel values neighbor each other; local binary patterns are robust and fast texture descriptors; and frequency based features capture periodicity. Texture inspection finds weave defects, surface roughness classes, and tissue types.

Statistical features aggregate pixel distributions: mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis. Simple and useful for material classification.

Corner and interest point features, like Harris corners and scale invariant features, describe distinctive local structures for matching images: tracking, stitching, and recognition. These underpin applications like panorama creation and object recognition.

Measurement is the quantitative heart of machine vision. Pixel measurements convert to real world units through calibration, which we will study next. Dimensional gauging measures lengths, diameters, angles, and positions to tolerances. Machine vision gauges achieve micrometer precision with careful imaging.

Feature selection reduces dimensionality: keeping only features that discriminate between classes improves speed and accuracy. Principal component analysis compresses features into informative combinations.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian automotive parts supplier measures bolt diameters with shape features and calibrated pixel to millimeter conversion, catching out of tolerance parts automatically. A textile mill detects weave defects using texture features and frequency analysis. A student classifies three bean varieties by color and shape features with simple classifiers. Measure what matters: choose features aligned to the defect or property of interest.

A practical habit: visualize feature distributions for good and bad classes; separation in feature space predicts classification success before you train any classifier.

PART 6 - CALIBRATION AND GEOMETRIC MEASUREMENT

Let me walk you through calibration: the essential bridge from pixels to the physical world.

Calibration establishes the mathematical relationship between pixel coordinates and real world coordinates. Without it, you count pixels; with it, you measure millimeters and drive robots.

Camera calibration models the imaging geometry. The pinhole camera model describes how three dimensional points project onto two dimensional images through a camera matrix containing intrinsic parameters: focal lengths, optical center, and distortion coefficients. Calibration computes these parameters from images of a known pattern, a checkerboard or dot grid viewed from several poses. Open source calibration tools make this routine.

Distortion calibration corrects lens effects: barrel and pincushion distortion bend straight lines; undistortion maps images back to ideal geometry. Measurement accuracy depends on it.

Hand eye calibration relates the camera frame to the robot frame. Eye to hand configuration: camera fixed, observing the robot workspace. Eye in hand configuration: camera mounted on the robot arm. Hand eye calibration solves for the transform between camera and robot, enabling the fundamental capability: see a point in pixels, command the robot to move there in millimeters.

World coordinate calibration maps image coordinates to a working plane using a reference object or known geometry. For planar measurement tasks this is simple and powerful.

Measurement accuracy analysis matters professionally. Sources of error: calibration quality, lighting effects on edges, lens distortion, mechanical vibration, and temperature drift. Error budgets and repeated measurements quantify accuracy honestly. Sub pixel edge detection, interpolating edge positions to fractions of a pixel, dramatically improves measurement precision.

Three dimensional measurement extends vision into depth. Stereo vision uses two cameras and triangulation, like human eyes. Structured light projects known patterns and computes depth from deformation. Time of flight cameras measure depth from photon travel time. Each suits different ranges, accuracies, and costs.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian fabrication shop calibrates a camera over a cutting bed to verify part dimensions against computer aided design drawings. A robotics student performs hand eye calibration so her robot arm picks parts exactly where the camera sees them; the first successful pick is a milestone moment. An agricultural sorting line uses a calibrated camera to grade fruit size in millimeters for export standards. Calibrate carefully, verify with known objects, and document the calibration for reproducibility.

A practical habit: verify calibration with a known reference object at regular intervals in production; calibration drift is a silent accuracy killer.

PART 7 - CLASSIFICATION, DETECTION, AND DEEP LEARNING IN VISION

Let me walk you through classification and detection: deciding what things are and where they are, from classical methods to modern deep learning.

Classification assigns labels to objects or images: good or defective, which variety, which disease. Detection locates and classifies multiple objects in scenes: find all the crates and tell me their positions. Both are core machine vision tasks.

Classical classification uses features you extracted and simple decision rules. Nearest neighbor classifies by similarity to stored examples. Support vector machines find optimal separating boundaries in feature space. Decision trees provide readable rule structures. Ensemble methods combine many weak classifiers into strong ones. These classical methods work well with good features and modest data, and remain valuable for their transparency and speed.

Deep learning transformed vision. Convolutional neural networks learn features hierarchically from data: edges, textures, parts, objects. Trained on labeled images, they classify with remarkable accuracy. Architectures evolved from early convolutional networks to deep residual networks and beyond. Transfer learning uses networks pre trained on large datasets and fine tunes them for your task with modest data, the practical workhorse of industrial deep vision.

Object detection networks localize and classify simultaneously. Two stage detectors first propose regions then classify them; single stage detectors predict boxes and classes directly in one pass, favoring speed. Real time detection enables robot guidance and video analysis.

Semantic and instance segmentation label every pixel, powering applications from medical imaging to autonomous driving perception.

Training deep vision models practically. Collect and label data carefully; label quality determines model quality. Augment data with rotations, brightness changes, and noise to build robustness. Split data into training, validation, and test sets honestly. Monitor for overfitting: memorizing training images instead of learning. Evaluate with metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, and confusion matrices.

Deploying deep models considers speed and hardware. Graphics processing units accelerate inference; edge devices run compact models near cameras. Model compression and quantization shrink models for embedded deployment.

Combining classical and learned methods is often best: classical imaging for controlled measurement, learned models for complex classification, and domain logic to combine results.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian manufacturing quality system uses a convolutional network trained on a few thousand defect images to classify surface flaws, with classical measurement for dimensional checks. A cassava disease detector runs a compact model on a phone at the farm edge. A logistics hub reads damaged package labels with a fine tuned detector. Deep learning brings power demanding data discipline; classical methods keep precision and speed where they shine.

A practical habit: before training any model, define the decision costs: is a missed defect worse than a false alarm. Set thresholds to serve those costs, not just accuracy.

PART 8 - ROBOT GUIDANCE, SYSTEM DESIGN, AND PERFORMANCE EVALUATION

Let me walk you through vision guided robots and the professional practice of designing and evaluating complete machine vision systems.

Vision guided robotics closes the loop between seeing and acting. The core loop: the camera sees the workspace; vision localizes objects and computes their poses; calibration converts pixel coordinates to robot coordinates; the planner computes a path; the robot acts; the camera verifies the result. This is applied robot vision in its purest form.

Guidance tasks include pick and place of randomly presented parts, bin picking from cluttered bins, conveyor tracking of moving objects, and assembly guidance inserting parts with vision corrections. Pose estimation determines position and orientation of objects, six dimensional poses for full guidance. Techniques range from template matching and geometric hashing to learned keypoint methods.

Visual servoing controls robot motion continuously from image feedback: position based servoing computes errors in world coordinates; image based servoing regulates image features directly. Visual servoing gives smooth, robust tracking and control.

Conveyor tracking synchronizes vision with moving products, triggering the camera at the right moments and commanding the robot to intercept moving parts, common in packaging lines.

System design is a professional discipline. Define requirements first: what decisions, what speed, what accuracy, what environment. Design the imaging station: lighting, optics, camera, mounting. Choose algorithms to requirements. Integrate with machines and controllers over industrial interfaces. Build in calibration and self diagnostics. Document everything.

Performance evaluation quantifies system quality. Metrics: detection rate and miss rate for defects; false alarm rate; measurement accuracy and repeatability; cycle time; and uptime. Confusion matrices summarize classification results. Gauge repeatability and reproducibility studies quantify measurement systems honestly. Test on real production distributions, not curated samples.

Real life practicals. A Nigerian beverage line installs vision guided robotic pickers that sort mixed bottles from a conveyor, calibrated daily and verified with test parts. An electronics assembler uses visual servoing to insert connectors with sub millimeter precision. A student capstone builds a bin picking cell and learns that presentation, structured lighting and part feeders, often matters more than the algorithm. Design the whole system: image, algorithm, calibration, integration, and evaluation.

A practical habit: write a test protocol with known good and known bad samples before commissioning; run it at every shift change to confirm the system still performs.

PART 9 - APPLICATIONS ACROSS INDUSTRIES

Robotics and automation guidance: bin picking, assembly, welding seam tracking, and mobile robot navigation using vision.

Logistics and retail: barcode and code reading, package dimensioning, damage detection, and automated sorting.

Agriculture: grading produce by size, color, and ripeness; detecting diseases and pests; guiding harvesting robots. For African agriculture, machine vision supports export quality standards and reduces post harvest losses.

Healthcare: microscope image analysis, cell counting, pathology assistance, and surgical instrument tracking. Vision extends scarce specialist capacity.

Document and identity processing: reading forms, verifying documents, and assisting banking workflows.

Security and access: face recognition and license plate recognition raise both capability and privacy responsibilities.

Real life practicals from our continent. A Nigerian fruit export packhouse installs grading machines that sort oranges by color and size to European standards, increasing revenue per crate. A South African winery inspects grape quality with vision on the sorting line. A Kenyan seed company grades seed lots with vision for uniformity. Each success followed the discipline of this course: control the image, extract the right features, measure, classify, and evaluate honestly.

PART 10 - TRENDS, ETHICS, AND COURSE COMPLETION

Let me close with trends, ethics, and a synthesis of your machine vision education.

Trends shaping the field. Deep learning continues to expand what vision can do with less hand crafting. Edge intelligence brings processing to cameras and sensors. Hyperspectral and multispectral imaging see beyond human vision for food safety and agriculture. Three dimensional vision matures for bin picking and metrology. Self supervised and few shot learning reduce labeling burdens. Vision combined with other senses, sensor fusion, gives robust perception. And cloud connected vision enables fleet learning across many installations.

Ethics and social responsibility in machine vision. Privacy: cameras watch people; systems must minimize, protect, and respect personal data. Fairness: learned models can encode biases from training data; diverse data and testing prevent discrimination. Transparency: inspection decisions should be explainable and auditable. Workforce impact: vision automation changes jobs; responsible organizations retrain and redeploy workers. And environmental impact: efficiency gains should serve sustainability.

For Nigeria and Africa, machine vision is an industrial competitiveness technology. Quality inspection enables export markets. Agricultural grading reduces losses and raises farmer incomes. Document vision supports financial inclusion. Building local expertise keeps the value on the continent; the engineers trained in courses like this one are the foundation.

Glossary of Key Terms

Machine vision: imaging and processing for inspection, measurement, and guidance. Computer vision: science of extracting information from images. Robot vision: vision systems guiding robot actions. Image acquisition: capturing images with lighting, optics, and cameras. Bright field illumination: even lighting with defects appearing dark. Dark field illumination: low angle lighting emphasizing surface features. Back lighting: lighting behind objects for silhouette measurement. Structured light: projected patterns for three dimensional measurement. Focal length: lens parameter controlling magnification and field of view. Depth of field: distance range in sharp focus. Charge coupled device: image sensor technology. Complementary metal oxide semiconductor: dominant modern sensor technology. Area scan camera: camera capturing two dimensional images. Line scan camera: camera building images line by line. Histogram equalization: contrast enhancement over full range. Median filter: noise removal preserving edges. Morphological operations: shape transformations on binary images. Thresholding: segmentation by brightness values. Otsu's method: automatic threshold selection. Canny edge detector: classic edge detection algorithm. Watershed segmentation: separation by flooding intensity basins. Region growing: segmentation by merging similar neighbors. Semantic segmentation: pixel level class labeling. Instance segmentation: separating individual object pixels. Hu moments: rotation invariant shape descriptors. Local binary pattern: texture descriptor feature. Camera calibration: computing imaging geometry parameters. Pinhole camera model: ideal projection model. Hand eye calibration: transform between camera and robot frames. Stereo vision: depth from two camera triangulation. Time of flight camera: depth from photon travel time. Sub pixel edge detection: edge localization finer than pixels. Pose estimation: determining object position and orientation. Visual servoing: robot control from image feedback. Template matching: finding patterns by similarity search. Transfer learning: adapting pre trained models to new tasks. Convolutional neural network: deep network for image analysis. Object detection: locating and classifying objects in scenes. Confusion matrix: table of classification results by class. Gauge repeatability and reproducibility: measurement system analysis. Augmentation: expanding training data with transformations.
