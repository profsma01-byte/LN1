MODULE 1: Addiction as a System Problem
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Addiction as a System Problem. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Addiction is a brain disease and a family disease. The family organises around addiction: who covers, who fights, who hides. Recovery requires family change, not only user change.

Let me say this as simply as I can. Addiction as a System Problem is not a label you paste on a person. It is a way of seeing how people move together, the way you would watch dancers and notice who leads, who follows, and who keeps stepping on whose toes. Once you can see the dance, you can teach a new step.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Ogbomosho, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving addiction as a system problem. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows addiction as a system problem in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Thank you both for coming. Before we begin, let me say what I already see: two people who are still here, still trying. Who would like to start?"
Wife: "I am tired of being ignored."
Therapist: "Ignored. That is a strong word. Can you give me one recent moment when you felt ignored?"
Wife: "Last night. I spoke and he kept looking at his phone."
Therapist: "And when she spoke and you kept looking at the phone, what was happening for you?"
Husband: "I was reading work messages. I did not know she needed me."
Therapist: "So one of you needed attention and the other did not know it was being asked for. That gap is where we will work."

Notice what the therapist did in that dialogue. She did not rush to solve. She named the feeling, slowed the pace, and invited the family to look at the pattern together. That is the difference between advice and therapy. Advice tells people what to do. Therapy helps people see clearly enough to decide for themselves.

Now let us name the mistakes young therapists make here. The first is taking sides too early. The moment you sound like the wife's lawyer, the husband stops listening. The second is rushing to comfort. Tears are not an emergency; they are a language. Let the tears speak before you hand out tissues. The third is talking more than the family. If you are speaking for more than a third of the session, you are working alone.

Let me test your understanding. A husband complains that his wife no longer respects him, and the wife complains that he no longer talks to her. Which is the presenting problem, and which is the pattern? Write your answer before you read on. The pattern is this: he feels disrespected, so he withdraws; she feels abandoned, so she criticises; he feels more disrespected. Two complaints, one dance.

Here is your practical step for this week. Choose one family you know well, perhaps your own, and for three days write down one interaction at dinner. Who spoke first? Who was interrupted? Who left the table first? Do not judge; just record. Patterns appear when you look with a pencil in your hand.

Quick reflection: Write in your notebook three ways Addiction as a System Problem applies to families in your community. Bring your answer to class next week. We will discuss together.


MODULE 2: Family Roles
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Family Roles. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Common roles are hero, scapegoat, lost child, mascot, and enabler. These roles help the family cope but keep the problem. Help families see roles and choose healthier ones.

Now let me explain it plainly, the way I would explain it to a market woman in Ibadan. Family Roles simply means that people in a family carry each other. If one person carries too much, the others feel lighter but weaker. If one person lets go, everyone stumbles. Balance is the goal, not perfection.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Makurdi lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of family roles became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. The therapist taught them a simple rule for the first month: "Nobody makes permanent decisions while the wound is fresh." That single rule reduced the fighting. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows family roles in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "When the two of you argue about this, what do you hear in your mind?"
Husband: "I hear that I am a failure."
Therapist: "And what do you hear, madam?"
Wife: "I hear that I am not a good wife."
Therapist: "You see, you are not really fighting each other. You are fighting two old voices. Let us separate the voices from the people, and then we can talk about the actual problem."

Watch how she used the genogram. She did not use it as paperwork; she used it as a story map. 'Who else in this family carried this burden?' she asked. The family discovered a pattern across three generations. Insight that comes from the family's own history is far more powerful than insight imported from a textbook.

Watch out for these mistakes. Do not label a family 'resistant' when they are simply afraid. Do not diagnose from one session. Do not allow one person to describe the whole family for all the others. And do not treat every problem as a trauma or every sadness as depression. Good therapists are slow to name and quick to listen.

Ask yourself. When a family says 'we have tried everything', what have they not tried? Usually they have not tried consistency, or they have not tried kindness, or they have not tried asking for help in the right place. Explore the word 'everything' with curiosity rather than acceptance.

This week, build one ritual. Choose a family you work with or your own home, and create a fifteen-minute routine that happens at the same time every day: a walk, a prayer, a cup of tea with no phones. Rituals are small investments with large returns. Report what happened.

MODULE 3: Enabling and Codependency
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Enabling and Codependency. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Enabling means helping addiction continue: covering, lying, paying debts. Codependency means the partner's life is controlled by addiction. Teach families to stop enabling with love and boundaries.

Here is the simple English version. Enabling and Codependency means that behaviour in a family is not random. It follows a pattern, and patterns can be learned and unlearned. Nobody teaches a family to quarrel every Sunday, yet the quarrel appears like a visitor who knows the time. Therapy teaches the family to change the appointment.

Now, let us look at this in real life. Let me tell you about a family in Zaria. They came to therapy because the matter of enabling and codependency had turned their home into a place of silence. The father would not speak at dinner. The mother cried in the kitchen. The children learned to become invisible. When the therapist finally had all of them in one room, the truth came out gently: nobody had ever been taught how to handle enabling and codependency, so each person had invented a private strategy. Those private strategies were colliding every day. The therapist did not scold anyone. She drew their strategies on paper, and for the first time the family saw the same picture. That picture became the beginning of a new conversation.

You see, my students, this example shows enabling and codependency in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Describe a normal Tuesday in your house."
Father: "We wake, we rush, we work, we come home, we sleep."
Therapist: "Where is the part of Tuesday when the family is actually together?"
Father: (silence)
Therapist: "That silence is important. It is telling us something. What would you like Tuesday to look like?"
Father: "I would like to eat with them."
Therapist: "Then let us build Tuesday around that meal."

Watch the therapist's moves. She used the family's own words instead of her textbook words. She asked one question at a time. She let the silence work. And when the emotion rose, she did not run from it; she stayed, steady as a wall, so the family could lean without falling.

Here is a list of errors to keep before your eyes. Do not assume a quiet family is a healthy family. Do not assume a loud family is a violent family. Do not assume that the person who called for help is the person who needs the most help. And do not assume the problem is new; often it has been growing quietly for years.

Here is a hard one. A couple wants you to tell them whether to divorce. What is your answer? You do not decide for them. You help them clarify their values, their options, their children's needs, and their readiness. Discernment is not advice-giving; it is accompanying a decision.

Your assignment is to write a letter you will never send. Write to a family member who hurt you, describing what you felt and what you needed. Do not send it. Then notice whether your body feels lighter. Unspoken pain becomes a therapist's blind spot; spoken pain becomes compassion.

MODULE 4: Alcohol Addiction
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Alcohol Addiction. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Alcohol harms health, work, and family. Assess use, withdrawal risk, and readiness. Refer for medical detox when needed. Families learn support, boundaries, and Al-Anon resources.

In plain words, Alcohol Addiction is about who carries the weight and who carries the blame. In unhealthy families, the weakest person often carries both. In healthy families, the weight is shared and blame is replaced by responsibility. That shift is the heart of our work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Enugu, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving alcohol addiction. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows alcohol addiction in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your mother called you lazy. What happened inside you when she said it?"
Teenager: "I stopped listening."
Therapist: "That is honest. And what would you have wanted her to say instead?"
Teenager: "That she is worried, not that I am useless."
Therapist: "That is a sentence we can work with. Mother, could you say what you actually feel, using his sentence as a guide?"
Mother: "I am worried about your future. I am afraid for you."
Teenager: "Then say that. I can hear that."

Observe the moment of repair. The husband raised his voice, and the therapist said calmly, 'Let us pause for a breath.' That single sentence modelled what the couple needed to learn. Therapists teach by what they do in the room, not only by what they say.

Let me point out what goes wrong. Therapists often chase the identified patient and ignore the marriage. They often focus on the individual's feelings and miss the family's pattern. They often promise confidentiality that they cannot keep. Each of these errors costs trust, and trust is expensive to rebuild.

Test yourself on ethics. Your client asks you to keep a secret from her husband that affects their marriage. Do you agree? No. You explain at intake that you do not hold secrets that affect the relationship, and you help her find the courage to tell the truth with support.

This week, practise the pause. Before you give advice in any conversation, wait five seconds and ask one question instead. Notice what changes. Most of us speak too soon; the pause is where the other person's wisdom gets a chance to appear.

MODULE 5: Drug Addiction
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Drug Addiction. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Drugs affect brain and behaviour. Families need education, safety, and treatment links. Address stigma with compassion. Recovery is possible with treatment and support.

Let me use a market example. A trader in Bodija market knows the price of every item and the mood of every customer. That is systems awareness. Drug Addiction asks you to bring the same awareness into the family. Who is tense today? Who is quiet? What changed since last week? Read the family the way a trader reads a market.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Jos lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of drug addiction became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. The therapist taught them a simple rule for the first month: "Nobody makes permanent decisions while the wound is fresh." That single rule reduced the fighting. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows drug addiction in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "On a scale of one to ten, how safe does this home feel to each of you?"
Wife: "Four."
Husband: "Seven."
Therapist: "Two different numbers under one roof. That is worth understanding. Wife, what makes it a four?"
Wife: "I never know his mood when he comes in."
Therapist: "Husband, what would it take for her number to rise by one point this week?"
Husband: "I can call her before I leave work."
Therapist: "That is a real, doable step. Let us test it this week."

Observe three skills in that exchange. First, joining: the therapist entered the family's world before asking anything of it. Second, reframing: she changed the meaning of the behaviour without denying the pain. Third, homework: she sent the family home with one small, clear task. Those three moves are worth more than a hundred clever interpretations.

Let me speak candidly. The most dangerous mistake is not a technique error; it is a character error. A therapist who gossips, exaggerates, or plays favourites will harm families no matter how many models he knows. Skill without integrity is a sharp knife in a child's hand.

Here is a question to stretch you. The grandmother is the most powerful person in the home, but she refuses to attend sessions. How do you work with the system without her in the room? You honour her through the family, you ask permission before changes, and you invite her through a respected messenger. Systems change through respect, not confrontation.

Practise this sentence this week: 'Help me understand.' Use it whenever you feel tempted to correct someone. Notice how the other person's body relaxes when they hear it. That sentence is a key that opens locked rooms.

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Gambling and Other Addictions. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Gambling, phone, and other addictions harm families financially and emotionally. Assess impact, teach boundaries, and connect to support. Technology addiction needs family rules.

Let me put it simply. In plain terms, Gambling and Other Addictions is the study of what happens between people, not just inside them. A man may be patient at work and impatient at home, and the difference is not in his character alone; it is in the patterns of the home. Change the pattern, and you often change the man.

Now, let us look at this in real life. Let me tell you about a family in Benin City. They came to therapy because the matter of gambling and other addictions had turned their home into a place of silence. The father would not speak at dinner. The mother cried in the kitchen. The children learned to become invisible. When the therapist finally had all of them in one room, the truth came out gently: nobody had ever been taught how to handle gambling and other addictions, so each person had invented a private strategy. Those private strategies were colliding every day. The therapist did not scold anyone. She drew their strategies on paper, and for the first time the family saw the same picture. That picture became the beginning of a new conversation.

You see, my students, this example shows gambling and other addictions in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You said your son is stubborn. Tell me about a time this week when he was not stubborn."
Mother: (pause) "On Sunday, when we cooked together."
Therapist: "What was different about Sunday?"
Mother: "I was not rushing. I was just with him."
Therapist: "So connection changed his behaviour before discipline did. That is a very important discovery. What would it take to have two Sundays in a week?"

Look closely at her language. She used 'we' more than 'you'. She said 'we will try something' rather than 'you must change'. That language builds a team. A family that feels teamed with the therapist will take risks; a family that feels examined will hide.

Let me warn you about three common errors. First, ignoring the body. A family in crisis may be hungry, exhausted, or sick, and no amount of talking will help until those needs are addressed. Second, ignoring the money. Many 'communication problems' are actually money problems wearing a mask. Third, ignoring the community. If the church, mosque, or extended family opposes the therapy, the therapy may fail before it begins.

Let me pose a case. A wife tells you in private that she is afraid of her husband, but in the joint session she says everything is fine. What do you do? You do not confront her publicly. You make a safety plan privately, you assess the danger carefully, and you never conduct joint sessions where violence is active without a plan.

This week, sit with one family and ask only one question: 'What is the one thing you wish we could change first?' Then be quiet. Let them fill the silence. Whatever they say first is usually the doorway to everything else.

MODULE 7: Motivational Interviewing
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Motivational Interviewing. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Use motivational interviewing to build readiness. Ask open questions, reflect, and support change. Do not argue. Help the user find their own reasons to change.

Let me give you the short version. Motivational Interviewing says that every family is trying to solve a problem, even when the solution looks like the problem. The husband who stays late at the office may be avoiding a fight. The wife who shouts may be trying to be heard. Look for the purpose behind the behaviour before you judge it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Ogbomosho, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving motivational interviewing. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows motivational interviewing in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Everyone in this family has been carrying something alone. Today I want us to put some of it down. Who will start?"
Grandmother: "I will. I am tired of being the one who keeps the peace."
Therapist: "Thank you for your courage. And when you keep the peace, who is freed from doing it?"
Grandmother: "Everyone else."
Therapist: "So your burden has become their permission to stay silent. Let us see if we can share that load differently."

Did you notice how she handled the resistance? She did not argue. She agreed with the part that was true and then asked a question that opened a door. That is the therapist's art: to be firm without being rigid, and gentle without being soft.

These mistakes are common and costly. Do not use technical words to impress. Do not take phone calls in session. Do not allow a session to run without a plan. Do not forget the homework from the previous week. Families notice whether you remember them, and remembering is a form of respect.

Here is a question for you. If a family says 'our son is the problem', what are three other places you would look before accepting that? Think: the marriage, the family rules, and the wider system of school and relatives. A therapist who looks only where the family points will often miss the real wound.

Your practical task is to observe a disagreement without intervening. Watch two people argue, in your family or at work, and notice the cycle: who pursues, who withdraws, who rescues. Write the cycle as a diagram. Bring it to class, with names changed, and we will study it together.

MODULE 8: Family Recovery Support

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Family Recovery Support. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Teach families to support recovery: encouragement, routines, and accountability. Celebrate small wins. Plan for relapse without shame. Relapse is part of recovery for many.

Here is another way of saying it. Family Recovery Support is about the rules nobody wrote down. Every family has them: who speaks first, who decides money, who is allowed to be angry, who must always be strong. Once you can name those unwritten rules, the family can decide whether to keep them or rewrite them.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Makurdi lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of family recovery support became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows family recovery support in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I am going to ask a strange question. If the problem disappeared tonight, what is the first small thing you would notice tomorrow morning?"
Husband: "She would greet me at the door."
Wife: "He would ask about my day."
Therapist: "So the miracle is not a mansion or a lottery win. It is a greeting and a question. That tells me your marriage is closer to healing than you think."

Notice how she ended. She did not end on the problem; she ended on a picture of the family's preferred future. 'Imagine next Sunday, what would be different?' The family left with hope in their hands. Hope is not decoration; it is the fuel of change.

Now, the mistakes. Do not interrupt a father's story to correct his theology. Do not correct a mother's grammar while she is pouring out her heart. Do not ask six questions at once. Do not fill every silence. Silence is often the moment a family is deciding to trust you, so let it breathe.

Here is a question about your own heart. Which family would you find hardest to treat, and why? The answer reveals your blind spot. A therapist who knows his blind spot can guard against it; a therapist who denies it will be ruled by it.

This week, do a listening audit. Count how many times you interrupt someone in a day. Do not scold yourself; just count. Awareness is the first step. Next week, try to reduce the number by half.

MODULE 9: Boundaries and Consequences
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Boundaries and Consequences. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Healthy boundaries protect the family. Teach families to set clear, kind boundaries: "We love you. We will not cover drinking. We will support treatment." Boundaries are love with limits.

Let me explain it as a doctor would. A doctor does not treat a symptom without examining the body. Boundaries and Consequences is the examination of the family body. The symptom is only the complaint at the door; the illness is in the circulation of love, respect, and truth between members. Treat the circulation, and the symptom often fades.

Now, let us look at this in real life. Let me tell you about a family in Zaria. They came to therapy because the matter of boundaries and consequences had turned their home into a place of silence. The father would not speak at dinner. The mother cried in the kitchen. The children learned to become invisible. When the therapist finally had all of them in one room, the truth came out gently: nobody had ever been taught how to handle boundaries and consequences, so each person had invented a private strategy. Those private strategies were colliding every day. The therapist did not scold anyone. She drew their strategies on paper, and for the first time the family saw the same picture. That picture became the beginning of a new conversation.

You see, my students, this example shows boundaries and consequences in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You have both said the word respect many times. What does respect look like in your house, in actions, not in theory?"
Wife: "He would not correct me in front of his mother."
Husband: "She would not discuss our money with her sister."
Therapist: "Now we have something specific. These are not feelings; these are agreements. Let us write them down and test them for two weeks."

Let me point out what she avoided. She did not ask 'Why did you do that?' which invites defence. She asked 'What happened just before that?' which invites observation. Small changes in question shape produce large changes in conversation.

Here is my counsel. Do not confuse culture with abuse. Do not confuse respect with silence. Do not confuse forgiveness with pretending nothing happened. Do not confuse a husband's provision with his presence. And do not confuse a wife's submission with her having no voice.

Check your thinking here. What is the difference between a boundary and a wall? A boundary says 'this is where I end and you begin, and I can still love you'. A wall says 'you cannot reach me'. Families often build walls and call them boundaries. Your task is to turn walls into doors.

Your assignment this week is small but powerful. Ask one family member, 'What is one thing I could do differently that would make you feel more respected?' Then listen without defending. Write down the answer word for word. Bring it to class. This exercise teaches you how it feels to be on the receiving end of your own questions.

MODULE 10: Children of Addicted Parents
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Children of Addicted Parents. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Children suffer in addiction homes. Protect them, give them support, and connect to school and counselling. Children need safety and love, not secrets.

Now, why does Children of Addicted Parents matter so much in our setting? Because in Nigeria, a family is rarely just two people. It is grandparents, uncles, aunties, in-laws, pastors, imams, and neighbours. Whatever we study in this course must be applied with that wider family in view. A therapy that ignores the extended family will be outvoted by it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Enugu, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving children of addicted parents. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows children of addicted parents in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I notice you have not looked at your husband since you sat down."
Wife: "There is nothing to look at."
Therapist: "That sentence carries a lot of pain. I am not here to judge it. I am here to understand it. When did you stop looking at him?"
Wife: (crying) "When he stopped looking at me."
Therapist: "Then let us begin exactly there. One look, one sentence, one honest moment."

Here is a subtle skill. She asked each member the same question in turn: 'What would you want the others to understand about you?' The quiet daughter spoke last and said the most important thing. Equal airtime is not a technique of fairness; it is a technique of discovery.

Here are the traps to avoid. Do not confuse understanding with agreement. You can understand why a husband shouts without approving of the shouting. Do not use your own marriage as the standard for every family. Do not quote research to argue with a grandmother; honour her first, then offer another view. And never promise a result you cannot control, such as 'your marriage will surely be saved'.

Consider this scenario. A father brings his teenage son for 'discipline problems' but refuses to discuss his own drinking. How do you proceed? You join with the father's concern, you keep the son's welfare central, and you gently raise the drinking as one part of the picture, without making it a condition of your respect.

Your task is to interview an elder. Ask an older person in your community: 'How were conflicts settled in your family when you were young? What did your parents do that you would keep, and what would you change?' Write one page. You will learn more about culture in one hour than in ten textbooks.

MODULE 11: Relapse Prevention
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Relapse Prevention. This is a very important topic in Addiction and Family Systems. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Teach families to identify triggers, plan responses, and build routines. Create a relapse plan: who to call, where to go, what to do. Planning prevents crisis.

Let me be practical. When Relapse Prevention is handled badly, the damage rarely stays with one person. It moves to the children, then to the children's children. When it is handled well, the benefit moves in exactly the same way. That is why your work in this course is not a small thing; it is generational work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Jos lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of relapse prevention became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. The therapist taught them a simple rule for the first month: "Nobody makes permanent decisions while the wound is fresh." That single rule reduced the fighting. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows relapse prevention in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your daughter says she feels unheard. What do you hear when she says that?"
Father: "I hear that I have failed as a father."
Therapist: "So her pain lands on your shame, and your shame responds with anger. Do you see the cycle?"
Father: "I do."
Therapist: "Good. Then the next time she speaks, try to hear her pain without hearing a verdict about you. That is the skill we will practise today."

Here is the skill to copy. She listened for the feeling behind the complaint. The wife said 'he is careless', but the therapist heard 'I am afraid I do not matter to him'. When she named that fear gently, the wife wept and the husband softened. Accurate empathy is a surgical instrument.

Let me speak plainly about errors. The worst mistake is making the family feel judged. Once shame fills the room, learning stops. The second worst is rescuing the family from every discomfort. Growth lives just beyond discomfort, and if you remove all discomfort, you remove the growth. Walk with them through the fire; do not carry them.

Ask yourself this. If you only had three questions to ask any family, what would they be? Here are mine: What has been happening? What have you already tried? What would be different if this were better? Those three questions give you a story, a history of solutions, and a goal.

Practise the phrase 'say more about that'. Use it three times a day in ordinary conversations. You will be amazed how much people have been waiting to say and how rarely they are invited.

You see, my students, this example shows community and faith support in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. Therapist: "Both of you have said you want the marriage to work. What has each of you done in the last month that made it a little better?"

Wife: "I stopped bringing my mother into our arguments."

PART TWO: REAL LIFE PRACTICALS - STEP BY STEP
================================================================

REAL LIFE PRACTICAL 1: Enabling Practice
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, theory without practice is empty. So let us do a real life practical on Enabling Practice. We will work through it in the style of the Ethics Decision Frame, which is one of the practical frames you will use in different settings.

Scenario: A wife covers her husband's drinking. Help her set a boundary with love. A family wants to support recovery. Create a weekly support plan with routines and accountability.

Let me walk you through this practical step by step, as you would do it in real life.

Step One: Name the ethical question in one sentence. For example: 'May I keep this information private when it may affect another person's safety?' Vague dilemmas cannot be resolved, so precision comes first.

Step Two: Gather the facts. Who is involved? What exactly was disclosed? What are the legal duties in your jurisdiction? What is known, and what is assumed? Write the facts separately from the fears.

Step Three: Identify the principles at stake. Autonomy, nonmaleficence (do no harm), beneficence (do good), justice, fidelity, and the client's own cultural and faith values. Where principles pull in different directions, say so honestly.

Step Four: Consult before deciding. Call a supervisor, a trusted senior colleague, or a professional body. Consultation is not indecision; it is diligence. Record whom you consulted and what was discussed.

Step Five: Choose the least harmful, most transparent course. Explain your reasoning to the client in plain language, including what you can keep private and what you must share. Document the conversation.

Step Six: Act, and protect the vulnerable first. If a child, a suicidal person, or a threatened third party is at risk, safety overrides the usual privacy rules, within the law.

Step Seven: Review the outcome and record what you learned. Ethical competence grows through reflection on hard decisions, not through memorising rules.

These steps take courage as much as skill. It takes courage to ask about violence, to name a secret, to sit with tears, and to say 'I do not know, but I will find out'. Courage can be practised, and this lab is that practice.

You see, this practical is not difficult. It needs patience, respect, and skill. If you follow these steps, you will help many families. Practice this practical with a classmate this week. One person will be the therapist, the others will be the family. Switch roles after twenty minutes. Write down what you learned.

Important note: Always respect culture and faith. In Nigeria and in many African homes, elders are respected, faith is important, and the family is extended. Do not fight culture. Work with culture. Use proverbs, prayers, and respectful language where appropriate. The family will accept help faster when they feel their values are honoured.

Sixth, what will you do next session? Tell the family exactly what you will do, what you hope they will do, and what you will celebrate if it works. Give hope, and end with encouragement.

PART THREE-B: DEEPER TEACHING NOTES AND EXTENDED EXPLANATIONS

My dear students, before we move to the techniques section, I want to go deeper with you. The notes that follow are extended teachings for this specific course. Listen slowly. Take notes. These are the ideas that separate a competent therapist from an excellent one.


DEEPER EXPLANATION ONE: ADDICTION IS A FAMILY DANCE
----------------------------------------------------------------

Addiction is a dance with many dancers. One person drinks, another covers, another shouts, another hides the money, another prays all night, and another pretends that all is well. The drinker is responsible for the drinking, but the family is responsible for the dance that keeps the drinking comfortable. Your job is not to put all the blame on the family; it is to show the family how its love has become a crutch. When the crutch is removed with compassion, the person with addiction is forced to face reality, and the family is freed from exhaustion.

DEEPER EXPLANATION TWO: THE HERO, THE SCAPEGOAT, AND THE LOST CHILD
----------------------------------------------------------------

In many homes affected by addiction, children take on roles. The hero tries to be perfect to keep the family's name clean. The scapegoat acts out and carries the family's anger. The lost child becomes invisible and quiet. The mascot makes jokes to ease tension. These roles are survival strategies. They help children cope, but they cost them their childhood. When you work with such a family, name the roles with kindness: "It seems you have been carrying the weight of being the good one. That is a heavy load for a child." Recognition often brings tears, and tears bring relief.

DEEPER EXPLANATION THREE: THE WIFE WHO COVERED THE DEBTS
----------------------------------------------------------------

A woman in Abuja had paid her husband's gambling debts three times. Each time, he promised to stop. Each time, he returned to the betting shop. The therapist did not tell her to leave him. The therapist helped her see that her covering was helping the gambling continue. Together, they wrote a boundary: "I love you. I will not pay another gambling debt. If the rent is short because of gambling, we will talk about a plan, but the money will not come quietly from my purse." She shook as she said it. The husband was angry for a week. Then he began to attend a support group, because the old soft landing was gone. The boundary was not cruelty. It was the first honest act of love in years.

DEEPER EXPLANATION FOUR: MOTIVATIONAL INTERVIEWING IN FIVE MINUTES
----------------------------------------------------------------

Motivational interviewing is not a lecture. It is a conversation that helps the person hear their own reasons for change. Ask, "What does drinking give you? What does it cost you? On a scale of one to ten, how ready are you to change? Why not a lower number?" That last question is powerful, because it invites the person to argue for their own readiness instead of against your advice. Reflect what you hear, summarise it, and ask, "What is one small step you are willing to take this week?" One small step, honestly chosen, is worth more than a hundred commands.

DEEPER EXPLANATION FIVE: RECOVERY INVOLVES THE WHOLE FAMILY
----------------------------------------------------------------

Recovery is not just stopping a substance. It is rebuilding trust, routines, and finances. The family needs education, support, and realistic expectations. Relapse may happen, and the family needs a plan for it that includes honesty without shame. Connect the family to support groups, faith communities, and medical care. No family recovers alone, and no therapist should carry the work alone either.

PRACTICAL FOR THIS WEEK: THE ENABLING AUDIT
----------------------------------------------------------------

Write down five things you or a family you know do to "help" someone whose behaviour is harming them. For each, ask: Does this action help the person face reality, or does it hide the consequences? Then write one boundary that would replace the enabling with honest love. Discuss your answers with a trusted friend or supervisor.

COMMON MISTAKES
----------------------------------------------------------------

Do not moralise or preach; shame fuels addiction. Do not promise secrecy where children are at risk. Do not demand that the family stop all contact; work towards safety, not isolation. Do not expect a straight line of recovery; progress comes in waves. And do not treat the person with addiction as the only client; the family is your client too.

PART FOUR: KEY TECHNIQUES YOU MUST MASTER
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, in Addiction and Family Systems you must master practical techniques. Theory is good, but techniques change lives. Let me list the key techniques for this course, and I will explain how to use each one.

Technique 1: Motivational Interviewing

How to use it: first explain why the technique exists, not only what it is. Families cooperate when they understand the purpose. Second, model it in a low-stakes example, such as how to greet a neighbour. Third, have the family rehearse the real situation while you coach gently. Fourth, ask, "What felt natural, and what felt strange?" Fifth, agree on a practice plan for the week.

Real life tip: use the family's own names and situations in the rehearsal. Practice that sounds like their real life transfers to their real life.

Common mistake: correcting too much too soon. Let the first attempt be imperfect. Confidence comes before polish.

Technique 2: Boundary Setting

How to use it: teach it in three sentences. One, what it is. Two, when to use it. Three, what to say. Then show it. Then have the family show you. Then ask each member to name one moment this week when they will use it. Write those moments down and review them next session.

Real life tip: ask a respected elder or pastor to endorse the technique publicly. Cultural endorsement speeds adoption dramatically.

Common mistake: using the technique only in the office. If it stays in the office, nothing changes at home.

Technique 3: Family Roles Work

How to use it: start with a story. Tell a short story of a family who used the technique and what changed. Stories lower resistance and create hope. Then walk through the steps on paper so the family can see them. Then practise in pairs inside the room, with one person as the coach. End by asking, "What would make this hard at home, and how will you handle that?"

Real life tip: keep a small collection of true, anonymised stories from your practice and community. Stories travel further than instructions.

Common mistake: ignoring the obstacles the family names. If they say "my husband will not participate", that is the real problem to solve before the technique can work.

Technique 4: Relapse Planning

How to use it: use the teach-back method. Explain the technique briefly, then ask the family to teach it back to you in their own words. If they cannot, the teaching was not clear enough. Then rehearse it once, and once more with a small obstacle added, such as someone interrupting. Finish with a written note in simple language that they can keep at home.

Real life tip: write the note in the language the family thinks in, or provide a short voice note if literacy is limited. Accessibility is respect.

Common mistake: assuming understanding because heads were nodding. Nodding is politeness; teach-back is proof.

Technique 5: Support Groups

How to use it: begin with a plain invitation. "This is a way to help you talk better. Let us try it together." Then demonstrate it yourself, with a colleague or with your own words. Let the family practise while you watch, and give kind, specific feedback. Send the skill home as a small assignment. A technique works only when it is practised at home.

Real life tip: many Nigerian families respect proverbs. Teach this technique with a short proverb so it is remembered long after the session.

Common mistake: do not stack techniques. One per session, mastered, is worth more than five half-learned.

Key Terms to Remember
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Enabling: behaviour that protects a person from the consequences of their harmful behaviour, thereby keeping the behaviour going.
- Codependency: a pattern where one person's life becomes organised around another person's problems, often seen in families affected by addiction.
- Recovery: the process of returning to health and function after illness, trauma, or addiction.
- Relapse: a return to a problem behaviour after a period of improvement; it is planned for, not stigmatised.
- Boundaries: the invisible lines that define who is responsible for what, and where one person's self ends and another's begins.
