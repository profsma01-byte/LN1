MODULE 1: Understanding Psychopathology
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Understanding Psychopathology. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Psychopathology is mental illness affecting thoughts, feelings, and behaviour. It is influenced by biology, psychology, and social factors. Families need understanding, not blame.

Let me say this as simply as I can. Understanding Psychopathology is not a label you paste on a person. It is a way of seeing how people move together, the way you would watch dancers and notice who leads, who follows, and who keeps stepping on whose toes. Once you can see the dance, you can teach a new step.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Enugu came to therapy because their eldest son had stopped eating with the family. The parents blamed school. The teachers blamed friends. The grandmother blamed witchcraft. The therapist asked about understanding psychopathology, and the mother began to weep. It emerged that the family had been carrying the matter of understanding psychopathology alone for two years, hiding it from relatives to protect the family name. The hiding had become heavier than the problem. When the therapist helped them speak about understanding psychopathology with one trusted aunt, the son started eating again the same week. Secrets are heavy loads, and sometimes the first intervention is simply to share the weight.

You see, my students, this example shows understanding psychopathology in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Thank you both for coming. Before we begin, let me say what I already see: two people who are still here, still trying. Who would like to start?"
Wife: "I am tired of being ignored."
Therapist: "Ignored. That is a strong word. Can you give me one recent moment when you felt ignored?"
Wife: "Last night. I spoke and he kept looking at his phone."
Therapist: "And when she spoke and you kept looking at the phone, what was happening for you?"
Husband: "I was reading work messages. I did not know she needed me."
Therapist: "So one of you needed attention and the other did not know it was being asked for. That gap is where we will work."

Notice what the therapist did in that dialogue. She did not rush to solve. She named the feeling, slowed the pace, and invited the family to look at the pattern together. That is the difference between advice and therapy. Advice tells people what to do. Therapy helps people see clearly enough to decide for themselves.

Now let us name the mistakes young therapists make here. The first is taking sides too early. The moment you sound like the wife's lawyer, the husband stops listening. The second is rushing to comfort. Tears are not an emergency; they are a language. Let the tears speak before you hand out tissues. The third is talking more than the family. If you are speaking for more than a third of the session, you are working alone.

Let me test your understanding. A husband complains that his wife no longer respects him, and the wife complains that he no longer talks to her. Which is the presenting problem, and which is the pattern? Write your answer before you read on. The pattern is this: he feels disrespected, so he withdraws; she feels abandoned, so she criticises; he feels more disrespected. Two complaints, one dance.

Here is your practical step for this week. Choose one family you know well, perhaps your own, and for three days write down one interaction at dinner. Who spoke first? Who was interrupted? Who left the table first? Do not judge; just record. Patterns appear when you look with a pencil in your hand.

MODULE 2: Depression in Families
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Depression in Families. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Depression affects energy, mood, and relationships. Families feel helpless and guilty. Teach families about depression, support without enabling, and encourage treatment.

Now let me explain it plainly, the way I would explain it to a market woman in Ibadan. Depression in Families simply means that people in a family carry each other. If one person carries too much, the others feel lighter but weaker. If one person lets go, everyone stumbles. Balance is the goal, not perfection.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Jos, a wife discovered that her husband had been hiding a large debt. The revelation triggered a crisis about depression in families that nearly ended the marriage. The therapist helped them do something counter-intuitive: instead of arguing about the money first, they spent a whole session on the question "What were you afraid would happen if you told me?" The husband said, "That you would see me as a failure." The wife said, "That you did not trust me." Neither of those fears was about money. Once the fears were spoken, the budget became a shared project instead of a courtroom.

You see, my students, this example shows depression in families in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "When the two of you argue about this, what do you hear in your mind?"
Husband: "I hear that I am a failure."
Therapist: "And what do you hear, madam?"
Wife: "I hear that I am not a good wife."
Therapist: "You see, you are not really fighting each other. You are fighting two old voices. Let us separate the voices from the people, and then we can talk about the actual problem."

Watch how she used the genogram. She did not use it as paperwork; she used it as a story map. 'Who else in this family carried this burden?' she asked. The family discovered a pattern across three generations. Insight that comes from the family's own history is far more powerful than insight imported from a textbook.

Watch out for these mistakes. Do not label a family 'resistant' when they are simply afraid. Do not diagnose from one session. Do not allow one person to describe the whole family for all the others. And do not treat every problem as a trauma or every sadness as depression. Good therapists are slow to name and quick to listen.

Ask yourself. When a family says 'we have tried everything', what have they not tried? Usually they have not tried consistency, or they have not tried kindness, or they have not tried asking for help in the right place. Explore the word 'everything' with curiosity rather than acceptance.

This week, build one ritual. Choose a family you work with or your own home, and create a fifteen-minute routine that happens at the same time every day: a walk, a prayer, a cup of tea with no phones. Rituals are small investments with large returns. Report what happened.

MODULE 3: Anxiety Disorders
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Anxiety Disorders. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Anxiety includes worry, panic, and avoidance. Families accommodate anxiety, which maintains it. Teach families to support exposure and coping, not avoidance.

Here is the simple English version. Anxiety Disorders means that behaviour in a family is not random. It follows a pattern, and patterns can be learned and unlearned. Nobody teaches a family to quarrel every Sunday, yet the quarrel appears like a visitor who knows the time. Therapy teaches the family to change the appointment.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Benin City came to therapy after a public embarrassment: a child's behaviour had been discussed in the church. The family was humiliated, and the matter of anxiety disorders had become a family secret guarded by silence. The therapist's first task was to separate shame from responsibility. Shame says, "You are bad." Responsibility says, "Something happened, and we can respond." Over several sessions, the family learned to speak about anxiety disorders in a way that was honest without being self-destructive. The church later became a source of support rather than judgement, because the family learned to ask for the right kind of help.

You see, my students, this example shows anxiety disorders in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Describe a normal Tuesday in your house."
Father: "We wake, we rush, we work, we come home, we sleep."
Therapist: "Where is the part of Tuesday when the family is actually together?"
Father: (silence)
Therapist: "That silence is important. It is telling us something. What would you like Tuesday to look like?"
Father: "I would like to eat with them."
Therapist: "Then let us build Tuesday around that meal."

Watch the therapist's moves. She used the family's own words instead of her textbook words. She asked one question at a time. She let the silence work. And when the emotion rose, she did not run from it; she stayed, steady as a wall, so the family could lean without falling.

Here is a list of errors to keep before your eyes. Do not assume a quiet family is a healthy family. Do not assume a loud family is a violent family. Do not assume that the person who called for help is the person who needs the most help. And do not assume the problem is new; often it has been growing quietly for years.

Here is a hard one. A couple wants you to tell them whether to divorce. What is your answer? You do not decide for them. You help them clarify their values, their options, their children's needs, and their readiness. Discernment is not advice-giving; it is accompanying a decision.

Your assignment is to write a letter you will never send. Write to a family member who hurt you, describing what you felt and what you needed. Do not send it. Then notice whether your body feels lighter. Unspoken pain becomes a therapist's blind spot; spoken pain becomes compassion.

MODULE 4: Schizophrenia and Psychosis
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Schizophrenia and Psychosis. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Schizophrenia affects thinking and perception. Families need psychoeducation, communication skills, and support. Medication and family support together improve outcomes.

In plain words, Schizophrenia and Psychosis is about who carries the weight and who carries the blame. In unhealthy families, the weakest person often carries both. In healthy families, the weight is shared and blame is replaced by responsibility. That shift is the heart of our work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Ogbomosho came to therapy because their eldest son had stopped eating with the family. The parents blamed school. The teachers blamed friends. The grandmother blamed witchcraft. The therapist asked about schizophrenia and psychosis, and the mother began to weep. It emerged that the family had been carrying the matter of schizophrenia and psychosis alone for two years, hiding it from relatives to protect the family name. The hiding had become heavier than the problem. When the therapist helped them speak about schizophrenia and psychosis with one trusted aunt, the son started eating again the same week. Secrets are heavy loads, and sometimes the first intervention is simply to share the weight.

You see, my students, this example shows schizophrenia and psychosis in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your mother called you lazy. What happened inside you when she said it?"
Teenager: "I stopped listening."
Therapist: "That is honest. And what would you have wanted her to say instead?"
Teenager: "That she is worried, not that I am useless."
Therapist: "That is a sentence we can work with. Mother, could you say what you actually feel, using his sentence as a guide?"
Mother: "I am worried about your future. I am afraid for you."
Teenager: "Then say that. I can hear that."

Observe the moment of repair. The husband raised his voice, and the therapist said calmly, 'Let us pause for a breath.' That single sentence modelled what the couple needed to learn. Therapists teach by what they do in the room, not only by what they say.

Let me point out what goes wrong. Therapists often chase the identified patient and ignore the marriage. They often focus on the individual's feelings and miss the family's pattern. They often promise confidentiality that they cannot keep. Each of these errors costs trust, and trust is expensive to rebuild.

Test yourself on ethics. Your client asks you to keep a secret from her husband that affects their marriage. Do you agree? No. You explain at intake that you do not hold secrets that affect the relationship, and you help her find the courage to tell the truth with support.

This week, practise the pause. Before you give advice in any conversation, wait five seconds and ask one question instead. Notice what changes. Most of us speak too soon; the pause is where the other person's wisdom gets a chance to appear.

MODULE 5: Bipolar Disorder
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Bipolar Disorder. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Bipolar disorder involves mood swings. Families need education about cycles, early warning signs, and crisis plans. Support stability with routines and treatment.

Let me use a market example. A trader in Bodija market knows the price of every item and the mood of every customer. That is systems awareness. Bipolar Disorder asks you to bring the same awareness into the family. Who is tense today? Who is quiet? What changed since last week? Read the family the way a trader reads a market.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Makurdi, a wife discovered that her husband had been hiding a large debt. The revelation triggered a crisis about bipolar disorder that nearly ended the marriage. The therapist helped them do something counter-intuitive: instead of arguing about the money first, they spent a whole session on the question "What were you afraid would happen if you told me?" The husband said, "That you would see me as a failure." The wife said, "That you did not trust me." Neither of those fears was about money. Once the fears were spoken, the budget became a shared project instead of a courtroom.

You see, my students, this example shows bipolar disorder in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "On a scale of one to ten, how safe does this home feel to each of you?"
Wife: "Four."
Husband: "Seven."
Therapist: "Two different numbers under one roof. That is worth understanding. Wife, what makes it a four?"
Wife: "I never know his mood when he comes in."
Therapist: "Husband, what would it take for her number to rise by one point this week?"
Husband: "I can call her before I leave work."
Therapist: "That is a real, doable step. Let us test it this week."

Observe three skills in that exchange. First, joining: the therapist entered the family's world before asking anything of it. Second, reframing: she changed the meaning of the behaviour without denying the pain. Third, homework: she sent the family home with one small, clear task. Those three moves are worth more than a hundred clever interpretations.

Let me speak candidly. The most dangerous mistake is not a technique error; it is a character error. A therapist who gossips, exaggerates, or plays favourites will harm families no matter how many models he knows. Skill without integrity is a sharp knife in a child's hand.

Here is a question to stretch you. The grandmother is the most powerful person in the home, but she refuses to attend sessions. How do you work with the system without her in the room? You honour her through the family, you ask permission before changes, and you invite her through a respected messenger. Systems change through respect, not confrontation.

Practise this sentence this week: 'Help me understand.' Use it whenever you feel tempted to correct someone. Notice how the other person's body relaxes when they hear it. That sentence is a key that opens locked rooms.

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Trauma and PTSD. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

PTSD affects families through symptoms and avoidance. Teach families about trauma, safety, and support. Refer for trauma-focused care.

Let me put it simply. In plain terms, Trauma and PTSD is the study of what happens between people, not just inside them. A man may be patient at work and impatient at home, and the difference is not in his character alone; it is in the patterns of the home. Change the pattern, and you often change the man.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Zaria came to therapy after a public embarrassment: a child's behaviour had been discussed in the church. The family was humiliated, and the matter of trauma and PTSD had become a family secret guarded by silence. The therapist's first task was to separate shame from responsibility. Shame says, "You are bad." Responsibility says, "Something happened, and we can respond." Over several sessions, the family learned to speak about trauma and PTSD in a way that was honest without being self-destructive. The church later became a source of support rather than judgement, because the family learned to ask for the right kind of help.

You see, my students, this example shows trauma and PTSD in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You said your son is stubborn. Tell me about a time this week when he was not stubborn."
Mother: (pause) "On Sunday, when we cooked together."
Therapist: "What was different about Sunday?"
Mother: "I was not rushing. I was just with him."
Therapist: "So connection changed his behaviour before discipline did. That is a very important discovery. What would it take to have two Sundays in a week?"

Look closely at her language. She used 'we' more than 'you'. She said 'we will try something' rather than 'you must change'. That language builds a team. A family that feels teamed with the therapist will take risks; a family that feels examined will hide.

Let me warn you about three common errors. First, ignoring the body. A family in crisis may be hungry, exhausted, or sick, and no amount of talking will help until those needs are addressed. Second, ignoring the money. Many 'communication problems' are actually money problems wearing a mask. Third, ignoring the community. If the church, mosque, or extended family opposes the therapy, the therapy may fail before it begins.

Let me pose a case. A wife tells you in private that she is afraid of her husband, but in the joint session she says everything is fine. What do you do? You do not confront her publicly. You make a safety plan privately, you assess the danger carefully, and you never conduct joint sessions where violence is active without a plan.

This week, sit with one family and ask only one question: 'What is the one thing you wish we could change first?' Then be quiet. Let them fill the silence. Whatever they say first is usually the doorway to everything else.

Personality traits affect relationships. Families need boundaries, communication skills, and realistic expectations. Therapy focuses on patterns, not labels.

Let me give you the short version. Personality and Relationship Problems says that every family is trying to solve a problem, even when the solution looks like the problem. The husband who stays late at the office may be avoiding a fight. The wife who shouts may be trying to be heard. Look for the purpose behind the behaviour before you judge it.

The hiding had become heavier than the problem. When the therapist helped them speak about personality and relationship problems with one trusted aunt, the son started eating again the same week. Secrets are heavy loads, and sometimes the first intervention is simply to share the weight.

You see, my students, this example shows personality and relationship problems in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Everyone in this family has been carrying something alone. Today I want us to put some of it down. Who will start?"
Grandmother: "I will. I am tired of being the one who keeps the peace."
Therapist: "Thank you for your courage. And when you keep the peace, who is freed from doing it?"
Grandmother: "Everyone else."
Therapist: "So your burden has become their permission to stay silent. Let us see if we can share that load differently."

Did you notice how she handled the resistance? She did not argue. She agreed with the part that was true and then asked a question that opened a door. That is the therapist's art: to be firm without being rigid, and gentle without being soft.

These mistakes are common and costly. Do not use technical words to impress. Do not take phone calls in session. Do not allow a session to run without a plan. Do not forget the homework from the previous week. Families notice whether you remember them, and remembering is a form of respect.

Here is a question for you. If a family says 'our son is the problem', what are three other places you would look before accepting that? Think: the marriage, the family rules, and the wider system of school and relatives. A therapist who looks only where the family points will often miss the real wound.

Your practical task is to observe a disagreement without intervening. Watch two people argue, in your family or at work, and notice the cycle: who pursues, who withdraws, who rescues. Write the cycle as a diagram. Bring it to class, with names changed, and we will study it together.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I am going to ask a strange question. If the problem disappeared tonight, what is the first small thing you would notice tomorrow morning?"
Husband: "She would greet me at the door."
Wife: "He would ask about my day."
Therapist: "So the miracle is not a mansion or a lottery win. It is a greeting and a question. That tells me your marriage is closer to healing than you think."

Notice how she ended. She did not end on the problem; she ended on a picture of the family's preferred future. 'Imagine next Sunday, what would be different?' The family left with hope in their hands. Hope is not decoration; it is the fuel of change.

Now, the mistakes. Do not interrupt a father's story to correct his theology. Do not correct a mother's grammar while she is pouring out her heart. Do not ask six questions at once. Do not fill every silence. Silence is often the moment a family is deciding to trust you, so let it breathe.

Here is a question about your own heart. Which family would you find hardest to treat, and why? The answer reveals your blind spot. A therapist who knows his blind spot can guard against it; a therapist who denies it will be ruled by it.

This week, do a listening audit. Count how many times you interrupt someone in a day. Do not scold yourself; just count. Awareness is the first step. Next week, try to reduce the number by half.

Let me explain it as a doctor would. A doctor does not treat a symptom without examining the body. Neurodevelopmental Disorders is the examination of the family body. The symptom is only the complaint at the door; the illness is in the circulation of love, respect, and truth between members. Treat the circulation, and the symptom often fades.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Benin City came to therapy after a public embarrassment: a child's behaviour had been discussed in the church. The family was humiliated, and the matter of neurodevelopmental disorders had become a family secret guarded by silence. The therapist's first task was to separate shame from responsibility. Shame says, "You are bad." Responsibility says, "Something happened, and we can respond." Over several sessions, the family learned to speak about neurodevelopmental disorders in a way that was honest without being self-destructive. The church later became a source of support rather than judgement, because the family learned to ask for the right kind of help.

You see, my students, this example shows neurodevelopmental disorders in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You have both said the word respect many times. What does respect look like in your house, in actions, not in theory?"
Wife: "He would not correct me in front of his mother."
Husband: "She would not discuss our money with her sister."
Therapist: "Now we have something specific. These are not feelings; these are agreements. Let us write them down and test them for two weeks."

Let me point out what she avoided. She did not ask 'Why did you do that?' which invites defence. She asked 'What happened just before that?' which invites observation. Small changes in question shape produce large changes in conversation.

Here is my counsel. Do not confuse culture with abuse. Do not confuse respect with silence. Do not confuse forgiveness with pretending nothing happened. Do not confuse a husband's provision with his presence. And do not confuse a wife's submission with her having no voice.

Check your thinking here. What is the difference between a boundary and a wall? A boundary says 'this is where I end and you begin, and I can still love you'. A wall says 'you cannot reach me'. Families often build walls and call them boundaries. Your task is to turn walls into doors.

Your assignment this week is small but powerful. Ask one family member, 'What is one thing I could do differently that would make you feel more respected?' Then listen without defending. Write down the answer word for word. Bring it to class. This exercise teaches you how it feels to be on the receiving end of your own questions.

MODULE 10: Stigma and Culture
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Stigma and Culture. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Stigma harms families. Cultural beliefs about mental illness vary. Teach families with respect for culture and faith. Reduce stigma with education.

Now, why does Stigma and Culture matter so much in our setting? Because in Nigeria, a family is rarely just two people. It is grandparents, uncles, aunties, in-laws, pastors, imams, and neighbours. Whatever we study in this course must be applied with that wider family in view. A therapy that ignores the extended family will be outvoted by it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Ogbomosho came to therapy because their eldest son had stopped eating with the family. The parents blamed school. The teachers blamed friends. The grandmother blamed witchcraft. The therapist asked about stigma and culture, and the mother began to weep. It emerged that the family had been carrying the matter of stigma and culture alone for two years, hiding it from relatives to protect the family name. The hiding had become heavier than the problem. When the therapist helped them speak about stigma and culture with one trusted aunt, the son started eating again the same week. Secrets are heavy loads, and sometimes the first intervention is simply to share the weight.

You see, my students, this example shows stigma and culture in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I notice you have not looked at your husband since you sat down."
Wife: "There is nothing to look at."
Therapist: "That sentence carries a lot of pain. I am not here to judge it. I am here to understand it. When did you stop looking at him?"
Wife: (crying) "When he stopped looking at me."
Therapist: "Then let us begin exactly there. One look, one sentence, one honest moment."

Here is a subtle skill. She asked each member the same question in turn: 'What would you want the others to understand about you?' The quiet daughter spoke last and said the most important thing. Equal airtime is not a technique of fairness; it is a technique of discovery.

Here are the traps to avoid. Do not confuse understanding with agreement. You can understand why a husband shouts without approving of the shouting. Do not use your own marriage as the standard for every family. Do not quote research to argue with a grandmother; honour her first, then offer another view. And never promise a result you cannot control, such as 'your marriage will surely be saved'.

Consider this scenario. A father brings his teenage son for 'discipline problems' but refuses to discuss his own drinking. How do you proceed? You join with the father's concern, you keep the son's welfare central, and you gently raise the drinking as one part of the picture, without making it a condition of your respect.

Your task is to interview an elder. Ask an older person in your community: 'How were conflicts settled in your family when you were young? What did your parents do that you would keep, and what would you change?' Write one page. You will learn more about culture in one hour than in ten textbooks.

MODULE 11: Family Psychoeducation
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Family Psychoeducation. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Psychoeducation teaches families about illness, treatment, and support. Knowledge reduces blame and builds cooperation. It is a core family intervention.

Let me be practical. When Family Psychoeducation is handled badly, the damage rarely stays with one person. It moves to the children, then to the children's children. When it is handled well, the benefit moves in exactly the same way. That is why your work in this course is not a small thing; it is generational work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Makurdi, a wife discovered that her husband had been hiding a large debt. The revelation triggered a crisis about family psychoeducation that nearly ended the marriage. The therapist helped them do something counter-intuitive: instead of arguing about the money first, they spent a whole session on the question "What were you afraid would happen if you told me?" The husband said, "That you would see me as a failure." The wife said, "That you did not trust me." Neither of those fears was about money. Once the fears were spoken, the budget became a shared project instead of a courtroom.

You see, my students, this example shows family psychoeducation in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your daughter says she feels unheard. What do you hear when she says that?"
Father: "I hear that I have failed as a father."
Therapist: "So her pain lands on your shame, and your shame responds with anger. Do you see the cycle?"
Father: "I do."
Therapist: "Good. Then the next time she speaks, try to hear her pain without hearing a verdict about you. That is the skill we will practise today."

Here is the skill to copy. She listened for the feeling behind the complaint. The wife said 'he is careless', but the therapist heard 'I am afraid I do not matter to him'. When she named that fear gently, the wife wept and the husband softened. Accurate empathy is a surgical instrument.

Let me speak plainly about errors. The worst mistake is making the family feel judged. Once shame fills the room, learning stops. The second worst is rescuing the family from every discomfort. Growth lives just beyond discomfort, and if you remove all discomfort, you remove the growth. Walk with them through the fire; do not carry them.

Ask yourself this. If you only had three questions to ask any family, what would they be? Here are mine: What has been happening? What have you already tried? What would be different if this were better? Those three questions give you a story, a history of solutions, and a goal.

Practise the phrase 'say more about that'. Use it three times a day in ordinary conversations. You will be amazed how much people have been waiting to say and how rarely they are invited.

MODULE 12: Collaborative Care
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Collaborative Care. This is a very important topic in Psychopathology in Family Contexts. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Family therapists collaborate with psychiatrists, psychologists, and medical doctors. Team care improves outcomes. Communicate with consent.

I want you to notice something important. What we are calling Collaborative Care here is not a theoretical idea you will admire and forget. It is a lens that changes what you see in the first ten minutes of a session. Two therapists can sit in the same room with the same family; the one who understands Collaborative Care will see a pattern, while the other will only see a problem.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Zaria came to therapy after a public embarrassment: a child's behaviour had been discussed in the church. The family was humiliated, and the matter of collaborative care had become a family secret guarded by silence. The therapist's first task was to separate shame from responsibility. Shame says, "You are bad." Responsibility says, "Something happened, and we can respond." Over several sessions, the family learned to speak about collaborative care in a way that was honest without being self-destructive. The church later became a source of support rather than judgement, because the family learned to ask for the right kind of help.

You see, my students, this example shows collaborative care in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Both of you have said you want the marriage to work. What has each of you done in the last month that made it a little better?"
Husband: "I stopped shouting."
Wife: "I stopped bringing my mother into our arguments."
Therapist: "So you are both already working. That means I am not starting from zero. I am joining a project that is already underway."

Watch the therapist handle the secret. When the mother hinted at something painful, the therapist did not pounce. She said, 'There seems to be something heavy here. We do not have to open it today, but when you are ready, I will be here.' Safety was preserved and the door was left open.

Let me caution you about three things. First, the rescue fantasy: believing you must save the family yourself. You are a guide, not a saviour. Second, the expert trap: speaking as if you have never struggled at home. Humility opens doors that expertise cannot. Third, the speed trap: rushing change to prove you are effective. Slow, steady change lasts.

Think about this. A couple has stopped fighting. Is that progress? Not always. Some couples stop fighting because they have given up. Check for warmth, humour, and touch. Peace without connection can be the silence before separation.

Do this exercise. Draw a simple map of your own family of origin, showing who was close, who was distant, and who carried the most responsibility. Then write one sentence about how that map affects your work with families today. Self-knowledge is not optional for a therapist; it is equipment.

That question has rebuilt more homes than any lecture.

You see, my students, this example shows ethics and safety in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Let us talk about money, but not about who is right. When you were growing up, what did your family teach you about money?"
Husband: "That a man must provide, no matter what."
Wife: "That a woman must save, because men cannot be trusted with money."
Therapist: "Now I understand the fight. You are not arguing about naira. You are arguing about two childhoods. Let us negotiate between those childhoods."

Avoid these pitfalls. Do not make the eldest child your co-therapist. Do not discuss a family's case in the church corridor. Do not accept a gift that makes you owe the family a favourable report. Do not let a session become a tribunal where the absent person is tried and convicted.

Strong answers respect his belief, name his courage in coming, and offer therapy as a form of strength rather than a confession of weakness.

REAL LIFE PRACTICAL 1: Psychoeducation Practice
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, theory without practice is empty. So let us do a real life practical on Psychoeducation Practice. We will work through it in the style of the Group Facilitation Frame, which is one of the practical frames you will use in different settings.

Scenario: Teach a family about depression. Use simple words and cultural respect. A family blames a member with schizophrenia. How do you reduce blame with education?

Let me walk you through this practical step by step, as you would do it in real life.

Step One: Prepare the room and the frame. Chairs in a circle, a visible agenda, agreed time boundaries, and a simple confidentiality statement read aloud.

Step Two: Welcome each member by name and invite a one-line check-in. 'My name, my family, and one word for how I am arriving today.' This lowers anxiety quickly.

Step Three: Teach one idea, briefly. Ten minutes of teaching, then practice. Adults learn groups by doing, not by listening to long lectures.

Step Four: Facilitate, do not lecture. Use rounds, pairs, and small-group exercises. Draw out the quiet members and gently limit the dominant ones.

Step Five: Handle emotion and conflict with structure. Name the tension, remind the group of the rules, and invite the group to respond as a community rather than as a jury.

Step Six: Close with appreciation and a task. Each member names one thing they will try and one person they will tell. Accountability turns insight into action.

Step Seven: Debrief yourself and the co-leader. What happened in the group's process, and what did it mirror from the families they live with?

These steps take courage as much as skill. It takes courage to ask about violence, to name a secret, to sit with tears, and to say 'I do not know, but I will find out'. Courage can be practised, and this lab is that practice.

You see, this practical is not difficult. It needs patience, respect, and skill. If you follow these steps, you will help many families. Practice this practical with a classmate this week. One person will be the therapist, the others will be the family. Switch roles after twenty minutes. Write down what you learned.

Important note: Always respect culture and faith. In Nigeria and in many African homes, elders are respected, faith is important, and the family is extended. Do not fight culture. Work with culture. Use proverbs, prayers, and respectful language where appropriate. The family will accept help faster when they feel their values are honoured.

Fifth, what is your ethical concern? Is there abuse? Is there danger? Is a referral needed? Protect life first. If there is violence, make a safety plan. If there is mental illness, refer. Ethics is protection, not paperwork.

Sixth, what will you do next session? Tell the family exactly what you will do, what you hope they will do, and what you will celebrate if it works. Give hope, and end with encouragement.

PART THREE-B: DEEPER TEACHING NOTES AND EXTENDED EXPLANATIONS

DEEPER EXPLANATION ONE: THE FAMILY FEELS THE ILLNESS TOO
----------------------------------------------------------------

When one person has a mental illness, the whole family lives with it. A mother with depression may not cook for weeks, and the children may feel unloved. A father with psychosis may shout at night, and the family may live in fear. A husband with bipolar disorder may spend money wildly in a manic episode, and the wife may feel betrayed. Families need two things: education and support. Education removes blame. Support removes isolation. Together, they turn a family from a confused bystander into a capable partner in care.

DEEPER EXPLANATION TWO: WHAT FAMILIES SHOULD NOT DO
----------------------------------------------------------------

Families often do things that feel helpful but make things worse. They hide the illness from relatives and neighbours, which deepens shame. They argue with delusions, which increases agitation. They pay for every consequence of a manic spending spree without limits, which removes the natural pressure to seek treatment. They lecture a depressed person to "be strong," which adds guilt to pain. A good therapist does not shame families for these mistakes; instead, the therapist gently offers better alternatives: honest education, calm communication, clear boundaries, and consistent treatment.

DEEPER EXPLANATION THREE: THE FAMILY WITH A SON IN PSYCHOSIS
----------------------------------------------------------------

A family in Enugu brought their twenty-year-old son, who believed that neighbours were poisoning his food. He had stopped school and refused to eat. The family was exhausted and frightened. The therapist did four things. First, she listened to the family's fear and praised their courage in coming. Second, she explained psychosis in simple words: "His brain is sending false alarms. He is not choosing this." Third, she helped the family respond calmly to his fears without agreeing with them: "I hear that you feel afraid. I am here with you." Fourth, she connected the family to psychiatric care and helped them build a daily routine of meals, medication, and rest. Recovery was slow, but the family was no longer alone, and the son was no longer blamed.

DEEPER EXPLANATION FOUR: THE STIGMA WALL
----------------------------------------------------------------

Stigma is a wall between families and help. In many communities, mental illness is attributed to spiritual attack, curses, or sin. A wise therapist does not mock these beliefs. Instead, the therapist respects the family's faith, prays with them if appropriate, and gently adds medical knowledge: "Faith and medicine can work together. God gave us doctors too." When faith and medicine are framed as partners rather than rivals, families accept help more readily.

PRACTICAL FOR THIS WEEK: THE FAMILY PSYCHOEDUCATION SESSION
----------------------------------------------------------------

Choose one condition, such as depression. Prepare a fifteen-minute psychoeducation session in simple English for a family. Include: what it is, what causes it, what it is not, what helps, what family members can do, and when to seek urgent help. Present it to your study group and invite questions. The ability to teach simply is a core clinical skill.

COMMON MISTAKES
----------------------------------------------------------------

Do not blame the family for the illness. Do not argue with delusions. Do not minimise medication or dismiss it. Do not treat mental illness as a spiritual failure. Do not work outside your competence; collaborate with psychiatrists and refer when needed. And do not forget the siblings and children, who often carry silent burdens while everyone focuses on the identified patient.

PART FOUR: KEY TECHNIQUES YOU MUST MASTER
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, in Psychopathology in Family Contexts you must master practical techniques. Theory is good, but techniques change lives. Let me list the key techniques for this course, and I will explain how to use each one.

Technique 1: Psychoeducation

How to use it: first explain why the technique exists, not only what it is. Families cooperate when they understand the purpose. Second, model it in a low-stakes example, such as how to greet a neighbour. Third, have the family rehearse the real situation while you coach gently. Fourth, ask, "What felt natural, and what felt strange?" Fifth, agree on a practice plan for the week.

Real life tip: use the family's own names and situations in the rehearsal. Practice that sounds like their real life transfers to their real life.

Common mistake: correcting too much too soon. Let the first attempt be imperfect. Confidence comes before polish.

Technique 2: Communication Skills

How to use it: teach it in three sentences. One, what it is. Two, when to use it. Three, what to say. Then show it. Then have the family show you. Then ask each member to name one moment this week when they will use it. Write those moments down and review them next session.

Real life tip: ask a respected elder or pastor to endorse the technique publicly. Cultural endorsement speeds adoption dramatically.

Common mistake: using the technique only in the office. If it stays in the office, nothing changes at home.

Technique 3: Crisis Planning

How to use it: start with a story. Tell a short story of a family who used the technique and what changed. Stories lower resistance and create hope. Then walk through the steps on paper so the family can see them. Then practise in pairs inside the room, with one person as the coach. End by asking, "What would make this hard at home, and how will you handle that?"

Real life tip: keep a small collection of true, anonymised stories from your practice and community. Stories travel further than instructions.

Common mistake: ignoring the obstacles the family names. If they say "my husband will not participate", that is the real problem to solve before the technique can work.

Technique 4: Collaboration

How to use it: use the teach-back method. Explain the technique briefly, then ask the family to teach it back to you in their own words. If they cannot, the teaching was not clear enough. Then rehearse it once, and once more with a small obstacle added, such as someone interrupting. Finish with a written note in simple language that they can keep at home.

Real life tip: write the note in the language the family thinks in, or provide a short voice note if literacy is limited. Accessibility is respect.

Common mistake: assuming understanding because heads were nodding. Nodding is politeness; teach-back is proof.

Technique 5: Stigma Reduction

How to use it: begin with a plain invitation. "This is a way to help you talk better. Let us try it together." Then demonstrate it yourself, with a colleague or with your own words. Let the family practise while you watch, and give kind, specific feedback. Send the skill home as a small assignment. A technique works only when it is practised at home.

Real life tip: many Nigerian families respect proverbs. Teach this technique with a short proverb so it is remembered long after the session.

Common mistake: do not stack techniques. One per session, mastered, is worth more than five half-learned.

4. Faith and family are pillars; build with them, not against them.
5. Document, consult, and never practise beyond your competence.

I encourage you, my dear students. Do not be discouraged when a family does not change quickly. Change takes time. Be patient. Be kind. Be prayerful. Your work matters: you are helping marriages, protecting children, and healing communities. God bless you as you continue.

Key Terms to Remember
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Depression: a persistent low mood with loss of interest, changes in sleep and appetite, and reduced energy; it affects the whole family.
- Anxiety: a state of worry and bodily tension about a threat that may or may not happen; it becomes a problem when it limits daily life.
- Schizophrenia: a serious mental illness involving disturbances in thinking, perception, and behaviour; families need education and support.
- Stigma: negative social judgement attached to a condition or family situation, which stops people seeking help.
- Psychoeducation: teaching families about a condition, its effects, and what helps, in clear and simple language.
