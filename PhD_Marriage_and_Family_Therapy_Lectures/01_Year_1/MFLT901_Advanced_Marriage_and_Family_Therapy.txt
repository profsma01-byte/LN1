MODULE 1: The Systems View of Marriage
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to The Systems View of Marriage. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Marriage is not two individuals alone. It is a system of two people, their children, their extended families, their faith, their work, and their community. When one part changes, all parts feel it. A good therapist looks at the whole system, not only at one person.

Let me say this as simply as I can. The Systems View of Marriage is not a label you paste on a person. It is a way of seeing how people move together, the way you would watch dancers and notice who leads, who follows, and who keeps stepping on whose toes. Once you can see the dance, you can teach a new step.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Port Harcourt, a husband and wife came for help with the systems view of marriage. The husband was a deacon; the wife was a teacher. Both were respected in church. In public they smiled; at home they barely spoke. The therapist named this gap with kindness: "You are two good people living two separate lives." The wife cried first. The husband followed. What emerged was not scandal but exhaustion, and a shared fear of disappointing their families. Therapy for the systems view of marriage became, in practice, permission for two tired people to be honest. Honesty, in their case, was the healing.

You see, my students, this example shows the systems view of marriage in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Thank you both for coming. Before we begin, let me say what I already see: two people who are still here, still trying. Who would like to start?"
Wife: "I am tired of being ignored."
Therapist: "Ignored. That is a strong word. Can you give me one recent moment when you felt ignored?"
Wife: "Last night. I spoke and he kept looking at his phone."
Therapist: "And when she spoke and you kept looking at the phone, what was happening for you?"
Husband: "I was reading work messages. I did not know she needed me."
Therapist: "So one of you needed attention and the other did not know it was being asked for. That gap is where we will work."

Notice what the therapist did in that dialogue. She did not rush to solve. She named the feeling, slowed the pace, and invited the family to look at the pattern together. That is the difference between advice and therapy. Advice tells people what to do. Therapy helps people see clearly enough to decide for themselves.

Now let us name the mistakes young therapists make here. The first is taking sides too early. The moment you sound like the wife's lawyer, the husband stops listening. The second is rushing to comfort. Tears are not an emergency; they are a language. Let the tears speak before you hand out tissues. The third is talking more than the family. If you are speaking for more than a third of the session, you are working alone.

Let me test your understanding. A husband complains that his wife no longer respects him, and the wife complains that he no longer talks to her. Which is the presenting problem, and which is the pattern? Write your answer before you read on. The pattern is this: he feels disrespected, so he withdraws; she feels abandoned, so she criticises; he feels more disrespected. Two complaints, one dance.

Here is your practical step for this week. Choose one family you know well, perhaps your own, and for three days write down one interaction at dinner. Who spoke first? Who was interrupted? Who left the table first? Do not judge; just record. Patterns appear when you look with a pencil in your hand.

Quick reflection: Write in your notebook three ways The Systems View of Marriage applies to families in your community. Bring your answer to class next week. We will discuss together.


MODULE 2: Assessment of Couples
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Assessment of Couples. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Advanced assessment means you gather information from all partners, observe interaction, and understand patterns. You ask about history, strengths, problems, and goals. You use genograms, questionnaires, and observation. Assessment is not testing alone. It is understanding the story of the couple.

Now let me explain it plainly, the way I would explain it to a market woman in Ibadan. Assessment of Couples simply means that people in a family carry each other. If one person carries too much, the others feel lighter but weaker. If one person lets go, everyone stumbles. Balance is the goal, not perfection.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A pastor in Onitsha came to therapy secretly, ashamed that he needed help. His marriage was cold, his children were distant, and the matter of assessment of couples had become a private torment. He said, "I counsel others; who counsels me?" The therapist honoured his courage and helped him see that asking for help is not the opposite of faith; it is a form of honesty. Over several months, he learned to bring the same pastoral patience home. His wife later said, "The man who came back to us was the man I married." Ministry had been consuming the minister, and therapy returned him to his own house.

You see, my students, this example shows assessment of couples in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "When the two of you argue about this, what do you hear in your mind?"
Husband: "I hear that I am a failure."
Therapist: "And what do you hear, madam?"
Wife: "I hear that I am not a good wife."
Therapist: "You see, you are not really fighting each other. You are fighting two old voices. Let us separate the voices from the people, and then we can talk about the actual problem."

Watch how she used the genogram. She did not use it as paperwork; she used it as a story map. 'Who else in this family carried this burden?' she asked. The family discovered a pattern across three generations. Insight that comes from the family's own history is far more powerful than insight imported from a textbook.

Watch out for these mistakes. Do not label a family 'resistant' when they are simply afraid. Do not diagnose from one session. Do not allow one person to describe the whole family for all the others. And do not treat every problem as a trauma or every sadness as depression. Good therapists are slow to name and quick to listen.

Ask yourself. When a family says 'we have tried everything', what have they not tried? Usually they have not tried consistency, or they have not tried kindness, or they have not tried asking for help in the right place. Explore the word 'everything' with curiosity rather than acceptance.

This week, build one ritual. Choose a family you work with or your own home, and create a fifteen-minute routine that happens at the same time every day: a walk, a prayer, a cup of tea with no phones. Rituals are small investments with large returns. Report what happened.

MODULE 3: The Couple Life Cycle
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to The Couple Life Cycle. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Couples move through stages: dating, early marriage, childbearing, school age, adolescence, launching, and later life. Each stage has new tasks and new stress. Problems often appear at stage changes. A good therapist helps couples prepare for the next stage with peace.

Here is the simple English version. The Couple Life Cycle means that behaviour in a family is not random. It follows a pattern, and patterns can be learned and unlearned. Nobody teaches a family to quarrel every Sunday, yet the quarrel appears like a visitor who knows the time. Therapy teaches the family to change the appointment.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Kaduna, a young couple married for two years came to therapy because of the couple life cycle. They had expected marriage to be easy, and they were ashamed that it was not. The therapist normalised their struggle: "Every marriage has a first crisis. The crisis is not the problem. The silence around the crisis is the problem." They learned a simple weekly ritual: twenty minutes, no phones, three questions each. What went well? What was hard? What do you need from me? Within two months, the shame had lifted, and the marriage had a structure for repair.

You see, my students, this example shows the couple life cycle in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Describe a normal Tuesday in your house."
Father: "We wake, we rush, we work, we come home, we sleep."
Therapist: "Where is the part of Tuesday when the family is actually together?"
Father: (silence)
Therapist: "That silence is important. It is telling us something. What would you like Tuesday to look like?"
Father: "I would like to eat with them."
Therapist: "Then let us build Tuesday around that meal."

Watch the therapist's moves. She used the family's own words instead of her textbook words. She asked one question at a time. She let the silence work. And when the emotion rose, she did not run from it; she stayed, steady as a wall, so the family could lean without falling.

Here is a list of errors to keep before your eyes. Do not assume a quiet family is a healthy family. Do not assume a loud family is a violent family. Do not assume that the person who called for help is the person who needs the most help. And do not assume the problem is new; often it has been growing quietly for years.

Here is a hard one. A couple wants you to tell them whether to divorce. What is your answer? You do not decide for them. You help them clarify their values, their options, their children's needs, and their readiness. Discernment is not advice-giving; it is accompanying a decision.

Your assignment is to write a letter you will never send. Write to a family member who hurt you, describing what you felt and what you needed. Do not send it. Then notice whether your body feels lighter. Unspoken pain becomes a therapist's blind spot; spoken pain becomes compassion.

Quick reflection: Write in your notebook three ways The Couple Life Cycle applies to families in your community. Bring your answer to class next week. We will discuss together.


MODULE 4: Conflict and Repair
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Conflict and Repair. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

All couples have conflict. Healthy couples learn to repair after conflict. Repair means apologising, forgiving, and reconnecting. Couples who repair quickly stay together. Couples who withdraw and blame separate. Teach couples to repair with humility.

In plain words, Conflict and Repair is about who carries the weight and who carries the blame. In unhealthy families, the weakest person often carries both. In healthy families, the weight is shared and blame is replaced by responsibility. That shift is the heart of our work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Osogbo, a husband and wife came for help with conflict and repair. The husband was a deacon; the wife was a teacher. Both were respected in church. In public they smiled; at home they barely spoke. The therapist named this gap with kindness: "You are two good people living two separate lives." The wife cried first. The husband followed. What emerged was not scandal but exhaustion, and a shared fear of disappointing their families. Therapy for conflict and repair became, in practice, permission for two tired people to be honest. Honesty, in their case, was the healing.

You see, my students, this example shows conflict and repair in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your mother called you lazy. What happened inside you when she said it?"
Teenager: "I stopped listening."
Therapist: "That is honest. And what would you have wanted her to say instead?"
Teenager: "That she is worried, not that I am useless."
Therapist: "That is a sentence we can work with. Mother, could you say what you actually feel, using his sentence as a guide?"
Mother: "I am worried about your future. I am afraid for you."
Teenager: "Then say that. I can hear that."

Observe the moment of repair. The husband raised his voice, and the therapist said calmly, 'Let us pause for a breath.' That single sentence modelled what the couple needed to learn. Therapists teach by what they do in the room, not only by what they say.

Let me point out what goes wrong. Therapists often chase the identified patient and ignore the marriage. They often focus on the individual's feelings and miss the family's pattern. They often promise confidentiality that they cannot keep. Each of these errors costs trust, and trust is expensive to rebuild.

Test yourself on ethics. Your client asks you to keep a secret from her husband that affects their marriage. Do you agree? No. You explain at intake that you do not hold secrets that affect the relationship, and you help her find the courage to tell the truth with support.

This week, practise the pause. Before you give advice in any conversation, wait five seconds and ask one question instead. Notice what changes. Most of us speak too soon; the pause is where the other person's wisdom gets a chance to appear.

MODULE 5: Intimacy and Connection
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Intimacy and Connection. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Intimacy has many forms: emotional, physical, intellectual, and spiritual. Couples need all forms. When intimacy is low, couples feel like roommates. Therapists help couples rebuild intimacy step by step, with respect and consent.

Let me use a market example. A trader in Bodija market knows the price of every item and the mood of every customer. That is systems awareness. Intimacy and Connection asks you to bring the same awareness into the family. Who is tense today? Who is quiet? What changed since last week? Read the family the way a trader reads a market.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A pastor in Ibadan came to therapy secretly, ashamed that he needed help. His marriage was cold, his children were distant, and the matter of intimacy and connection had become a private torment. He said, "I counsel others; who counsels me?" The therapist honoured his courage and helped him see that asking for help is not the opposite of faith; it is a form of honesty. Over several months, he learned to bring the same pastoral patience home. His wife later said, "The man who came back to us was the man I married." Ministry had been consuming the minister, and therapy returned him to his own house.

You see, my students, this example shows intimacy and connection in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "On a scale of one to ten, how safe does this home feel to each of you?"
Wife: "Four."
Husband: "Seven."
Therapist: "Two different numbers under one roof. That is worth understanding. Wife, what makes it a four?"
Wife: "I never know his mood when he comes in."
Therapist: "Husband, what would it take for her number to rise by one point this week?"
Husband: "I can call her before I leave work."
Therapist: "That is a real, doable step. Let us test it this week."

Observe three skills in that exchange. First, joining: the therapist entered the family's world before asking anything of it. Second, reframing: she changed the meaning of the behaviour without denying the pain. Third, homework: she sent the family home with one small, clear task. Those three moves are worth more than a hundred clever interpretations.

Let me speak candidly. The most dangerous mistake is not a technique error; it is a character error. A therapist who gossips, exaggerates, or plays favourites will harm families no matter how many models he knows. Skill without integrity is a sharp knife in a child's hand.

Here is a question to stretch you. The grandmother is the most powerful person in the home, but she refuses to attend sessions. How do you work with the system without her in the room? You honour her through the family, you ask permission before changes, and you invite her through a respected messenger. Systems change through respect, not confrontation.

Practise this sentence this week: 'Help me understand.' Use it whenever you feel tempted to correct someone. Notice how the other person's body relaxes when they hear it. That sentence is a key that opens locked rooms.

MODULE 6: Money, Work, and Stress
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Money, Work, and Stress. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Money and work stress are top causes of marital conflict. Couples argue about spending, saving, debt, and support for extended family. Therapists teach couples to make money plans together and to support each other's work with understanding.

Let me put it simply. In plain terms, Money, Work, and Stress is the study of what happens between people, not just inside them. A man may be patient at work and impatient at home, and the difference is not in his character alone; it is in the patterns of the home. Change the pattern, and you often change the man.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Kano, a young couple married for two years came to therapy because of money, work, and stress. They had expected marriage to be easy, and they were ashamed that it was not. The therapist normalised their struggle: "Every marriage has a first crisis. The crisis is not the problem. The silence around the crisis is the problem." They learned a simple weekly ritual: twenty minutes, no phones, three questions each. What went well? What was hard? What do you need from me? Within two months, the shame had lifted, and the marriage had a structure for repair.

You see, my students, this example shows money, work, and stress in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You said your son is stubborn. Tell me about a time this week when he was not stubborn."
Mother: (pause) "On Sunday, when we cooked together."
Therapist: "What was different about Sunday?"
Mother: "I was not rushing. I was just with him."
Therapist: "So connection changed his behaviour before discipline did. That is a very important discovery. What would it take to have two Sundays in a week?"

Look closely at her language. She used 'we' more than 'you'. She said 'we will try something' rather than 'you must change'. That language builds a team. A family that feels teamed with the therapist will take risks; a family that feels examined will hide.

Let me warn you about three common errors. First, ignoring the body. A family in crisis may be hungry, exhausted, or sick, and no amount of talking will help until those needs are addressed. Second, ignoring the money. Many 'communication problems' are actually money problems wearing a mask. Third, ignoring the community. If the church, mosque, or extended family opposes the therapy, the therapy may fail before it begins.

Let me pose a case. A wife tells you in private that she is afraid of her husband, but in the joint session she says everything is fine. What do you do? You do not confront her publicly. You make a safety plan privately, you assess the danger carefully, and you never conduct joint sessions where violence is active without a plan.

This week, sit with one family and ask only one question: 'What is the one thing you wish we could change first?' Then be quiet. Let them fill the silence. Whatever they say first is usually the doorway to everything else.

MODULE 7: Extended Family and Boundaries

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Extended Family and Boundaries. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

In Africa, extended family is important. Parents, in-laws, and siblings influence marriage. Healthy couples set boundaries with love. They honour elders but make their own decisions together. Boundaries protect the marriage without disrespecting culture.

Let me give you the short version. Extended Family and Boundaries says that every family is trying to solve a problem, even when the solution looks like the problem. The husband who stays late at the office may be avoiding a fight. The wife who shouts may be trying to be heard. Look for the purpose behind the behaviour before you judge it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Port Harcourt, a husband and wife came for help with extended family and boundaries. The husband was a deacon; the wife was a teacher. Both were respected in church. In public they smiled; at home they barely spoke. The therapist named this gap with kindness: "You are two good people living two separate lives." The wife cried first. The husband followed. What emerged was not scandal but exhaustion, and a shared fear of disappointing their families. You see, my students, this example shows extended family and boundaries in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Everyone in this family has been carrying something alone. Today I want us to put some of it down. Who will start?"
Grandmother: "I will. I am tired of being the one who keeps the peace."
Therapist: "Thank you for your courage. And when you keep the peace, who is freed from doing it?"
Grandmother: "Everyone else."
Therapist: "So your burden has become their permission to stay silent. Let us see if we can share that load differently."

Did you notice how she handled the resistance? She did not argue. She agreed with the part that was true and then asked a question that opened a door. That is the therapist's art: to be firm without being rigid, and gentle without being soft.

These mistakes are common and costly. Do not use technical words to impress. Do not take phone calls in session. Do not allow a session to run without a plan. Do not forget the homework from the previous week. Families notice whether you remember them, and remembering is a form of respect.

Here is a question for you. If a family says 'our son is the problem', what are three other places you would look before accepting that? Think: the marriage, the family rules, and the wider system of school and relatives. A therapist who looks only where the family points will often miss the real wound.

Your practical task is to observe a disagreement without intervening. Watch two people argue, in your family or at work, and notice the cycle: who pursues, who withdraws, who rescues. Write the cycle as a diagram. Bring it to class, with names changed, and we will study it together.

Here is another way of saying it. Faith and Spirituality in Marriage is about the rules nobody wrote down. Every family has them: who speaks first, who decides money, who is allowed to be angry, who must always be strong. Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I am going to ask a strange question. If the problem disappeared tonight, what is the first small thing you would notice tomorrow morning?"
Husband: "She would greet me at the door."
Wife: "He would ask about my day."
Therapist: "So the miracle is not a mansion or a lottery win. It is a greeting and a question. That tells me your marriage is closer to healing than you think."

Notice how she ended. She did not end on the problem; she ended on a picture of the family's preferred future. 'Imagine next Sunday, what would be different?' The family left with hope in their hands. Hope is not decoration; it is the fuel of change.

Here is a question about your own heart. Which family would you find hardest to treat, and why? The answer reveals your blind spot. A therapist who knows his blind spot can guard against it; a therapist who denies it will be ruled by it.

This week, do a listening audit. Count how many times you interrupt someone in a day. Do not scold yourself; just count. Awareness is the first step. Next week, try to reduce the number by half.

Therapist: "You have both said the word respect many times. What does respect look like in your house, in actions, not in theory?"
Wife: "He would not correct me in front of his mother."
Husband: "She would not discuss our money with her sister."
Therapist: "Now we have something specific. These are not feelings; these are agreements. Let us write them down and test them for two weeks."

Let me point out what she avoided. She did not ask 'Why did you do that?' which invites defence. She asked 'What happened just before that?' which invites observation. Small changes in question shape produce large changes in conversation.

Here is my counsel. Do not confuse culture with abuse. Do not confuse respect with silence. Do not confuse forgiveness with pretending nothing happened. Do not confuse a husband's provision with his presence. And do not confuse a wife's submission with her having no voice.

Check your thinking here. What is the difference between a boundary and a wall? A boundary says 'this is where I end and you begin, and I can still love you'. A wall says 'you cannot reach me'. Families often build walls and call them boundaries. Your task is to turn walls into doors.

Your assignment this week is small but powerful. Ask one family member, 'What is one thing I could do differently that would make you feel more respected?' Then listen without defending. Write down the answer word for word. Bring it to class. This exercise teaches you how it feels to be on the receiving end of your own questions.

MODULE 10: Infidelity and Betrayal
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Infidelity and Betrayal. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Infidelity breaks trust. Healing requires full disclosure, accountability, and rebuilding trust over time. The therapist must be neutral, help both partners express pain, and guide the couple through grief, anger, and forgiveness. Not all marriages survive, but the therapist must help with honesty.

Now, why does Infidelity and Betrayal matter so much in our setting? Because in Nigeria, a family is rarely just two people. It is grandparents, uncles, aunties, in-laws, pastors, imams, and neighbours. Whatever we study in this course must be applied with that wider family in view. A therapy that ignores the extended family will be outvoted by it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Osogbo, a husband and wife came for help with infidelity and betrayal. The husband was a deacon; the wife was a teacher. Both were respected in church. In public they smiled; at home they barely spoke. The therapist named this gap with kindness: "You are two good people living two separate lives." The wife cried first. The husband followed. What emerged was not scandal but exhaustion, and a shared fear of disappointing their families. Therapy for infidelity and betrayal became, in practice, permission for two tired people to be honest. Honesty, in their case, was the healing.

You see, my students, this example shows infidelity and betrayal in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I notice you have not looked at your husband since you sat down."
Wife: "There is nothing to look at."
Therapist: "That sentence carries a lot of pain. I am not here to judge it. I am here to understand it. When did you stop looking at him?"
Wife: (crying) "When he stopped looking at me."
Therapist: "Then let us begin exactly there. One look, one sentence, one honest moment."

Here is a subtle skill. She asked each member the same question in turn: 'What would you want the others to understand about you?' The quiet daughter spoke last and said the most important thing. Equal airtime is not a technique of fairness; it is a technique of discovery.

Here are the traps to avoid. Do not confuse understanding with agreement. You can understand why a husband shouts without approving of the shouting. Do not use your own marriage as the standard for every family. Do not quote research to argue with a grandmother; honour her first, then offer another view. And never promise a result you cannot control, such as 'your marriage will surely be saved'.

Consider this scenario. A father brings his teenage son for 'discipline problems' but refuses to discuss his own drinking. How do you proceed? You join with the father's concern, you keep the son's welfare central, and you gently raise the drinking as one part of the picture, without making it a condition of your respect.

Your task is to interview an elder. Ask an older person in your community: 'How were conflicts settled in your family when you were young? What did your parents do that you would keep, and what would you change?' Write one page. You will learn more about culture in one hour than in ten textbooks.

MODULE 11: Divorce Discernment and Decision

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Divorce Discernment and Decision. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Some couples come when one partner wants to leave. Discernment counselling helps the couple decide: repair, separate, or divorce. The therapist helps them clarify goals, values, and readiness. The decision must be informed and respectful, especially where children are involved.

Let me be practical. When Divorce Discernment and Decision is handled badly, the damage rarely stays with one person. It moves to the children, then to the children's children. When it is handled well, the benefit moves in exactly the same way. That is why your work in this course is not a small thing; it is generational work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A pastor in Ibadan came to therapy secretly, ashamed that he needed help. His marriage was cold, his children were distant, and the matter of divorce discernment and decision had become a private torment. He said, "I counsel others; who counsels me?" The therapist honoured his courage and helped him see that asking for help is not the opposite of faith; it is a form of honesty. Over several months, he learned to bring the same pastoral patience home. His wife later said, "The man who came back to us was the man I married." Ministry had been consuming the minister, and therapy returned him to his own house.

You see, my students, this example shows divorce discernment and decision in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your daughter says she feels unheard. What do you hear when she says that?"
Father: "I hear that I have failed as a father."
Therapist: "So her pain lands on your shame, and your shame responds with anger. Do you see the cycle?"
Father: "I do."
Therapist: "Good. Then the next time she speaks, try to hear her pain without hearing a verdict about you. That is the skill we will practise today."

Here is the skill to copy. She listened for the feeling behind the complaint. The wife said 'he is careless', but the therapist heard 'I am afraid I do not matter to him'. When she named that fear gently, the wife wept and the husband softened. Accurate empathy is a surgical instrument.

Let me speak plainly about errors. The worst mistake is making the family feel judged. Once shame fills the room, learning stops. The second worst is rescuing the family from every discomfort. Growth lives just beyond discomfort, and if you remove all discomfort, you remove the growth. Walk with them through the fire; do not carry them.

Ask yourself this. If you only had three questions to ask any family, what would they be? Here are mine: What has been happening? What have you already tried? What would be different if this were better? Those three questions give you a story, a history of solutions, and a goal.

Practise the phrase 'say more about that'. Use it three times a day in ordinary conversations. You will be amazed how much people have been waiting to say and how rarely they are invited.

MODULE 12: Therapist Self-Awareness
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Therapist Self-Awareness. This is a very important topic in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Advanced therapists examine their own marriages, families, and biases. Your own story affects how you see clients. Self-awareness, supervision, and personal therapy help you stay neutral and effective. Know yourself before you help others.

I want you to notice something important. What we are calling Therapist Self-Awareness here is not a theoretical idea you will admire and forget. It is a lens that changes what you see in the first ten minutes of a session. Two therapists can sit in the same room with the same family; the one who understands Therapist Self-Awareness will see a pattern, while the other will only see a problem.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Kano, a young couple married for two years came to therapy because of therapist self-Awareness. They had expected marriage to be easy, and they were ashamed that it was not. The therapist normalised their struggle: "Every marriage has a first crisis. The crisis is not the problem. The silence around the crisis is the problem." They learned a simple weekly ritual: twenty minutes, no phones, three questions each. What went well? What was hard? What do you need from me? Within two months, the shame had lifted, and the marriage had a structure for repair.

You see, my students, this example shows therapist self-Awareness in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Both of you have said you want the marriage to work. What has each of you done in the last month that made it a little better?"
Husband: "I stopped shouting."
Wife: "I stopped bringing my mother into our arguments."
Therapist: "So you are both already working. That means I am not starting from zero. I am joining a project that is already underway."

Watch the therapist handle the secret. When the mother hinted at something painful, the therapist did not pounce. She said, 'There seems to be something heavy here. We do not have to open it today, but when you are ready, I will be here.' Safety was preserved and the door was left open.

Let me caution you about three things. First, the rescue fantasy: believing you must save the family yourself. You are a guide, not a saviour. Second, the expert trap: speaking as if you have never struggled at home. Humility opens doors that expertise cannot. Third, the speed trap: rushing change to prove you are effective. Slow, steady change lasts.

Think about this. A couple has stopped fighting. Is that progress? Not always. Some couples stop fighting because they have given up. Check for warmth, humour, and touch. Peace without connection can be the silence before separation.

Do this exercise. Draw a simple map of your own family of origin, showing who was close, who was distant, and who carried the most responsibility. Then write one sentence about how that map affects your work with families today. Self-knowledge is not optional for a therapist; it is equipment.

To study Ethics and Competence is to ask every member of the family, gently but firmly, one question: what is your part in this, and what will you do about it? Not the other person's part. Your part. Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Let us talk about money, but not about who is right. When you were growing up, what did your family teach you about money?"

REAL LIFE PRACTICAL 1: Couple Assessment Interview
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, theory without practice is empty. So let us do a real life practical on Couple Assessment Interview. We will work through it in the style of the Ethics Decision Frame, which is one of the practical frames you will use in different settings.

Scenario: A couple comes for the first time. The husband talks a lot, the wife is silent. How do you assess both partners fairly? A couple in Ibadan argues about in-laws. The husband says his wife disrespects his mother. The wife says his mother controls their home. Use assessment and boundary skills.

Let me walk you through this practical step by step, as you would do it in real life.

Step One: Name the ethical question in one sentence. For example: 'May I keep this information private when it may affect another person's safety?' Vague dilemmas cannot be resolved, so precision comes first.

Step Two: Gather the facts. Who is involved? What exactly was disclosed? What are the legal duties in your jurisdiction? What is known, and what is assumed? Write the facts separately from the fears.

Step Three: Identify the principles at stake. Autonomy, nonmaleficence (do no harm), beneficence (do good), justice, fidelity, and the client's own cultural and faith values. Where principles pull in different directions, say so honestly.

Step Four: Consult before deciding. Call a supervisor, a trusted senior colleague, or a professional body. Consultation is not indecision; it is diligence. Record whom you consulted and what was discussed.

Step Five: Choose the least harmful, most transparent course. Explain your reasoning to the client in plain language, including what you can keep private and what you must share. Document the conversation.

Step Six: Act, and protect the vulnerable first. If a child, a suicidal person, or a threatened third party is at risk, safety overrides the usual privacy rules, within the law.

Step Seven: Review the outcome and record what you learned. Ethical competence grows through reflection on hard decisions, not through memorising rules.

When therapists skip these steps, families feel rushed, and rushing breeds resistance. When therapists follow them, even imperfectly, families relax, because structure communicates care. I have seen a single well-run first session change the direction of an entire family's year.

PART THREE: CASE STUDIES - THINK LIKE A THERAPIST

CASE STUDY 1: The Silent Wife
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us learn from a real case. I will tell you the story, and you will think like a therapist.

A wife in Oyo State came for counselling because her husband beat her when she spoke. She was silent in the session and looked down. The therapist built trust slowly, met her alone, made a safety plan, and worked with the husband on anger. After two months, the wife began to speak, and the husband joined a men's group. The marriage survived because safety came first and change was supported.

Now let us analyse this case together, using six questions.

First, what is the presenting problem? The family names one problem, but the real problem is often deeper. A family may say "our son is stubborn", while the true issue is marital conflict, neglect, or trauma. Look deeper with care, and never argue with the family's account; simply add to it.

Second, what is the family system? Who is in the family? Who holds power? Who is close, and who is distant? Where is the triangle? Map the system in your mind, and then check the map with the family.

Third, what are the strengths? Maybe the family prays together. Maybe the parents work hard. Maybe the children are talented. Build on strengths. Do not only look at problems.

Fourth, what intervention will you use? Choose one clear intervention from Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Do not try everything at once. Explain it simply, demonstrate it, and practise it in the room.

Fifth, what is your ethical concern? Is there abuse? Is there danger? Is a referral needed? Protect life first. If there is violence, make a safety plan. If there is mental illness, refer. Ethics is protection, not paperwork.

Sixth, what will you do next session? Tell the family exactly what you will do, what you hope they will do, and what you will celebrate if it works. Give hope, and end with encouragement.

Let me ask you: if you were the therapist in this case, what would you do first? Write your answer in your notebook. Discuss with your group. There is no single perfect answer, but there are better and worse answers. Choose the answer that is respectful, safe, and practical.

Remember, every case teaches you something. Keep a case diary. Record what you did, what worked, and what you would do differently. Over time, that diary becomes your own clinical textbook.

Finally, we plan the next session, ending with hope. Tell the family exactly what you will do, what you hope they will do, and what you will celebrate if it works. Give hope, and end with encouragement.

Let me ask you: if you were the therapist in this case, what would you do first? Write your answer in your notebook. Discuss with your group. There is no single perfect answer, but there are better and worse answers. Choose the answer that is respectful, safe, and practical.

Before you close your notebook, ask yourself one more question: what would this family need from me if they returned next week, and what would they need from their own community? Therapy is part of a larger web of care.


CASE STUDY 3: The Overworked Pastor
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us learn from a real case. I will tell you the story, and you will think like a therapist.

A pastor in Enugu worked day and night for church and had no time for his wife and three children. The wife felt like a widow. The children failed in school. The therapist helped the family create a weekly rest day and family time. The pastor learned to delegate church work. The family healed when time and attention were restored.

Now let us analyse this case together, using six questions.

Start with the symptom and the story behind it. The family names one problem, but the real problem is often deeper. A family may say "our son is stubborn", while the true issue is marital conflict, neglect, or trauma. Look deeper with care, and never argue with the family's account; simply add to it.

Then draw the structure: who leads, who protects, who is left out. Who is in the family? Who holds power? Who is close, and who is distant? Where is the triangle? Map the system in your mind, and then check the map with the family.

Then ask what this family has survived, and what resources remain. Maybe the family prays together. Maybe the parents work hard. Maybe the children are talented. Build on strengths. Do not only look at problems.

Then match a technique to the pattern you have identified. Choose one clear intervention from Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy. Do not try everything at once. Explain it simply, demonstrate it, and practise it in the room.

Then check the ethical and legal boundaries of the case. Is there abuse? Is there danger? Is a referral needed? Protect life first. If there is violence, make a safety plan. If there is mental illness, refer. Ethics is protection, not paperwork.

Then rehearse the opening sentence of your next session. Tell the family exactly what you will do, what you hope they will do, and what you will celebrate if it works. Give hope, and end with encouragement.

Let me ask you: if you were the therapist in this case, what would you do first? Write your answer in your notebook. Discuss with your group. There is no single perfect answer, but there are better and worse answers. Choose the answer that is respectful, safe, and practical.

A case is never only a case. It is a family with a name, a history, and a future. Hold that weight gently, and let it keep you humble.

DEEPER EXPLANATION ONE: WHY MARRIAGE NEEDS A SYSTEMS LENS
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let me take you deeper into the systems view. When a couple comes to you, they usually bring one story. The wife says, "My husband is cold." The husband says, "My wife is always complaining." If you believe only one story, you will fail. But if you listen to both, you will see a dance. She complains, so he withdraws. He withdraws, so she complains louder. Neither of them started the dance alone, and neither can stop it alone.

This is why we say the problem lives between people, not inside one person. A therapist who understands this stops asking "Who is wrong?" and starts asking "What happens between you?" That one change in question changes everything. It removes shame. It invites teamwork. It gives the couple a common enemy, which is the pattern, not each other.

In real life, I have seen this in a home in Surulere. The husband worked two jobs and came home tired. The wife stayed home with three children and felt invisible. Every evening she greeted him with complaints. Every evening he answered with silence and his phone. She concluded he did not love her. He concluded she did not appreciate his sacrifice. Both were lonely. Both were faithful. Both were hurting. The therapist did not side with either one. The therapist drew the cycle on paper and said, "Look at this dance. Who will step out first?" The wife laughed. The husband became quiet. That laughter was the beginning of healing, because they finally saw the same picture.

DEEPER EXPLANATION TWO: THE FIRST SESSION IN DETAIL
----------------------------------------------------------------

The first session is the most important session. In the first ten minutes, the couple decides silently whether they will trust you. So you must be warm, calm, and fair. Greet both. Thank both. Ask both why they came. Then set the rules: one person speaks at a time, no insults, no walking out, and everything shared is confidential within the limits of safety.

Then you gather history with respect. How did you meet? What attracted you? What was your first year like? When did the trouble start? What changed around that time? A new baby? A new job? A move? A death? Money trouble? These questions do not only collect facts. They remind the couple that they once loved each other. That memory is medicine.

Then you watch them argue. Yes, you invite a small argument in the room. Ask them to discuss a small disagreement for five minutes, and you observe. Who interrupts? Who looks away? Who brings in the mother-in-law? Who becomes the peacemaker? You are not being cruel; you are being a doctor. A doctor must see the wound before treating it.

At the end, you give hope and a small task. Say, "You have survived many storms. You are here because you still care. This week, I want you to do one thing: every night before sleep, say one thing you appreciate about each other. Nothing more." Small tasks succeed. Success builds trust. Trust keeps them coming back.

PRACTICAL FOR THIS WEEK: THE DANCE MAP
----------------------------------------------------------------

Take a plain sheet of paper. Draw two circles, one for each partner. Draw arrows between them showing what each does when there is tension. Write the feeling under each arrow. Keep it simple and honest. Then ask yourself: if I was the therapist, where would I interrupt this cycle? What small experiment would I give this couple? Bring your map to class and explain it in three minutes. This exercise will train your eyes to see patterns instead of blame.

COMMON MISTAKES IN ADVANCED PRACTICE
----------------------------------------------------------------

First mistake: working harder than the couple. If you are the only one sweating, stop. Give them work. Second mistake: rescuing too quickly. When the wife cries, do not rush to comfort her and silence the husband. Let the emotion do its work. Third mistake: ignoring culture. In many Nigerian homes, the husband expects public respect and the wife expects financial protection. If you ignore these expectations, you will lose the room. Name them with respect, then negotiate them with kindness. Fourth mistake: forgetting your own marriage. A therapist with a bleeding wound will bleed on the client. Keep your own house in order, and seek supervision when your own story rises in the room.

PART FOUR: KEY TECHNIQUES YOU MUST MASTER
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, in Advanced Marriage and Family Therapy you must master practical techniques. Theory is good, but techniques change lives. Let me list the key techniques for this course, and I will explain how to use each one.

Technique 1: Active Listening

How to use it: first explain why the technique exists, not only what it is. Families cooperate when they understand the purpose. Second, model it in a low-stakes example, such as how to greet a neighbour. Third, have the family rehearse the real situation while you coach gently. Fourth, ask, "What felt natural, and what felt strange?" Fifth, agree on a practice plan for the week.

Real life tip: use the family's own names and situations in the rehearsal. Practice that sounds like their real life transfers to their real life.

Common mistake: correcting too much too soon. Let the first attempt be imperfect. Confidence comes before polish.

Technique 2: Gottman Method

How to use it: teach it in three sentences. One, what it is. Two, when to use it. Three, what to say. Then show it. Then have the family show you. Then ask each member to name one moment this week when they will use it. Write those moments down and review them next session.

Real life tip: ask a respected elder or pastor to endorse the technique publicly. Cultural endorsement speeds adoption dramatically.

Common mistake: using the technique only in the office. If it stays in the office, nothing changes at home.

Technique 3: Emotionally Focused Therapy

How to use it: start with a story. Tell a short story of a family who used the technique and what changed. Stories lower resistance and create hope. Then walk through the steps on paper so the family can see them. Then practise in pairs inside the room, with one person as the coach. End by asking, "What would make this hard at home, and how will you handle that?"

Real life tip: keep a small collection of true, anonymised stories from your practice and community. Stories travel further than instructions.

Common mistake: ignoring the obstacles the family names. If they say "my husband will not participate", that is the real problem to solve before the technique can work.

Technique 4: Genogram

How to use it: use the teach-back method. Explain the technique briefly, then ask the family to teach it back to you in their own words. If they cannot, the teaching was not clear enough. Then rehearse it once, and once more with a small obstacle added, such as someone interrupting. Finish with a written note in simple language that they can keep at home.

Real life tip: write the note in the language the family thinks in, or provide a short voice note if literacy is limited. Accessibility is respect.

Common mistake: assuming understanding because heads were nodding. Nodding is politeness; teach-back is proof.

Technique 5: Discernment Counselling

How to use it: begin with a plain invitation. "This is a way to help you talk better. Let us try it together." Then demonstrate it yourself, with a colleague or with your own words. Let the family practise while you watch, and give kind, specific feedback. Send the skill home as a small assignment. A technique works only when it is practised at home.

Real life tip: many Nigerian families respect proverbs. Teach this technique with a short proverb so it is remembered long after the session.

Common mistake: do not stack techniques. One per session, mastered, is worth more than five half-learned.

Key Terms to Remember
----------------------------------------------------------------
- System: a set of connected parts that influence one another, such as a family.
- Homeostasis: a system's tendency to maintain stability by resisting change, which also resists therapy.
- Genogram: a family tree drawn across three or more generations, showing relationships, patterns, and events.
- Repair: the act of reconnecting after conflict through apology, warmth, or humour.
- Discernment: the careful process of clarifying values and options before making a serious decision such as separation or divorce.
- Boundaries: the invisible lines that define who is responsible for what, and where one person's self ends and another's begins.
- Intimacy: emotional, physical, and spiritual closeness between partners, built through honesty, safety, and attention.
