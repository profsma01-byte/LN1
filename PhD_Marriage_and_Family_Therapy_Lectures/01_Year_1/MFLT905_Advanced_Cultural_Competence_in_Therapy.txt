MODULE 1: Culture and Family
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Culture and Family. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Culture shapes family: marriage, parenting, gender roles, respect, and healing. In Nigeria, family is extended, elders are honoured, and faith is central. Therapists must learn the culture of each family, not assume.

Let me say this as simply as I can. Culture and Family is not a label you paste on a person. It is a way of seeing how people move together, the way you would watch dancers and notice who leads, who follows, and who keeps stepping on whose toes. Once you can see the dance, you can teach a new step.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Osogbo ran a successful business together and a failing home. The father managed the shop. The mother managed the accounts. The children managed their own loneliness. The presenting problem was culture and family, but the real problem was that the family had become a company with no living room. The therapist invited them to separate the two: business meetings at the shop, family time at home, no ledgers at dinner. It sounded simple. It was not. It took three months of practice before the father could sit through a meal without discussing profit. On the day he finally did, his youngest daughter said, "Daddy, you are here." He wept.

You see, my students, this example shows culture and family in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Thank you both for coming. Before we begin, let me say what I already see: two people who are still here, still trying. Who would like to start?"
Wife: "I am tired of being ignored."
Therapist: "Ignored. That is a strong word. Can you give me one recent moment when you felt ignored?"
Wife: "Last night. I spoke and he kept looking at his phone."
Therapist: "And when she spoke and you kept looking at the phone, what was happening for you?"
Husband: "I was reading work messages. I did not know she needed me."
Therapist: "So one of you needed attention and the other did not know it was being asked for. That gap is where we will work."

Notice what the therapist did in that dialogue. She did not rush to solve. She named the feeling, slowed the pace, and invited the family to look at the pattern together. That is the difference between advice and therapy. Advice tells people what to do. Therapy helps people see clearly enough to decide for themselves.

Now let us name the mistakes young therapists make here. The first is taking sides too early. The moment you sound like the wife's lawyer, the husband stops listening. The second is rushing to comfort. Tears are not an emergency; they are a language. Let the tears speak before you hand out tissues. The third is talking more than the family. If you are speaking for more than a third of the session, you are working alone.

Let me test your understanding. A husband complains that his wife no longer respects him, and the wife complains that he no longer talks to her. Which is the presenting problem, and which is the pattern? Write your answer before you read on. The pattern is this: he feels disrespected, so he withdraws; she feels abandoned, so she criticises; he feels more disrespected. Two complaints, one dance.

Here is your practical step for this week. Choose one family you know well, perhaps your own, and for three days write down one interaction at dinner. Who spoke first? Who was interrupted? Who left the table first? Do not judge; just record. Patterns appear when you look with a pencil in your hand.

MODULE 2: Cultural Humility
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Cultural Humility. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Cultural humility means you are a learner. You do not know everything about a family's culture. You ask, listen, and respect. Humility builds trust faster than expertise.

Now let me explain it plainly, the way I would explain it to a market woman in Ibadan. Cultural Humility simply means that people in a family carry each other. If one person carries too much, the others feel lighter but weaker. If one person lets go, everyone stumbles. Balance is the goal, not perfection.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Ibadan, a husband was transferred to another state for work. The family had to decide whether to move together or live apart. The question of cultural humility sat at the centre of every conversation. The therapist helped them do a structured decision exercise: each partner listed their fears, their hopes, their non-negotiables, and their willingness to compromise. Written down, the lists were surprisingly compatible. What had looked like an impossible conflict was actually a failure of process. They chose to move together for two years, with a review date. Decisions made in daylight are easier to live with than decisions made in fear.

You see, my students, this example shows cultural humility in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "When the two of you argue about this, what do you hear in your mind?"
Husband: "I hear that I am a failure."
Therapist: "And what do you hear, madam?"
Wife: "I hear that I am not a good wife."
Therapist: "You see, you are not really fighting each other. You are fighting two old voices. Let us separate the voices from the people, and then we can talk about the actual problem."

Watch how she used the genogram. She did not use it as paperwork; she used it as a story map. 'Who else in this family carried this burden?' she asked. The family discovered a pattern across three generations. Insight that comes from the family's own history is far more powerful than insight imported from a textbook.

Watch out for these mistakes. Do not label a family 'resistant' when they are simply afraid. Do not diagnose from one session. Do not allow one person to describe the whole family for all the others. And do not treat every problem as a trauma or every sadness as depression. Good therapists are slow to name and quick to listen.

Ask yourself. When a family says 'we have tried everything', what have they not tried? Usually they have not tried consistency, or they have not tried kindness, or they have not tried asking for help in the right place. Explore the word 'everything' with curiosity rather than acceptance.

This week, build one ritual. Choose a family you work with or your own home, and create a fifteen-minute routine that happens at the same time every day: a walk, a prayer, a cup of tea with no phones. Rituals are small investments with large returns. Report what happened.

MODULE 3: Cultural Assessment
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Cultural Assessment. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Ask about language, religion, migration, family structure, values, and healing traditions. Use cultural formulation. Understand what the family believes about illness, marriage, and change.

Here is the simple English version. Cultural Assessment means that behaviour in a family is not random. It follows a pattern, and patterns can be learned and unlearned. Nobody teaches a family to quarrel every Sunday, yet the quarrel appears like a visitor who knows the time. Therapy teaches the family to change the appointment.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Kano, a couple sat in front of a therapist and argued about cultural assessment for twenty minutes without pausing. Each one had a list of the other's failures. The therapist listened carefully, then asked one question: "If this problem were solved tonight, what would tomorrow morning look like in your house?" The room went quiet. The husband said, "We would eat breakfast together." The wife said, "He would ask me how I slept." Suddenly the argument was no longer about blame. It was about a lost breakfast and an unasked question. From that small picture, the therapist helped them build a plan for cultural assessment that felt human, not legal.

You see, my students, this example shows cultural assessment in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Describe a normal Tuesday in your house."
Father: "We wake, we rush, we work, we come home, we sleep."
Therapist: "Where is the part of Tuesday when the family is actually together?"
Father: (silence)
Therapist: "That silence is important. It is telling us something. What would you like Tuesday to look like?"
Father: "I would like to eat with them."
Therapist: "Then let us build Tuesday around that meal."

Watch the therapist's moves. She used the family's own words instead of her textbook words. She asked one question at a time. She let the silence work. And when the emotion rose, she did not run from it; she stayed, steady as a wall, so the family could lean without falling.

Here is a list of errors to keep before your eyes. Do not assume a quiet family is a healthy family. Do not assume a loud family is a violent family. Do not assume that the person who called for help is the person who needs the most help. And do not assume the problem is new; often it has been growing quietly for years.

Here is a hard one. A couple wants you to tell them whether to divorce. What is your answer? You do not decide for them. You help them clarify their values, their options, their children's needs, and their readiness. Discernment is not advice-giving; it is accompanying a decision.

Your assignment is to write a letter you will never send. Write to a family member who hurt you, describing what you felt and what you needed. Do not send it. Then notice whether your body feels lighter. Unspoken pain becomes a therapist's blind spot; spoken pain becomes compassion.

MODULE 4: Language and Interpreters
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Language and Interpreters. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Language carries meaning. Use interpreters well: brief them, speak to the client not the interpreter, and check understanding. Learn key words in local languages. Respect for language is respect for culture.

In plain words, Language and Interpreters is about who carries the weight and who carries the blame. In unhealthy families, the weakest person often carries both. In healthy families, the weight is shared and blame is replaced by responsibility. That shift is the heart of our work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Port Harcourt ran a successful business together and a failing home. The father managed the shop. The mother managed the accounts. The children managed their own loneliness. The presenting problem was language and interpreters, but the real problem was that the family had become a company with no living room. The therapist invited them to separate the two: business meetings at the shop, family time at home, no ledgers at dinner. It sounded simple. It was not. It took three months of practice before the father could sit through a meal without discussing profit. On the day he finally did, his youngest daughter said, "Daddy, you are here." He wept.

You see, my students, this example shows language and interpreters in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your mother called you lazy. What happened inside you when she said it?"
Teenager: "I stopped listening."
Therapist: "That is honest. And what would you have wanted her to say instead?"
Teenager: "That she is worried, not that I am useless."
Therapist: "That is a sentence we can work with. Mother, could you say what you actually feel, using his sentence as a guide?"
Mother: "I am worried about your future. I am afraid for you."
Teenager: "Then say that. I can hear that."

Observe the moment of repair. The husband raised his voice, and the therapist said calmly, 'Let us pause for a breath.' That single sentence modelled what the couple needed to learn. Therapists teach by what they do in the room, not only by what they say.

Let me point out what goes wrong. Therapists often chase the identified patient and ignore the marriage. They often focus on the individual's feelings and miss the family's pattern. They often promise confidentiality that they cannot keep. Each of these errors costs trust, and trust is expensive to rebuild.

Test yourself on ethics. Your client asks you to keep a secret from her husband that affects their marriage. Do you agree? No. You explain at intake that you do not hold secrets that affect the relationship, and you help her find the courage to tell the truth with support.

This week, practise the pause. Before you give advice in any conversation, wait five seconds and ask one question instead. Notice what changes. Most of us speak too soon; the pause is where the other person's wisdom gets a chance to appear.

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Working with Elders and Faith Leaders. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Elders, pastors, imams, and traditional leaders are important. Invite them when helpful. Respect their roles. Work as a team. The family will accept help faster when trusted leaders support it.

Let me use a market example. A trader in Bodija market knows the price of every item and the mood of every customer. That is systems awareness. Working with Elders and Faith Leaders asks you to bring the same awareness into the family. Who is tense today? Who is quiet? What changed since last week? Read the family the way a trader reads a market.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Onitsha, a husband was transferred to another state for work. The family had to decide whether to move together or live apart. The question of working with elders and faith leaders sat at the centre of every conversation. The therapist helped them do a structured decision exercise: each partner listed their fears, their hopes, their non-negotiables, and their willingness to compromise. Written down, the lists were surprisingly compatible. What had looked like an impossible conflict was actually a failure of process. They chose to move together for two years, with a review date. Decisions made in daylight are easier to live with than decisions made in fear.

You see, my students, this example shows working with elders and faith leaders in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "On a scale of one to ten, how safe does this home feel to each of you?"
Wife: "Four."
Husband: "Seven."
Therapist: "Two different numbers under one roof. That is worth understanding. Wife, what makes it a four?"
Wife: "I never know his mood when he comes in."
Therapist: "Husband, what would it take for her number to rise by one point this week?"
Husband: "I can call her before I leave work."
Therapist: "That is a real, doable step. Let us test it this week."

Observe three skills in that exchange. First, joining: the therapist entered the family's world before asking anything of it. Second, reframing: she changed the meaning of the behaviour without denying the pain. Third, homework: she sent the family home with one small, clear task. Those three moves are worth more than a hundred clever interpretations.

Let me speak candidly. The most dangerous mistake is not a technique error; it is a character error. A therapist who gossips, exaggerates, or plays favourites will harm families no matter how many models he knows. Skill without integrity is a sharp knife in a child's hand.

Here is a question to stretch you. The grandmother is the most powerful person in the home, but she refuses to attend sessions. How do you work with the system without her in the room? You honour her through the family, you ask permission before changes, and you invite her through a respected messenger. Systems change through respect, not confrontation.

Practise this sentence this week: 'Help me understand.' Use it whenever you feel tempted to correct someone. Notice how the other person's body relaxes when they hear it. That sentence is a key that opens locked rooms.

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Gender, Power, and Culture. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Culture includes gender roles and power. Therapists must respect culture while protecting women and children from harm. Balance respect and safety with wisdom and dialogue.

Let me put it simply. In plain terms, Gender, Power, and Culture is the study of what happens between people, not just inside them. A man may be patient at work and impatient at home, and the difference is not in his character alone; it is in the patterns of the home. Change the pattern, and you often change the man.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Kaduna, a couple sat in front of a therapist and argued about gender, power, and culture for twenty minutes without pausing. Each one had a list of the other's failures. The therapist listened carefully, then asked one question: "If this problem were solved tonight, what would tomorrow morning look like in your house?" The room went quiet. The husband said, "We would eat breakfast together." The wife said, "He would ask me how I slept." Suddenly the argument was no longer about blame. It was about a lost breakfast and an unasked question. From that small picture, the therapist helped them build a plan for gender, power, and culture that felt human, not legal.

You see, my students, this example shows gender, power, and culture in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You said your son is stubborn. Tell me about a time this week when he was not stubborn."
Mother: (pause) "On Sunday, when we cooked together."
Therapist: "What was different about Sunday?"
Mother: "I was not rushing. I was just with him."
Therapist: "So connection changed his behaviour before discipline did. That is a very important discovery. What would it take to have two Sundays in a week?"

Look closely at her language. She used 'we' more than 'you'. She said 'we will try something' rather than 'you must change'. That language builds a team. A family that feels teamed with the therapist will take risks; a family that feels examined will hide.

Let me warn you about three common errors. First, ignoring the body. A family in crisis may be hungry, exhausted, or sick, and no amount of talking will help until those needs are addressed. Second, ignoring the money. Many 'communication problems' are actually money problems wearing a mask. Third, ignoring the community. If the church, mosque, or extended family opposes the therapy, the therapy may fail before it begins.

Let me pose a case. A wife tells you in private that she is afraid of her husband, but in the joint session she says everything is fine. What do you do? You do not confront her publicly. You make a safety plan privately, you assess the danger carefully, and you never conduct joint sessions where violence is active without a plan.

This week, sit with one family and ask only one question: 'What is the one thing you wish we could change first?' Then be quiet. Let them fill the silence. Whatever they say first is usually the doorway to everything else.

Many families live between cultures: home culture and foreign culture. Japa returnees, students abroad, and mixed marriages face acculturation stress. Help families bridge cultures with respect.

Let me give you the short version. Migration, Japa, and Acculturation says that every family is trying to solve a problem, even when the solution looks like the problem. The husband who stays late at the office may be avoiding a fight. The wife who shouts may be trying to be heard. Look for the purpose behind the behaviour before you judge it.

It took three months of practice before the father could sit through a meal without discussing profit. On the day he finally did, his youngest daughter said, "Daddy, you are here." He wept.

You see, my students, this example shows migration, japa, and acculturation in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Everyone in this family has been carrying something alone. Today I want us to put some of it down. Who will start?"
Grandmother: "I will. I am tired of being the one who keeps the peace."
Therapist: "Thank you for your courage. And when you keep the peace, who is freed from doing it?"
Grandmother: "Everyone else."
Therapist: "So your burden has become their permission to stay silent. Let us see if we can share that load differently."

Did you notice how she handled the resistance? She did not argue. She agreed with the part that was true and then asked a question that opened a door. That is the therapist's art: to be firm without being rigid, and gentle without being soft.

These mistakes are common and costly. Do not use technical words to impress. Do not take phone calls in session. Do not allow a session to run without a plan. Do not forget the homework from the previous week. Families notice whether you remember them, and remembering is a form of respect.

Here is a question for you. If a family says 'our son is the problem', what are three other places you would look before accepting that? Think: the marriage, the family rules, and the wider system of school and relatives. A therapist who looks only where the family points will often miss the real wound.

Your practical task is to observe a disagreement without intervening. Watch two people argue, in your family or at work, and notice the cycle: who pursues, who withdraws, who rescues. Write the cycle as a diagram. Bring it to class, with names changed, and we will study it together.

When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You have both said the word respect many times. What does respect look like in your house, in actions, not in theory?"
Wife: "He would not correct me in front of his mother."
Husband: "She would not discuss our money with her sister."
Therapist: "Now we have something specific. These are not feelings; these are agreements. Let us write them down and test them for two weeks."

Let me point out what she avoided. She did not ask 'Why did you do that?' which invites defence. She asked 'What happened just before that?' which invites observation. Small changes in question shape produce large changes in conversation.

Here is my counsel. Do not confuse culture with abuse. Do not confuse respect with silence. Do not confuse forgiveness with pretending nothing happened. Do not confuse a husband's provision with his presence. And do not confuse a wife's submission with her having no voice.

Check your thinking here. What is the difference between a boundary and a wall? A boundary says 'this is where I end and you begin, and I can still love you'. A wall says 'you cannot reach me'. Families often build walls and call them boundaries. Your task is to turn walls into doors.

Your assignment this week is small but powerful. Ask one family member, 'What is one thing I could do differently that would make you feel more respected?' Then listen without defending. Write down the answer word for word. Bring it to class. This exercise teaches you how it feels to be on the receiving end of your own questions.

----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Adapting Therapy to Culture. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Adapt techniques: use family meetings, proverbs, prayer, and community support. Do not impose foreign models blindly. Make therapy fit the family, not the family fit the model.

Now, why does Adapting Therapy to Culture matter so much in our setting? Because in Nigeria, a family is rarely just two people. It is grandparents, uncles, aunties, in-laws, pastors, imams, and neighbours. Whatever we study in this course must be applied with that wider family in view. A therapy that ignores the extended family will be outvoted by it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Port Harcourt ran a successful business together and a failing home. The father managed the shop. The mother managed the accounts. The children managed their own loneliness. The presenting problem was adapting therapy to culture, but the real problem was that the family had become a company with no living room. The therapist invited them to separate the two: business meetings at the shop, family time at home, no ledgers at dinner. It sounded simple. It was not. It took three months of practice before the father could sit through a meal without discussing profit. On the day he finally did, his youngest daughter said, "Daddy, you are here." He wept.

You see, my students, this example shows adapting therapy to culture in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I notice you have not looked at your husband since you sat down."
Wife: "There is nothing to look at."
Therapist: "That sentence carries a lot of pain. I am not here to judge it. I am here to understand it. When did you stop looking at him?"
Wife: (crying) "When he stopped looking at me."
Therapist: "Then let us begin exactly there. One look, one sentence, one honest moment."

Here is a subtle skill. She asked each member the same question in turn: 'What would you want the others to understand about you?' The quiet daughter spoke last and said the most important thing. Equal airtime is not a technique of fairness; it is a technique of discovery.

Here are the traps to avoid. Do not confuse understanding with agreement. You can understand why a husband shouts without approving of the shouting. Do not use your own marriage as the standard for every family. Do not quote research to argue with a grandmother; honour her first, then offer another view. And never promise a result you cannot control, such as 'your marriage will surely be saved'.

Consider this scenario. A father brings his teenage son for 'discipline problems' but refuses to discuss his own drinking. How do you proceed? You join with the father's concern, you keep the son's welfare central, and you gently raise the drinking as one part of the picture, without making it a condition of your respect.

Your task is to interview an elder. Ask an older person in your community: 'How were conflicts settled in your family when you were young? What did your parents do that you would keep, and what would you change?' Write one page. You will learn more about culture in one hour than in ten textbooks.

MODULE 11: Cultural Competence and Ethics

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Cultural Competence and Ethics. This is a very important topic in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Cultural competence is an ethical duty. You must not harm families with cultural ignorance. Learn, consult, and refer when needed. Ethics and culture go together.

Let me be practical. When Cultural Competence and Ethics is handled badly, the damage rarely stays with one person. It moves to the children, then to the children's children. When it is handled well, the benefit moves in exactly the same way. That is why your work in this course is not a small thing; it is generational work.

You see, my students, this example shows cultural competence and ethics in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your daughter says she feels unheard. What do you hear when she says that?"
Father: "I hear that I have failed as a father."
Therapist: "So her pain lands on your shame, and your shame responds with anger. Do you see the cycle?"
Father: "I do."
Therapist: "Good. Then the next time she speaks, try to hear her pain without hearing a verdict about you. That is the skill we will practise today."

Here is the skill to copy. She listened for the feeling behind the complaint. The wife said 'he is careless', but the therapist heard 'I am afraid I do not matter to him'. When she named that fear gently, the wife wept and the husband softened. Accurate empathy is a surgical instrument.

Let me speak plainly about errors. The worst mistake is making the family feel judged. Once shame fills the room, learning stops. The second worst is rescuing the family from every discomfort. Growth lives just beyond discomfort, and if you remove all discomfort, you remove the growth. Walk with them through the fire; do not carry them.

Ask yourself this. If you only had three questions to ask any family, what would they be? Here are mine: What has been happening? What have you already tried? What would be different if this were better? Those three questions give you a story, a history of solutions, and a goal.

Practise the phrase 'say more about that'. Use it three times a day in ordinary conversations. You will be amazed how much people have been waiting to say and how rarely they are invited.

REAL LIFE PRACTICAL 1: Cultural Assessment Practice
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, theory without practice is empty. So let us do a real life practical on Cultural Assessment Practice. We will work through it in the style of the Assessment and Formulation Frame, which is one of the practical frames you will use in different settings.

Scenario: A Yoruba family, an Igbo family, and a Hausa family come for therapy. How do you assess each culture respectfully? A family wants a pastor and an elder in therapy. How do you include them as a team?

Let me walk you through this practical step by step, as you would do it in real life.

Step One: Contract the assessment. Tell the family how many sessions it will take and what will happen at the end. A clear frame lowers anxiety.

Step Two: Collect the structure. Who lives where, who earns, who decides, who is ill, who has died, who is estranged. Build the genogram with the family, not about them.

Step Three: Measure what matters. Use brief, valid tools for depression, anxiety, relationship satisfaction, and family functioning, and explain scores in plain language.

Step Four: Observe directly. Ask the family to plan something together for ten minutes, such as a weekend outing. Watch who leads, who defers, who sabotages, and who repairs.

Step Five: Weigh risk and strength together. Every formulation must contain both a risk statement and a strength statement. Families are never only problems.

Step Six: Present the formulation as a draft. Invite additions and corrections. A formulation the family owns is a formulation they will work with.

Step Seven: Convert to a plan with measures. Three goals, matched interventions, a review date, and a simple way to track progress.

In practice, these steps will not always run in order. A crisis may interrupt step three; a disclosure may reopen step one. That is normal. The frame is a map, not a prison.

You see, this practical is not difficult. It needs patience, respect, and skill. If you follow these steps, you will help many families. Practice this practical with a classmate this week. One person will be the therapist, the others will be the family. Switch roles after twenty minutes. Write down what you learned.

Important note: Always respect culture and faith. In Nigeria and in many African homes, elders are respected, faith is important, and the family is extended. Do not fight culture. Work with culture. Use proverbs, prayers, and respectful language where appropriate. The family will accept help faster when they feel their values are honoured.

PART THREE-B: DEEPER TEACHING NOTES AND EXTENDED EXPLANATIONS
================================================================

My dear students, before we move to the techniques section, I want to go deeper with you. The notes that follow are extended teachings for this specific course. Listen slowly. Take notes. These are the ideas that separate a competent therapist from an excellent one.


DEEPER EXPLANATION ONE: CULTURE IS NOT A BARRIER
----------------------------------------------------------------

Many young therapists fear culture. They worry they will say the wrong thing, use the wrong greeting, or misunderstand a custom. So they stay silent, and silence makes families feel unseen. The better way is curiosity. Say, "Teach me about your family's way of doing things. How do decisions get made in your home?" When you ask with humility, families become your teachers. They relax. They trust you. Culture stops being a wall and becomes a door.

DEEPER EXPLANATION TWO: FOUR FAMILIES, FOUR WORLDS
----------------------------------------------------------------

Consider four families. A Yoruba family in Ibadan values respect, age, and celebration. A Hausa family in Kano may emphasise religious guidance, modesty, and extended kin. An Igbo family in Onitsha may value achievement, business, and strong kinship ties. A family that returned from abroad may blend two worlds and feel torn between them. Each family needs a slightly different approach. The Yoruba father may want a respectful greeting and a proverb. The Hausa father may want assurance that faith is honoured. The Igbo mother may want practical strategies and respect for her hard work. The returnee couple may need help bridging two cultural scripts. One therapy, many doors.

DEEPER EXPLANATION THREE: LANGUAGE AND THE INTERPRETER
----------------------------------------------------------------

Never underestimate the power of language. A mother can say "I am tired" in English and feel nothing. The same mother can say "O ti su mi" in Yoruba and begin to weep, because the mother tongue reaches the heart. Where you can, allow the family to speak in the language of their pain. When you need an interpreter, look at the client, not the interpreter. Speak in short sentences. Ask the client, "Did that come out right?" Thank the interpreter publicly. And never use a child as interpreter for sensitive matters, because it reverses the family hierarchy and burdens the child.

DEEPER EXPLANATION FOUR: FAITH AS A RESOURCE
----------------------------------------------------------------

Faith is not the enemy of therapy; it is often its strongest ally. A couple who prays together has a ritual of connection. A widow who believes her husband is at peace grieves with less despair. A father who believes he is accountable to God may accept correction more readily. Ask, "What does your faith say about forgiveness, patience, or suffering?" Then build on that. Do not argue with faith. Do not replace faith. Work beside it.

PRACTICAL FOR THIS WEEK: THE CULTURAL INTERVIEW
----------------------------------------------------------------

Find someone from a culture different from yours and interview them for thirty minutes about family life. Ask: How are decisions made? Who is respected? How are conflicts settled? What is a taboo in this culture? What are the strengths of this family system? Write a one-page summary and bring it to class. Do this every month for a year, and you will become a far better therapist than any textbook can make you.

COMMON MISTAKES
----------------------------------------------------------------

Do not assume you know a family because of their tribe. Do not treat culture as an excuse for harm; abuse is never culture. Do not impose your own religious views. Do not call a family "resistant" when they are simply protecting their values. And do not perform cultural knowledge; ask, listen, and be corrected with grace.

PART FOUR: KEY TECHNIQUES YOU MUST MASTER
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, in Advanced Cultural Competence in Therapy you must master practical techniques. Theory is good, but techniques change lives. Let me list the key techniques for this course, and I will explain how to use each one.

Technique 1: Cultural Formulation

How to use it: first explain why the technique exists, not only what it is. Families cooperate when they understand the purpose. Second, model it in a low-stakes example, such as how to greet a neighbour. Third, have the family rehearse the real situation while you coach gently. Fourth, ask, "What felt natural, and what felt strange?" Fifth, agree on a practice plan for the week.

Real life tip: use the family's own names and situations in the rehearsal. Practice that sounds like their real life transfers to their real life.

Common mistake: correcting too much too soon. Let the first attempt be imperfect. Confidence comes before polish.

Technique 2: Interpreter Use

How to use it: teach it in three sentences. One, what it is. Two, when to use it. Three, what to say. Then show it. Then have the family show you. Then ask each member to name one moment this week when they will use it. Write those moments down and review them next session.

Real life tip: ask a respected elder or pastor to endorse the technique publicly. Cultural endorsement speeds adoption dramatically.

Common mistake: using the technique only in the office. If it stays in the office, nothing changes at home.

Technique 3: Community Partnership

How to use it: start with a story. Tell a short story of a family who used the technique and what changed. Stories lower resistance and create hope. Then walk through the steps on paper so the family can see them. Then practise in pairs inside the room, with one person as the coach. End by asking, "What would make this hard at home, and how will you handle that?"

Real life tip: keep a small collection of true, anonymised stories from your practice and community. Stories travel further than instructions.

Common mistake: ignoring the obstacles the family names. If they say "my husband will not participate", that is the real problem to solve before the technique can work.

Technique 4: Proverb Work

How to use it: use the teach-back method. Explain the technique briefly, then ask the family to teach it back to you in their own words. If they cannot, the teaching was not clear enough. Then rehearse it once, and once more with a small obstacle added, such as someone interrupting. Finish with a written note in simple language that they can keep at home.

Real life tip: write the note in the language the family thinks in, or provide a short voice note if literacy is limited. Accessibility is respect.

Common mistake: assuming understanding because heads were nodding. Nodding is politeness; teach-back is proof.

Technique 5: Bias Check

How to use it: begin with a plain invitation. "This is a way to help you talk better. Let us try it together." Then demonstrate it yourself, with a colleague or with your own words. Let the family practise while you watch, and give kind, specific feedback. Send the skill home as a small assignment. A technique works only when it is practised at home.

Real life tip: many Nigerian families respect proverbs. Teach this technique with a short proverb so it is remembered long after the session.

Common mistake: do not stack techniques. One per session, mastered, is worth more than five half-learned.

Key Terms to Remember
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Culture: the shared values, language, customs, and meanings of a group; a resource to be respected, not a barrier to be overcome.
- Humility: the stance of a learner who does not assume they fully understand another person's culture or experience.
- Interpreter: a person who translates between languages; interpreters must be used carefully to protect accuracy and confidentiality.
- Acculturation: the process of adjusting to a new culture while keeping parts of the old one; common in families that migrate or return from abroad.
- Bias: a leaning or prejudice in judgement that may be unconscious; therapists must examine their own biases in supervision.
