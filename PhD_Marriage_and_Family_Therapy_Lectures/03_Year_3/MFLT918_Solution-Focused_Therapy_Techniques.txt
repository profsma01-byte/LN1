MODULE 1: Solution-Focused Principles
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Solution-Focused Principles. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Solution-focused therapy assumes families have strengths and solutions. Focus on what works, not only on what is wrong. It is brief, respectful, and hopeful.

Let me say this as simply as I can. Solution-Focused Principles is not a label you paste on a person. It is a way of seeing how people move together, the way you would watch dancers and notice who leads, who follows, and who keeps stepping on whose toes. Once you can see the dance, you can teach a new step.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Warri lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of solution-Focused principles became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. The therapist taught them a simple rule for the first month: "Nobody makes permanent decisions while the wound is fresh." That single rule reduced the fighting. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows solution-Focused principles in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Thank you both for coming. Before we begin, let me say what I already see: two people who are still here, still trying. Who would like to start?"
Wife: "I am tired of being ignored."
Therapist: "Ignored. That is a strong word. Can you give me one recent moment when you felt ignored?"
Wife: "Last night. I spoke and he kept looking at his phone."
Therapist: "And when she spoke and you kept looking at the phone, what was happening for you?"
Husband: "I was reading work messages. I did not know she needed me."
Therapist: "So one of you needed attention and the other did not know it was being asked for. That gap is where we will work."

Notice what the therapist did in that dialogue. She did not rush to solve. She named the feeling, slowed the pace, and invited the family to look at the pattern together. That is the difference between advice and therapy. Advice tells people what to do. Therapy helps people see clearly enough to decide for themselves.

Now let us name the mistakes young therapists make here. The first is taking sides too early. The moment you sound like the wife's lawyer, the husband stops listening. The second is rushing to comfort. Tears are not an emergency; they are a language. Let the tears speak before you hand out tissues. The third is talking more than the family. If you are speaking for more than a third of the session, you are working alone.

Let me test your understanding. A husband complains that his wife no longer respects him, and the wife complains that he no longer talks to her. Which is the presenting problem, and which is the pattern? Write your answer before you read on. The pattern is this: he feels disrespected, so he withdraws; she feels abandoned, so she criticises; he feels more disrespected. Two complaints, one dance.

Here is your practical step for this week. Choose one family you know well, perhaps your own, and for three days write down one interaction at dinner. Who spoke first? Who was interrupted? Who left the table first? Do not judge; just record. Patterns appear when you look with a pencil in your hand.

MODULE 2: Miracle Question
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Miracle Question. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

The miracle question asks: "If a miracle happened and the problem was solved, what would be different?" It helps families imagine solutions and set goals.

Now let me explain it plainly, the way I would explain it to a market woman in Ibadan. Miracle Question simply means that people in a family carry each other. If one person carries too much, the others feel lighter but weaker. If one person lets go, everyone stumbles. Balance is the goal, not perfection.

Now, let us look at this in real life. Let me tell you about a family in Lagos. They came to therapy because the matter of miracle question had turned their home into a place of silence. The father would not speak at dinner. The mother cried in the kitchen. The children learned to become invisible. When the therapist finally had all of them in one room, the truth came out gently: nobody had ever been taught how to handle miracle question, so each person had invented a private strategy. Those private strategies were colliding every day. The therapist did not scold anyone. She drew their strategies on paper, and for the first time the family saw the same picture. That picture became the beginning of a new conversation.

You see, my students, this example shows miracle question in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "When the two of you argue about this, what do you hear in your mind?"
Husband: "I hear that I am a failure."
Therapist: "And what do you hear, madam?"
Wife: "I hear that I am not a good wife."
Therapist: "You see, you are not really fighting each other. You are fighting two old voices. Let us separate the voices from the people, and then we can talk about the actual problem."

Watch how she used the genogram. She did not use it as paperwork; she used it as a story map. 'Who else in this family carried this burden?' she asked. The family discovered a pattern across three generations. Insight that comes from the family's own history is far more powerful than insight imported from a textbook.

Watch out for these mistakes. Do not label a family 'resistant' when they are simply afraid. Do not diagnose from one session. Do not allow one person to describe the whole family for all the others. And do not treat every problem as a trauma or every sadness as depression. Good therapists are slow to name and quick to listen.

Ask yourself. When a family says 'we have tried everything', what have they not tried? Usually they have not tried consistency, or they have not tried kindness, or they have not tried asking for help in the right place. Explore the word 'everything' with curiosity rather than acceptance.

This week, build one ritual. Choose a family you work with or your own home, and create a fifteen-minute routine that happens at the same time every day: a walk, a prayer, a cup of tea with no phones. Rituals are small investments with large returns. Report what happened.

MODULE 3: Exception Questions
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Exception Questions. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Exception questions ask: "When is the problem better or absent?" Exceptions reveal solutions. Help families do more of what works.

Here is the simple English version. Exception Questions means that behaviour in a family is not random. It follows a pattern, and patterns can be learned and unlearned. Nobody teaches a family to quarrel every Sunday, yet the quarrel appears like a visitor who knows the time. Therapy teaches the family to change the appointment.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Abuja, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving exception questions. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows exception questions in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Describe a normal Tuesday in your house."
Father: "We wake, we rush, we work, we come home, we sleep."
Therapist: "Where is the part of Tuesday when the family is actually together?"
Father: (silence)
Therapist: "That silence is important. It is telling us something. What would you like Tuesday to look like?"
Father: "I would like to eat with them."
Therapist: "Then let us build Tuesday around that meal."

Watch the therapist's moves. She used the family's own words instead of her textbook words. She asked one question at a time. She let the silence work. And when the emotion rose, she did not run from it; she stayed, steady as a wall, so the family could lean without falling.

Here is a list of errors to keep before your eyes. Do not assume a quiet family is a healthy family. Do not assume a loud family is a violent family. Do not assume that the person who called for help is the person who needs the most help. And do not assume the problem is new; often it has been growing quietly for years.

Here is a hard one. A couple wants you to tell them whether to divorce. What is your answer? You do not decide for them. You help them clarify their values, their options, their children's needs, and their readiness. Discernment is not advice-giving; it is accompanying a decision.

Your assignment is to write a letter you will never send. Write to a family member who hurt you, describing what you felt and what you needed. Do not send it. Then notice whether your body feels lighter. Unspoken pain becomes a therapist's blind spot; spoken pain becomes compassion.

MODULE 4: Scaling Questions
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Scaling Questions. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Scaling asks: "On a scale of 1 to 10, where are you now? What would 1 point better look like?" Scaling makes goals clear and measurable.

In plain words, Scaling Questions is about who carries the weight and who carries the blame. In unhealthy families, the weakest person often carries both. In healthy families, the weight is shared and blame is replaced by responsibility. That shift is the heart of our work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Abeokuta lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of scaling questions became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. The therapist taught them a simple rule for the first month: "Nobody makes permanent decisions while the wound is fresh." That single rule reduced the fighting. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows scaling questions in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your mother called you lazy. What happened inside you when she said it?"
Teenager: "I stopped listening."
Therapist: "That is honest. And what would you have wanted her to say instead?"
Teenager: "That she is worried, not that I am useless."
Therapist: "That is a sentence we can work with. Mother, could you say what you actually feel, using his sentence as a guide?"
Mother: "I am worried about your future. I am afraid for you."
Teenager: "Then say that. I can hear that."

Observe the moment of repair. The husband raised his voice, and the therapist said calmly, 'Let us pause for a breath.' That single sentence modelled what the couple needed to learn. Therapists teach by what they do in the room, not only by what they say.

Let me point out what goes wrong. Therapists often chase the identified patient and ignore the marriage. They often focus on the individual's feelings and miss the family's pattern. They often promise confidentiality that they cannot keep. Each of these errors costs trust, and trust is expensive to rebuild.

Test yourself on ethics. Your client asks you to keep a secret from her husband that affects their marriage. Do you agree? No. You explain at intake that you do not hold secrets that affect the relationship, and you help her find the courage to tell the truth with support.

This week, practise the pause. Before you give advice in any conversation, wait five seconds and ask one question instead. Notice what changes. Most of us speak too soon; the pause is where the other person's wisdom gets a chance to appear.

MODULE 5: Compliments and Strengths
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Compliments and Strengths. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Compliments highlight strengths. Genuine compliments build hope and cooperation. Use them generously and sincerely.

Let me use a market example. A trader in Bodija market knows the price of every item and the mood of every customer. That is systems awareness. Compliments and Strengths asks you to bring the same awareness into the family. Who is tense today? Who is quiet? What changed since last week? Read the family the way a trader reads a market.

Now, let us look at this in real life. Let me tell you about a family in Ilorin. They came to therapy because the matter of compliments and strengths had turned their home into a place of silence. The father would not speak at dinner. The mother cried in the kitchen. The children learned to become invisible. When the therapist finally had all of them in one room, the truth came out gently: nobody had ever been taught how to handle compliments and strengths, so each person had invented a private strategy. Those private strategies were colliding every day. The therapist did not scold anyone. She drew their strategies on paper, and for the first time the family saw the same picture. That picture became the beginning of a new conversation.

You see, my students, this example shows compliments and strengths in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "On a scale of one to ten, how safe does this home feel to each of you?"
Wife: "Four."
Husband: "Seven."
Therapist: "Two different numbers under one roof. That is worth understanding. Wife, what makes it a four?"
Wife: "I never know his mood when he comes in."
Therapist: "Husband, what would it take for her number to rise by one point this week?"
Husband: "I can call her before I leave work."
Therapist: "That is a real, doable step. Let us test it this week."

Observe three skills in that exchange. First, joining: the therapist entered the family's world before asking anything of it. Second, reframing: she changed the meaning of the behaviour without denying the pain. Third, homework: she sent the family home with one small, clear task. Those three moves are worth more than a hundred clever interpretations.

Let me speak candidly. The most dangerous mistake is not a technique error; it is a character error. A therapist who gossips, exaggerates, or plays favourites will harm families no matter how many models he knows. Skill without integrity is a sharp knife in a child's hand.

Here is a question to stretch you. The grandmother is the most powerful person in the home, but she refuses to attend sessions. How do you work with the system without her in the room? You honour her through the family, you ask permission before changes, and you invite her through a respected messenger. Systems change through respect, not confrontation.

Practise this sentence this week: 'Help me understand.' Use it whenever you feel tempted to correct someone. Notice how the other person's body relaxes when they hear it. That sentence is a key that opens locked rooms.

MODULE 6: First Session Focus
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to First Session Focus. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Solution-focused first sessions focus on goals, exceptions, and strengths. Families leave with hope and tasks. Brief work can be powerful.

Let me put it simply. In plain terms, First Session Focus is the study of what happens between people, not just inside them. A man may be patient at work and impatient at home, and the difference is not in his character alone; it is in the patterns of the home. Change the pattern, and you often change the man.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Uyo, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving first session focus. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows first session focus in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You said your son is stubborn. Tell me about a time this week when he was not stubborn."
Mother: (pause) "On Sunday, when we cooked together."
Therapist: "What was different about Sunday?"
Mother: "I was not rushing. I was just with him."
Therapist: "So connection changed his behaviour before discipline did. That is a very important discovery. What would it take to have two Sundays in a week?"

Look closely at her language. She used 'we' more than 'you'. She said 'we will try something' rather than 'you must change'. That language builds a team. A family that feels teamed with the therapist will take risks; a family that feels examined will hide.

Let me warn you about three common errors. First, ignoring the body. A family in crisis may be hungry, exhausted, or sick, and no amount of talking will help until those needs are addressed. Second, ignoring the money. Many 'communication problems' are actually money problems wearing a mask. Third, ignoring the community. If the church, mosque, or extended family opposes the therapy, the therapy may fail before it begins.

Let me pose a case. A wife tells you in private that she is afraid of her husband, but in the joint session she says everything is fine. What do you do? You do not confront her publicly. You make a safety plan privately, you assess the danger carefully, and you never conduct joint sessions where violence is active without a plan.

This week, sit with one family and ask only one question: 'What is the one thing you wish we could change first?' Then be quiet. Let them fill the silence. Whatever they say first is usually the doorway to everything else.

MODULE 7: Homework in Solution-Focused Work
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Homework in Solution-Focused Work. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Homework includes exception tasks: notice what works. Families observe solutions between sessions. Observation builds change.

Let me give you the short version. Homework in Solution-Focused Work says that every family is trying to solve a problem, even when the solution looks like the problem. The husband who stays late at the office may be avoiding a fight. The wife who shouts may be trying to be heard. Look for the purpose behind the behaviour before you judge it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Warri lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of homework in solution-Focused work became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. The therapist taught them a simple rule for the first month: "Nobody makes permanent decisions while the wound is fresh." That single rule reduced the fighting. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows homework in solution-Focused work in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Everyone in this family has been carrying something alone. Today I want us to put some of it down. Who will start?"
Grandmother: "I will. I am tired of being the one who keeps the peace."
Therapist: "Thank you for your courage. And when you keep the peace, who is freed from doing it?"
Grandmother: "Everyone else."
Therapist: "So your burden has become their permission to stay silent. Let us see if we can share that load differently."

Did you notice how she handled the resistance? She did not argue. She agreed with the part that was true and then asked a question that opened a door. That is the therapist's art: to be firm without being rigid, and gentle without being soft.

These mistakes are common and costly. Do not use technical words to impress. Do not take phone calls in session. Do not allow a session to run without a plan. Do not forget the homework from the previous week. Families notice whether you remember them, and remembering is a form of respect.

Here is a question for you. If a family says 'our son is the problem', what are three other places you would look before accepting that? Think: the marriage, the family rules, and the wider system of school and relatives. A therapist who looks only where the family points will often miss the real wound.

Your practical task is to observe a disagreement without intervening. Watch two people argue, in your family or at work, and notice the cycle: who pursues, who withdraws, who rescues. Write the cycle as a diagram. Bring it to class, with names changed, and we will study it together.

Use play, scaling, and compliments with children. Children respond to hope and strengths. Keep language simple and fun.

Here is another way of saying it. Solution-Focused with Children is about the rules nobody wrote down. You see, my students, this example shows solution-Focused with children in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I am going to ask a strange question. If the problem disappeared tonight, what is the first small thing you would notice tomorrow morning?"
Husband: "She would greet me at the door."
Wife: "He would ask about my day."
Therapist: "So the miracle is not a mansion or a lottery win. It is a greeting and a question. That tells me your marriage is closer to healing than you think."

Notice how she ended. She did not end on the problem; she ended on a picture of the family's preferred future. 'Imagine next Sunday, what would be different?' The family left with hope in their hands. Hope is not decoration; it is the fuel of change.

Now, the mistakes. Do not interrupt a father's story to correct his theology. Do not correct a mother's grammar while she is pouring out her heart. Do not ask six questions at once. Do not fill every silence. Silence is often the moment a family is deciding to trust you, so let it breathe.

Here is a question about your own heart. Which family would you find hardest to treat, and why? The answer reveals your blind spot. A therapist who knows his blind spot can guard against it; a therapist who denies it will be ruled by it.

This week, do a listening audit. Count how many times you interrupt someone in a day. Do not scold yourself; just count. Awareness is the first step. Next week, try to reduce the number by half.

MODULE 9: Solution-Focused with Culture
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Solution-Focused with Culture. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Adapt solution-focused work to culture: use proverbs, faith, and community strengths. Hope is universal.

Let me explain it as a doctor would. A doctor does not treat a symptom without examining the body. Solution-Focused with Culture is the examination of the family body. The symptom is only the complaint at the door; the illness is in the circulation of love, respect, and truth between members. Treat the circulation, and the symptom often fades.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Abuja, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving solution-Focused with culture. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows solution-Focused with culture in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You have both said the word respect many times. What does respect look like in your house, in actions, not in theory?"
Wife: "He would not correct me in front of his mother."
Husband: "She would not discuss our money with her sister."
Therapist: "Now we have something specific. These are not feelings; these are agreements. Let us write them down and test them for two weeks."

Let me point out what she avoided. She did not ask 'Why did you do that?' which invites defence. She asked 'What happened just before that?' which invites observation. Small changes in question shape produce large changes in conversation.

Here is my counsel. Do not confuse culture with abuse. Do not confuse respect with silence. Do not confuse forgiveness with pretending nothing happened. Do not confuse a husband's provision with his presence. And do not confuse a wife's submission with her having no voice.

Check your thinking here. What is the difference between a boundary and a wall? A boundary says 'this is where I end and you begin, and I can still love you'. A wall says 'you cannot reach me'. Families often build walls and call them boundaries. Your task is to turn walls into doors.

Your assignment this week is small but powerful. Ask one family member, 'What is one thing I could do differently that would make you feel more respected?' Then listen without defending. Write down the answer word for word. Bring it to class. This exercise teaches you how it feels to be on the receiving end of your own questions.

MODULE 10: Solution-Focused and Systems
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Solution-Focused and Systems. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Solution-focused work fits systems thinking. Small changes in patterns bring big changes. Focus on interactions, not only individuals.

Now, why does Solution-Focused and Systems matter so much in our setting? Because in Nigeria, a family is rarely just two people. It is grandparents, uncles, aunties, in-laws, pastors, imams, and neighbours. Whatever we study in this course must be applied with that wider family in view. A therapy that ignores the extended family will be outvoted by it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Abeokuta lost their home to a flood. In the shelter, the matter of solution-Focused and systems became an unexpected battleground. The father wanted to rebuild immediately; the mother wanted to move to another town; the children wanted everything to be as it was. The therapist taught them a simple rule for the first month: "Nobody makes permanent decisions while the wound is fresh." That single rule reduced the fighting. With time, and with the help of their church and a community group, the family made decisions they could live with. Crisis reveals a family's fault lines, but it can also reveal its foundations.

You see, my students, this example shows solution-Focused and systems in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I notice you have not looked at your husband since you sat down."
Wife: "There is nothing to look at."
Therapist: "That sentence carries a lot of pain. I am not here to judge it. I am here to understand it. When did you stop looking at him?"
Wife: (crying) "When he stopped looking at me."
Therapist: "Then let us begin exactly there. One look, one sentence, one honest moment."

Here is a subtle skill. She asked each member the same question in turn: 'What would you want the others to understand about you?' The quiet daughter spoke last and said the most important thing. Equal airtime is not a technique of fairness; it is a technique of discovery.

Here are the traps to avoid. Do not confuse understanding with agreement. You can understand why a husband shouts without approving of the shouting. Do not use your own marriage as the standard for every family. Do not quote research to argue with a grandmother; honour her first, then offer another view. And never promise a result you cannot control, such as 'your marriage will surely be saved'.

Consider this scenario. A father brings his teenage son for 'discipline problems' but refuses to discuss his own drinking. How do you proceed? You join with the father's concern, you keep the son's welfare central, and you gently raise the drinking as one part of the picture, without making it a condition of your respect.

Your task is to interview an elder. Ask an older person in your community: 'How were conflicts settled in your family when you were young? What did your parents do that you would keep, and what would you change?' Write one page. You will learn more about culture in one hour than in ten textbooks.

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Brief Therapy Ethics. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Brief therapy must still be ethical: assess risk, set clear goals, and refer when needed. Brief does not mean careless.

Let me be practical. When Brief Therapy Ethics is handled badly, the damage rarely stays with one person. It moves to the children, then to the children's children. When it is handled well, the benefit moves in exactly the same way. That is why your work in this course is not a small thing; it is generational work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. Let me tell you about a family in Ilorin. They came to therapy because the matter of brief therapy ethics had turned their home into a place of silence. The father would not speak at dinner. The mother cried in the kitchen. The children learned to become invisible. When the therapist finally had all of them in one room, the truth came out gently: nobody had ever been taught how to handle brief therapy ethics, so each person had invented a private strategy. You see, my students, this example shows brief therapy ethics in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your daughter says she feels unheard. What do you hear when she says that?"
Father: "I hear that I have failed as a father."
Therapist: "So her pain lands on your shame, and your shame responds with anger. Do you see the cycle?"
Father: "I do."
Therapist: "Good. Then the next time she speaks, try to hear her pain without hearing a verdict about you. That is the skill we will practise today."

Here is the skill to copy. She listened for the feeling behind the complaint. The wife said 'he is careless', but the therapist heard 'I am afraid I do not matter to him'. When she named that fear gently, the wife wept and the husband softened. Accurate empathy is a surgical instrument.

Let me speak plainly about errors. The worst mistake is making the family feel judged. Once shame fills the room, learning stops. The second worst is rescuing the family from every discomfort. Growth lives just beyond discomfort, and if you remove all discomfort, you remove the growth. Walk with them through the fire; do not carry them.

Ask yourself this. If you only had three questions to ask any family, what would they be? Here are mine: What has been happening? What have you already tried? What would be different if this were better? Those three questions give you a story, a history of solutions, and a goal.

Practise the phrase 'say more about that'. Use it three times a day in ordinary conversations. You will be amazed how much people have been waiting to say and how rarely they are invited.

MODULE 12: Integrating Solution-Focused

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Integrating Solution-Focused. This is a very important topic in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Integrate solution-focused work with narrative, structural, and cultural models. Hope plus skill heals.

It is a lens that changes what you see in the first ten minutes of a session. Two therapists can sit in the same room with the same family; the one who understands Integrating Solution-Focused will see a pattern, while the other will only see a problem.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Uyo, two brothers had not spoken for six years. The dispute had started over land, but it had grown into something larger involving integrating solution-Focused. Their ageing mother begged them to reconcile. The therapist met each brother alone first, then brought them together. She did not ask who was right. She asked each man one question: "What do you want your mother to remember about her last years?" Both men wept. Six years of silence did not end in one session, but they agreed to share a meal with their mother. That meal became the first brick in a rebuilt road.

You see, my students, this example shows integrating solution-Focused in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Both of you have said you want the marriage to work. What has each of you done in the last month that made it a little better?"
Husband: "I stopped shouting."
Wife: "I stopped bringing my mother into our arguments."
Therapist: "So you are both already working. That means I am not starting from zero. I am joining a project that is already underway."

Watch the therapist handle the secret. When the mother hinted at something painful, the therapist did not pounce. She said, 'There seems to be something heavy here. We do not have to open it today, but when you are ready, I will be here.' Safety was preserved and the door was left open.

Let me caution you about three things. First, the rescue fantasy: believing you must save the family yourself. You are a guide, not a saviour. Second, the expert trap: speaking as if you have never struggled at home. Humility opens doors that expertise cannot. Third, the speed trap: rushing change to prove you are effective. Slow, steady change lasts.

Think about this. A couple has stopped fighting. Is that progress? Not always. Some couples stop fighting because they have given up. Check for warmth, humour, and touch. Peace without connection can be the silence before separation.

REAL LIFE PRACTICAL 1: Miracle Question Practice
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, theory without practice is empty. So let us do a real life practical on Miracle Question Practice. We will work through it in the style of The Clinical Interview, which is one of the practical frames you will use in different settings.

Scenario: Use the miracle question with a couple who fights. Help them imagine a better marriage. Use scaling with a parent who rates discipline as 2 out of 10. Help them move to 3.

Let me walk you through this practical step by step, as you would do it in real life.

Step One: Prepare yourself before the family arrives. Read the referral note, clear your desk, silence your phone, and take three calm breaths. A regulated therapist is the first intervention. Families read your face before they hear your words.

Step Two: Contract and consent. Explain what will happen today, how long it will take, and what confidentiality means and does not mean. Invite questions. A family that understands the rules will take more risks.

Step Three: Gather each voice separately. Ask each member, beginning with the eldest or the one who called, to describe the problem in their own words while the others listen without interrupting. Note the words each person chooses; those words are your map.

Step Four: Watch the interaction. Invite a small disagreement and observe rather than lead. Who interrupts? Who soothes? Who appeals to an absent relative? The live pattern is more reliable than any report.

Step Five: Formulate aloud. Share your hypothesis in ordinary language and invite correction: 'Here is what I think I am seeing. Is this right?' Families correct therapists generously, and the corrected hypothesis belongs to them.

Step Six: Design one experiment. Choose a single small change that tests the hypothesis, such as a ten-minute nightly check-in or a boundary phrased kindly. Write it down together and ask for a confidence rating.

Step Seven: Close with dignity. Summarise, thank each member by name, and confirm the next appointment. Then write your notes within the hour, while the room is still alive in your memory.

What families remember is not the model you used. They remember whether you were honest, whether you were fair, and whether you gave them something real to do when they got home. Every step here serves those three memories.

You see, this practical is not difficult. It needs patience, respect, and skill. If you follow these steps, you will help many families. Practice this practical with a classmate this week. One person will be the therapist, the others will be the family. Switch roles after twenty minutes. Write down what you learned.

Important note: Always respect culture and faith. In Nigeria and in many African homes, elders are respected, faith is important, and the family is extended. Do not fight culture. Work with culture. Use proverbs, prayers, and respectful language where appropriate. The family will accept help faster when they feel their values are honoured.

DEEPER EXPLANATION ONE: THE MIRACLE QUESTION DONE WELL
----------------------------------------------------------------

The miracle question is not a magic trick. It is an invitation to imagine. "Suppose tonight, while you are asleep, a miracle happens and the problems that brought you here are solved. But because you are asleep, you do not know it happened. When you wake tomorrow, what is the first small thing you would notice that tells you things are different?" Listen carefully. The wife may say, "He would make me tea." The husband may say, "She would not shout in the morning." Those small pictures are the seeds of goals. If the answer is vague, ask for details: Who would notice first? What would they see? What would you be doing differently? The miracle question works because it moves the family from complaint to possibility.

DEEPER EXPLANATION TWO: EXCEPTIONS ARE GOLD
----------------------------------------------------------------

Exceptions are times when the problem does not happen or happens less. A couple who fights every night also had a peaceful Saturday last week. A mother whose son refuses food also had one meal he ate happily. A teenager who skips school also attended one class fully. The solution-focused therapist asks, "What was different about that day? Who did what? How did you make it happen? Can you do it again on purpose?" Exceptions prove that the family already has the resources it needs. Your job is not to supply the solution; it is to help them notice and repeat it.

DEEPER EXPLANATION THREE: SCALING WITH PURPOSE
----------------------------------------------------------------

Scaling questions turn vague feelings into visible movement. "On a scale of one to ten, where one is 'we cannot speak without shouting' and ten is 'we speak with respect every day,' where are you today?" Suppose the answer is three. Ask, "What makes it a three and not a one?" That question highlights progress. Then ask, "What would a three-and-a-half look like this week?" Small steps are believable. A family who cannot imagine ten can imagine three-and-a-half, and three-and-a-half moves them forward.

DEEPER EXPLANATION FOUR: THE FAMILY THAT SOLVED ITSELF
----------------------------------------------------------------

A family in Lagos came because their eleven-year-old son was fighting at school. The therapist asked about exceptions. The mother remembered that the boy was calm during the weeks he visited his grandmother in the village. The father remembered that the fighting stopped after a camping trip with his uncle. The therapist asked, "What do grandmother and uncle do differently?" The family realised both gave the boy undivided attention and physical work, like fetching water and gardening. The therapist asked, "Could we build some of that into his week in Lagos?" They created a plan: a Saturday morning with Dad fixing things in the compound, and a weekly call with Grandmother. Within a month, the fighting reduced. The therapist did not solve the problem; the family did, with better questions.

PRACTICAL FOR THIS WEEK: THE EXCEPTION HUNT
----------------------------------------------------------------

Choose one problem in your own life or in a family you know. For one week, keep a simple log: on the days the problem was smaller, what was different? Who was present? What did you do? What time of day was it? At the end of the week, write three patterns you notice. This exercise trains your eye to see what is already working.

COMMON MISTAKES
----------------------------------------------------------------

Do not use solution-focused work to ignore pain or danger. Do not ask the miracle question in a crisis. Do not push positivity on a grieving family. Do not abandon the question if the family finds it strange; explain it simply and try again. And do not forget that some problems need structural and safety interventions before solutions can grow.

PART FOUR: KEY TECHNIQUES YOU MUST MASTER
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, in Solution-Focused Therapy Techniques you must master practical techniques. Theory is good, but techniques change lives. Let me list the key techniques for this course, and I will explain how to use each one.

Technique 1: Miracle Question

How to use it: first explain why the technique exists, not only what it is. Families cooperate when they understand the purpose. Second, model it in a low-stakes example, such as how to greet a neighbour. Third, have the family rehearse the real situation while you coach gently. Fourth, ask, "What felt natural, and what felt strange?" Fifth, agree on a practice plan for the week.

Real life tip: use the family's own names and situations in the rehearsal. Practice that sounds like their real life transfers to their real life.

Common mistake: correcting too much too soon. Let the first attempt be imperfect. Confidence comes before polish.

Technique 2: Exception Questions

How to use it: teach it in three sentences. One, what it is. Two, when to use it. Three, what to say. Then show it. Then have the family show you. Then ask each member to name one moment this week when they will use it. Write those moments down and review them next session.

Real life tip: ask a respected elder or pastor to endorse the technique publicly. Cultural endorsement speeds adoption dramatically.

Common mistake: using the technique only in the office. If it stays in the office, nothing changes at home.

Technique 3: Scaling

How to use it: start with a story. Tell a short story of a family who used the technique and what changed. Stories lower resistance and create hope. Then walk through the steps on paper so the family can see them. Then practise in pairs inside the room, with one person as the coach. End by asking, "What would make this hard at home, and how will you handle that?"

Real life tip: keep a small collection of true, anonymised stories from your practice and community. Stories travel further than instructions.

Common mistake: ignoring the obstacles the family names. If they say "my husband will not participate", that is the real problem to solve before the technique can work.

Technique 4: Compliments

How to use it: use the teach-back method. Explain the technique briefly, then ask the family to teach it back to you in their own words. If they cannot, the teaching was not clear enough. Then rehearse it once, and once more with a small obstacle added, such as someone interrupting. Finish with a written note in simple language that they can keep at home.

Real life tip: write the note in the language the family thinks in, or provide a short voice note if literacy is limited. Accessibility is respect.

Common mistake: assuming understanding because heads were nodding. Nodding is politeness; teach-back is proof.

Technique 5: Exception Tasks

How to use it: begin with a plain invitation. "This is a way to help you talk better. Let us try it together." Then demonstrate it yourself, with a colleague or with your own words. Let the family practise while you watch, and give kind, specific feedback. Send the skill home as a small assignment. A technique works only when it is practised at home.

Real life tip: many Nigerian families respect proverbs. Teach this technique with a short proverb so it is remembered long after the session.

Common mistake: do not stack techniques. One per session, mastered, is worth more than five half-learned.

Key Terms to Remember
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Solution: the change a family is seeking; solution-focused therapy begins by clarifying and building it.
- Exception: a time when a problem does not occur or is less severe; solution-focused therapy builds on these moments.
- Miracle: a solution-focused question asking how life would differ if the problem were solved.
- Scaling: a solution-focused technique that uses numeric ratings to make change visible and measurable.
- Strengths: the resources, virtues, and capacities a family already possesses.
