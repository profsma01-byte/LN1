MODULE 1: Law and Therapy
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Law and Therapy. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Therapists must know relevant laws: child protection, confidentiality, and family law. Law varies by state and country. Know your local law and consult lawyers.

Let me say this as simply as I can. Law and Therapy is not a label you paste on a person. It is a way of seeing how people move together, the way you would watch dancers and notice who leads, who follows, and who keeps stepping on whose toes. Once you can see the dance, you can teach a new step.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Lagos, a couple sat in front of a therapist and argued about law and therapy for twenty minutes without pausing. Each one had a list of the other's failures. The therapist listened carefully, then asked one question: "If this problem were solved tonight, what would tomorrow morning look like in your house?" The room went quiet. The husband said, "We would eat breakfast together." The wife said, "He would ask me how I slept." Suddenly the argument was no longer about blame. It was about a lost breakfast and an unasked question. From that small picture, the therapist helped them build a plan for law and therapy that felt human, not legal.

You see, my students, this example shows law and therapy in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Thank you both for coming. Before we begin, let me say what I already see: two people who are still here, still trying. Who would like to start?"
Wife: "I am tired of being ignored."
Therapist: "Ignored. That is a strong word. Can you give me one recent moment when you felt ignored?"
Wife: "Last night. I spoke and he kept looking at his phone."
Therapist: "And when she spoke and you kept looking at the phone, what was happening for you?"
Husband: "I was reading work messages. I did not know she needed me."
Therapist: "So one of you needed attention and the other did not know it was being asked for. That gap is where we will work."

Notice what the therapist did in that dialogue. She did not rush to solve. She named the feeling, slowed the pace, and invited the family to look at the pattern together. That is the difference between advice and therapy. Advice tells people what to do. Therapy helps people see clearly enough to decide for themselves.

Now let us name the mistakes young therapists make here. The first is taking sides too early. The moment you sound like the wife's lawyer, the husband stops listening. The second is rushing to comfort. Tears are not an emergency; they are a language. Let the tears speak before you hand out tissues. The third is talking more than the family. If you are speaking for more than a third of the session, you are working alone.

Let me test your understanding. A husband complains that his wife no longer respects him, and the wife complains that he no longer talks to her. Which is the presenting problem, and which is the pattern? Write your answer before you read on. The pattern is this: he feels disrespected, so he withdraws; she feels abandoned, so she criticises; he feels more disrespected. Two complaints, one dance.

Here is your practical step for this week. Choose one family you know well, perhaps your own, and for three days write down one interaction at dinner. Who spoke first? Who was interrupted? Who left the table first? Do not judge; just record. Patterns appear when you look with a pencil in your hand.

MODULE 2: Confidentiality Law
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Confidentiality Law. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Confidentiality has legal limits: danger, abuse, and court orders. Explain limits clearly. Document decisions. Law protects privacy and safety.

Now let me explain it plainly, the way I would explain it to a market woman in Ibadan. Confidentiality Law simply means that people in a family carry each other. If one person carries too much, the others feel lighter but weaker. If one person lets go, everyone stumbles. Balance is the goal, not perfection.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Abuja ran a successful business together and a failing home. The father managed the shop. The mother managed the accounts. The children managed their own loneliness. The presenting problem was confidentiality law, but the real problem was that the family had become a company with no living room. The therapist invited them to separate the two: business meetings at the shop, family time at home, no ledgers at dinner. It sounded simple. It was not. It took three months of practice before the father could sit through a meal without discussing profit. On the day he finally did, his youngest daughter said, "Daddy, you are here." He wept.

You see, my students, this example shows confidentiality law in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "When the two of you argue about this, what do you hear in your mind?"
Husband: "I hear that I am a failure."
Therapist: "And what do you hear, madam?"
Wife: "I hear that I am not a good wife."
Therapist: "You see, you are not really fighting each other. You are fighting two old voices. Let us separate the voices from the people, and then we can talk about the actual problem."

Watch how she used the genogram. She did not use it as paperwork; she used it as a story map. 'Who else in this family carried this burden?' she asked. The family discovered a pattern across three generations. Insight that comes from the family's own history is far more powerful than insight imported from a textbook.

Watch out for these mistakes. Do not label a family 'resistant' when they are simply afraid. Do not diagnose from one session. Do not allow one person to describe the whole family for all the others. And do not treat every problem as a trauma or every sadness as depression. Good therapists are slow to name and quick to listen.

Ask yourself. When a family says 'we have tried everything', what have they not tried? Usually they have not tried consistency, or they have not tried kindness, or they have not tried asking for help in the right place. Explore the word 'everything' with curiosity rather than acceptance.

This week, build one ritual. Choose a family you work with or your own home, and create a fifteen-minute routine that happens at the same time every day: a walk, a prayer, a cup of tea with no phones. Rituals are small investments with large returns. Report what happened.

MODULE 3: Child Protection Law
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Child Protection Law. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Child abuse must be reported according to law. Know reporting duties and procedures. Protect children first. Document reports.

Here is the simple English version. Child Protection Law means that behaviour in a family is not random. It follows a pattern, and patterns can be learned and unlearned. Nobody teaches a family to quarrel every Sunday, yet the quarrel appears like a visitor who knows the time. Therapy teaches the family to change the appointment.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Abeokuta, a husband was transferred to another state for work. The family had to decide whether to move together or live apart. The question of child protection law sat at the centre of every conversation. The therapist helped them do a structured decision exercise: each partner listed their fears, their hopes, their non-negotiables, and their willingness to compromise. Written down, the lists were surprisingly compatible. What had looked like an impossible conflict was actually a failure of process. They chose to move together for two years, with a review date. Decisions made in daylight are easier to live with than decisions made in fear.

You see, my students, this example shows child protection law in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Describe a normal Tuesday in your house."
Father: "We wake, we rush, we work, we come home, we sleep."
Therapist: "Where is the part of Tuesday when the family is actually together?"
Father: (silence)
Therapist: "That silence is important. It is telling us something. What would you like Tuesday to look like?"
Father: "I would like to eat with them."
Therapist: "Then let us build Tuesday around that meal."

Watch the therapist's moves. She used the family's own words instead of her textbook words. She asked one question at a time. She let the silence work. And when the emotion rose, she did not run from it; she stayed, steady as a wall, so the family could lean without falling.

Here is a list of errors to keep before your eyes. Do not assume a quiet family is a healthy family. Do not assume a loud family is a violent family. Do not assume that the person who called for help is the person who needs the most help. And do not assume the problem is new; often it has been growing quietly for years.

Here is a hard one. A couple wants you to tell them whether to divorce. What is your answer? You do not decide for them. You help them clarify their values, their options, their children's needs, and their readiness. Discernment is not advice-giving; it is accompanying a decision.

Your assignment is to write a letter you will never send. Write to a family member who hurt you, describing what you felt and what you needed. Do not send it. Then notice whether your body feels lighter. Unspoken pain becomes a therapist's blind spot; spoken pain becomes compassion.

MODULE 4: Divorce and Custody
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Divorce and Custody. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Divorce and custody involve courts. Therapists may provide reports and testimony. Be neutral, factual, and child-focused. Do not take sides in court.

In plain words, Divorce and Custody is about who carries the weight and who carries the blame. In unhealthy families, the weakest person often carries both. In healthy families, the weight is shared and blame is replaced by responsibility. That shift is the heart of our work.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Ilorin, a couple sat in front of a therapist and argued about divorce and custody for twenty minutes without pausing. Each one had a list of the other's failures. The therapist listened carefully, then asked one question: "If this problem were solved tonight, what would tomorrow morning look like in your house?" The room went quiet. The husband said, "We would eat breakfast together." The wife said, "He would ask me how I slept." Suddenly the argument was no longer about blame. It was about a lost breakfast and an unasked question. From that small picture, the therapist helped them build a plan for divorce and custody that felt human, not legal.

You see, my students, this example shows divorce and custody in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Your mother called you lazy. What happened inside you when she said it?"
Teenager: "I stopped listening."
Therapist: "That is honest. And what would you have wanted her to say instead?"
Teenager: "That she is worried, not that I am useless."
Therapist: "That is a sentence we can work with. Mother, could you say what you actually feel, using his sentence as a guide?"
Mother: "I am worried about your future. I am afraid for you."
Teenager: "Then say that. I can hear that."

Observe the moment of repair. The husband raised his voice, and the therapist said calmly, 'Let us pause for a breath.' That single sentence modelled what the couple needed to learn. Therapists teach by what they do in the room, not only by what they say.

Let me point out what goes wrong. Therapists often chase the identified patient and ignore the marriage. They often focus on the individual's feelings and miss the family's pattern. They often promise confidentiality that they cannot keep. Each of these errors costs trust, and trust is expensive to rebuild.

Test yourself on ethics. Your client asks you to keep a secret from her husband that affects their marriage. Do you agree? No. You explain at intake that you do not hold secrets that affect the relationship, and you help her find the courage to tell the truth with support.

This week, practise the pause. Before you give advice in any conversation, wait five seconds and ask one question instead. Notice what changes. Most of us speak too soon; the pause is where the other person's wisdom gets a chance to appear.

Domestic violence laws protect victims. Know protection orders and legal resources. Safety and legal advice go together.

Let me use a market example. A trader in Bodija market knows the price of every item and the mood of every customer. That is systems awareness. Domestic Violence Law asks you to bring the same awareness into the family. Who is tense today? Who is quiet? What changed since last week? Read the family the way a trader reads a market.

Now, let us look at this in real life. A family in Uyo ran a successful business together and a failing home. The father managed the shop. The mother managed the accounts. The children managed their own loneliness. The presenting problem was domestic violence law, but the real problem was that the family had become a company with no living room. The therapist invited them to separate the two: business meetings at the shop, family time at home, no ledgers at dinner. It sounded simple. It was not. It took three months of practice before the father could sit through a meal without discussing profit. On the day he finally did, his youngest daughter said, "Daddy, you are here." He wept.

You see, my students, this example shows domestic violence law in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "On a scale of one to ten, how safe does this home feel to each of you?"
Wife: "Four."
Husband: "Seven."
Therapist: "Two different numbers under one roof. That is worth understanding. Wife, what makes it a four?"
Wife: "I never know his mood when he comes in."
Therapist: "Husband, what would it take for her number to rise by one point this week?"
Husband: "I can call her before I leave work."
Therapist: "That is a real, doable step. Let us test it this week."

Observe three skills in that exchange. First, joining: the therapist entered the family's world before asking anything of it. Second, reframing: she changed the meaning of the behaviour without denying the pain. Third, homework: she sent the family home with one small, clear task. Those three moves are worth more than a hundred clever interpretations.

Let me speak candidly. The most dangerous mistake is not a technique error; it is a character error. A therapist who gossips, exaggerates, or plays favourites will harm families no matter how many models he knows. Skill without integrity is a sharp knife in a child's hand.

Here is a question to stretch you. The grandmother is the most powerful person in the home, but she refuses to attend sessions. How do you work with the system without her in the room? You honour her through the family, you ask permission before changes, and you invite her through a respected messenger. Systems change through respect, not confrontation.

Practise this sentence this week: 'Help me understand.' Use it whenever you feel tempted to correct someone. Notice how the other person's body relaxes when they hear it. That sentence is a key that opens locked rooms.

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Marriage Law. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Marriage laws vary by culture and state. Understand customary, religious, and statutory marriage. Legal knowledge helps families.

Let me put it simply. In plain terms, Marriage Law is the study of what happens between people, not just inside them. A man may be patient at work and impatient at home, and the difference is not in his character alone; it is in the patterns of the home. Change the pattern, and you often change the man.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Warri, a husband was transferred to another state for work. The family had to decide whether to move together or live apart. The question of marriage law sat at the centre of every conversation. The therapist helped them do a structured decision exercise: each partner listed their fears, their hopes, their non-negotiables, and their willingness to compromise. Written down, the lists were surprisingly compatible. What had looked like an impossible conflict was actually a failure of process. They chose to move together for two years, with a review date. Decisions made in daylight are easier to live with than decisions made in fear.

You see, my students, this example shows marriage law in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. Notice that nobody was blamed. The family was invited to look at the pattern, and the pattern began to loosen.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You said your son is stubborn. Tell me about a time this week when he was not stubborn."
Mother: (pause) "On Sunday, when we cooked together."
Therapist: "What was different about Sunday?"
Mother: "I was not rushing. I was just with him."
Therapist: "So connection changed his behaviour before discipline did. That is a very important discovery. What would it take to have two Sundays in a week?"

Look closely at her language. She used 'we' more than 'you'. She said 'we will try something' rather than 'you must change'. That language builds a team. A family that feels teamed with the therapist will take risks; a family that feels examined will hide.

Let me warn you about three common errors. First, ignoring the body. A family in crisis may be hungry, exhausted, or sick, and no amount of talking will help until those needs are addressed. Second, ignoring the money. Many 'communication problems' are actually money problems wearing a mask. Third, ignoring the community. If the church, mosque, or extended family opposes the therapy, the therapy may fail before it begins.

Let me pose a case. A wife tells you in private that she is afraid of her husband, but in the joint session she says everything is fine. What do you do? You do not confront her publicly. You make a safety plan privately, you assess the danger carefully, and you never conduct joint sessions where violence is active without a plan.

This week, sit with one family and ask only one question: 'What is the one thing you wish we could change first?' Then be quiet. Let them fill the silence. Whatever they say first is usually the doorway to everything else.

MODULE 7: Documentation Law
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Documentation Law. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Records may be requested by courts. Keep accurate records. Write factually. Records protect clients and you.

Let me give you the short version. Documentation Law says that every family is trying to solve a problem, even when the solution looks like the problem. The husband who stays late at the office may be avoiding a fight. The wife who shouts may be trying to be heard. Look for the purpose behind the behaviour before you judge it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Lagos, a couple sat in front of a therapist and argued about documentation law for twenty minutes without pausing. Each one had a list of the other's failures. The therapist listened carefully, then asked one question: "If this problem were solved tonight, what would tomorrow morning look like in your house?" The room went quiet. The husband said, "We would eat breakfast together." The wife said, "He would ask me how I slept." Suddenly the argument was no longer about blame. It was about a lost breakfast and an unasked question. From that small picture, the therapist helped them build a plan for documentation law that felt human, not legal.

You see, my students, this example shows documentation law in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Everyone in this family has been carrying something alone. Today I want us to put some of it down. Who will start?"
Grandmother: "I will. I am tired of being the one who keeps the peace."
Therapist: "Thank you for your courage. And when you keep the peace, who is freed from doing it?"
Grandmother: "Everyone else."
Therapist: "So your burden has become their permission to stay silent. Let us see if we can share that load differently."

Did you notice how she handled the resistance? She did not argue. She agreed with the part that was true and then asked a question that opened a door. That is the therapist's art: to be firm without being rigid, and gentle without being soft.

These mistakes are common and costly. Do not use technical words to impress. Do not take phone calls in session. Do not allow a session to run without a plan. Do not forget the homework from the previous week. Families notice whether you remember them, and remembering is a form of respect.

Here is a question for you. If a family says 'our son is the problem', what are three other places you would look before accepting that? Think: the marriage, the family rules, and the wider system of school and relatives. A therapist who looks only where the family points will often miss the real wound.

Your practical task is to observe a disagreement without intervening. Watch two people argue, in your family or at work, and notice the cycle: who pursues, who withdraws, who rescues. Write the cycle as a diagram. Bring it to class, with names changed, and we will study it together.

Testimony requires preparation and honesty. Know your role: fact witness or expert. Prepare with lawyers. Testify truthfully.

Here is another way of saying it. Testimony and Court Work is about the rules nobody wrote down. Every family has them: who speaks first, who decides money, who is allowed to be angry, who must always be strong. You see, my students, this example shows testimony and court work in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. The healing did not arrive in one dramatic moment. It arrived in small, repeated, ordinary acts.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I am going to ask a strange question. If the problem disappeared tonight, what is the first small thing you would notice tomorrow morning?"
Husband: "She would greet me at the door."
Wife: "He would ask about my day."
Therapist: "So the miracle is not a mansion or a lottery win. It is a greeting and a question. That tells me your marriage is closer to healing than you think."

Notice how she ended. She did not end on the problem; she ended on a picture of the family's preferred future. 'Imagine next Sunday, what would be different?' The family left with hope in their hands. Hope is not decoration; it is the fuel of change.

Now, the mistakes. Do not interrupt a father's story to correct his theology. Do not correct a mother's grammar while she is pouring out her heart. Do not ask six questions at once. Do not fill every silence. Silence is often the moment a family is deciding to trust you, so let it breathe.

Here is a question about your own heart. Which family would you find hardest to treat, and why? The answer reveals your blind spot. A therapist who knows his blind spot can guard against it; a therapist who denies it will be ruled by it.

This week, do a listening audit. Count how many times you interrupt someone in a day. Do not scold yourself; just count. Awareness is the first step. Next week, try to reduce the number by half.

MODULE 9: Informed Consent Law
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Informed Consent Law. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Consent has legal meaning. Get written consent where required. Document consent. Law and ethics align.

Let me explain it as a doctor would. A doctor does not treat a symptom without examining the body. Informed Consent Law is the examination of the family body. The symptom is only the complaint at the door; the illness is in the circulation of love, respect, and truth between members. Treat the circulation, and the symptom often fades.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Abeokuta, a husband was transferred to another state for work. The family had to decide whether to move together or live apart. The question of informed consent law sat at the centre of every conversation. The therapist helped them do a structured decision exercise: each partner listed their fears, their hopes, their non-negotiables, and their willingness to compromise. Written down, the lists were surprisingly compatible. What had looked like an impossible conflict was actually a failure of process. They chose to move together for two years, with a review date. Decisions made in daylight are easier to live with than decisions made in fear.

You see, my students, this example shows informed consent law in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. This shows how a single change in the family's way of relating can ripple through the whole home.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "You have both said the word respect many times. What does respect look like in your house, in actions, not in theory?"
Wife: "He would not correct me in front of his mother."
Husband: "She would not discuss our money with her sister."
Therapist: "Now we have something specific. These are not feelings; these are agreements. Let us write them down and test them for two weeks."

Let me point out what she avoided. She did not ask 'Why did you do that?' which invites defence. She asked 'What happened just before that?' which invites observation. Small changes in question shape produce large changes in conversation.

Here is my counsel. Do not confuse culture with abuse. Do not confuse respect with silence. Do not confuse forgiveness with pretending nothing happened. Do not confuse a husband's provision with his presence. And do not confuse a wife's submission with her having no voice.

Check your thinking here. What is the difference between a boundary and a wall? A boundary says 'this is where I end and you begin, and I can still love you'. A wall says 'you cannot reach me'. Families often build walls and call them boundaries. Your task is to turn walls into doors.

Your assignment this week is small but powerful. Ask one family member, 'What is one thing I could do differently that would make you feel more respected?' Then listen without defending. Write down the answer word for word. Bring it to class. This exercise teaches you how it feels to be on the receiving end of your own questions.

MODULE 10: Mandatory Reporting
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Mandatory Reporting. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Mandatory reporting means you must report certain abuse. Know what, when, and how to report. Failure to report has legal consequences.

Now, why does Mandatory Reporting matter so much in our setting? Because in Nigeria, a family is rarely just two people. It is grandparents, uncles, aunties, in-laws, pastors, imams, and neighbours. Whatever we study in this course must be applied with that wider family in view. A therapy that ignores the extended family will be outvoted by it.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Ilorin, a couple sat in front of a therapist and argued about mandatory reporting for twenty minutes without pausing. Each one had a list of the other's failures. The therapist listened carefully, then asked one question: "If this problem were solved tonight, what would tomorrow morning look like in your house?" The room went quiet. The husband said, "We would eat breakfast together." The wife said, "He would ask me how I slept." Suddenly the argument was no longer about blame. It was about a lost breakfast and an unasked question. From that small picture, the therapist helped them build a plan for mandatory reporting that felt human, not legal.

You see, my students, this example shows mandatory reporting in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. What changed was not the people. What changed was the space between them.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "I notice you have not looked at your husband since you sat down."
Wife: "There is nothing to look at."
Therapist: "That sentence carries a lot of pain. I am not here to judge it. I am here to understand it. When did you stop looking at him?"
Wife: (crying) "When he stopped looking at me."
Therapist: "Then let us begin exactly there. One look, one sentence, one honest moment."

Here is a subtle skill. She asked each member the same question in turn: 'What would you want the others to understand about you?' The quiet daughter spoke last and said the most important thing. Equal airtime is not a technique of fairness; it is a technique of discovery.

Here are the traps to avoid. Do not confuse understanding with agreement. You can understand why a husband shouts without approving of the shouting. Do not use your own marriage as the standard for every family. Do not quote research to argue with a grandmother; honour her first, then offer another view. And never promise a result you cannot control, such as 'your marriage will surely be saved'.

Consider this scenario. A father brings his teenage son for 'discipline problems' but refuses to discuss his own drinking. How do you proceed? You join with the father's concern, you keep the son's welfare central, and you gently raise the drinking as one part of the picture, without making it a condition of your respect.

Your task is to interview an elder. Ask an older person in your community: 'How were conflicts settled in your family when you were young? What did your parents do that you would keep, and what would you change?' Write one page. You will learn more about culture in one hour than in ten textbooks.

Work with lawyers respectfully: share information with consent, clarify roles, and protect clients. Collaboration helps families.

Therapist: "Your daughter says she feels unheard. What do you hear when she says that?"

MODULE 12: Ethics and Law Together
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, let us now turn our attention to Ethics and Law Together. This is a very important topic in Legal Issues in Family Therapy. Please listen carefully, because what you learn here will help you in the clinic, in the church, in the mosque, and in your own home.

Ethics and law overlap but differ. Follow both. When in doubt, consult supervisors and lawyers.

I want you to notice something important. What we are calling Ethics and Law Together here is not a theoretical idea you will admire and forget. It is a lens that changes what you see in the first ten minutes of a session. Two therapists can sit in the same room with the same family; the one who understands Ethics and Law Together will see a pattern, while the other will only see a problem.

Now, let us look at this in real life. In Warri, a husband was transferred to another state for work. The family had to decide whether to move together or live apart. The question of ethics and law together sat at the centre of every conversation. The therapist helped them do a structured decision exercise: each partner listed their fears, their hopes, their non-negotiables, and their willingness to compromise. Written down, the lists were surprisingly compatible. What had looked like an impossible conflict was actually a failure of process. They chose to move together for two years, with a review date. Decisions made in daylight are easier to live with than decisions made in fear.

You see, my students, this example shows ethics and law together in action. The problem was not only in one person. The problem lived in the pattern of interaction between them. When the pattern changed, the whole family changed. By the end, the family had not become perfect. It had become honest, and honesty was enough to restart growth.

Let me give you a short dialogue to show how a therapist can handle this topic in a session:

Therapist: "Both of you have said you want the marriage to work. What has each of you done in the last month that made it a little better?"
Husband: "I stopped shouting."
Wife: "I stopped bringing my mother into our arguments."
Therapist: "So you are both already working. That means I am not starting from zero. I am joining a project that is already underway."

Watch the therapist handle the secret. When the mother hinted at something painful, the therapist did not pounce. She said, 'There seems to be something heavy here. We do not have to open it today, but when you are ready, I will be here.' Safety was preserved and the door was left open.

Let me caution you about three things. First, the rescue fantasy: believing you must save the family yourself. You are a guide, not a saviour. Second, the expert trap: speaking as if you have never struggled at home. Humility opens doors that expertise cannot. Third, the speed trap: rushing change to prove you are effective. Slow, steady change lasts.

Think about this. A couple has stopped fighting. Is that progress? Not always. Some couples stop fighting because they have given up. Check for warmth, humour, and touch. Peace without connection can be the silence before separation.

PART TWO: REAL LIFE PRACTICALS - STEP BY STEP
================================================================

REAL LIFE PRACTICAL 1: Legal Dilemma Practice
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, theory without practice is empty. So let us do a real life practical on Legal Dilemma Practice. We will work through it in the style of the Ethics Decision Frame, which is one of the practical frames you will use in different settings.

Scenario: A client discloses child abuse. What is your legal duty? How do you report? A court requests therapy records. How do you respond legally and ethically?

Let me walk you through this practical step by step, as you would do it in real life.

Step One: Name the ethical question in one sentence. For example: 'May I keep this information private when it may affect another person's safety?' Vague dilemmas cannot be resolved, so precision comes first.

Step Two: Gather the facts. Who is involved? What exactly was disclosed? What are the legal duties in your jurisdiction? What is known, and what is assumed? Write the facts separately from the fears.

Step Three: Identify the principles at stake. Autonomy, nonmaleficence (do no harm), beneficence (do good), justice, fidelity, and the client's own cultural and faith values. Where principles pull in different directions, say so honestly.

Step Four: Consult before deciding. Call a supervisor, a trusted senior colleague, or a professional body. Consultation is not indecision; it is diligence. Record whom you consulted and what was discussed.

Step Five: Choose the least harmful, most transparent course. Explain your reasoning to the client in plain language, including what you can keep private and what you must share. Document the conversation.

Step Six: Act, and protect the vulnerable first. If a child, a suicidal person, or a threatened third party is at risk, safety overrides the usual privacy rules, within the law.

Step Seven: Review the outcome and record what you learned. Ethical competence grows through reflection on hard decisions, not through memorising rules.

When therapists skip these steps, families feel rushed, and rushing breeds resistance. When therapists follow them, even imperfectly, families relax, because structure communicates care. I have seen a single well-run first session change the direction of an entire family's year.

You see, this practical is not difficult. It needs patience, respect, and skill. If you follow these steps, you will help many families. Practice this practical with a classmate this week. One person will be the therapist, the others will be the family. Switch roles after twenty minutes. Write down what you learned.

Important note: Always respect culture and faith. In Nigeria and in many African homes, elders are respected, faith is important, and the family is extended. Do not fight culture. Work with culture. Use proverbs, prayers, and respectful language where appropriate. The family will accept help faster when they feel their values are honoured.

Sixth, what will you do next session? Tell the family exactly what you will do, what you hope they will do, and what you will celebrate if it works. Give hope, and end with encouragement.

DEEPER EXPLANATION ONE: KNOW YOUR LOCAL LAW
----------------------------------------------------------------

Law differs from place to place. What counts as child abuse, when you must report, how custody is decided, and what records courts can request all vary. Your first duty is to know the law where you practise. Read the relevant statutes, attend training, and build relationships with lawyers and child protection officers. Ignorance is not a defence, and it can cost a family dearly.

DEEPER EXPLANATION TWO: THE THERAPIST IN COURT
----------------------------------------------------------------

Courts are not therapy rooms. In court, your job is to be factual, neutral, and clear. Bring your records. Do not overstate your certainty. Distinguish what you observed from what you concluded. Do not advocate for a parent; advocate for the child's best interests and for truth. Dress professionally, answer the question asked, and say "I do not know" when you do not know. A therapist who stays honest in court protects the profession and serves the family.

DEEPER EXPLANATION THREE: THE CUSTODY REPORT THAT HELPED A CHILD
----------------------------------------------------------------

A couple in Abuja divorced and fought bitterly over custody of two children. Each parent wanted the therapist to testify against the other. The therapist declined to take sides. Instead, she conducted a child-focused assessment: observations of each parent with the children, interviews with teachers, and the children's own wishes, considered with care. Her report described each parent's strengths and each child's needs, and recommended a shared custody arrangement with clear routines. The court accepted the recommendation. The children later told their mother, "For the first time, someone asked what we wanted." The therapist's neutrality was not indifference; it was protection.

DEEPER EXPLANATION FOUR: WHEN THE LAW AND CULTURE DISAGREE
----------------------------------------------------------------

Sometimes a family's custom conflicts with the law. A widow may be pressured to marry her late husband's brother; a girl may be promised in marriage; a child may be beaten severely as discipline. As a therapist, you must protect the vulnerable while respecting culture. Use dialogue, involve respected elders and faith leaders, explain the law clearly, and escalate when a child's safety is at stake. You do not need to choose between culture and law; you need to work patiently toward safety and dignity for every family member.

PRACTICAL FOR THIS WEEK: THE LEGAL RESOURCE MAP

COMMON MISTAKES
----------------------------------------------------------------

Do not promise confidentiality that the law does not allow. Do not destroy records. Do not testify beyond your expertise. Do not let a parent hire you to "win" custody. And do not ignore legal issues because they feel uncomfortable; your client's safety may depend on them.

PART FOUR: KEY TECHNIQUES YOU MUST MASTER
----------------------------------------------------------------

My dear students, in Legal Issues in Family Therapy you must master practical techniques. Theory is good, but techniques change lives. Let me list the key techniques for this course, and I will explain how to use each one.

Technique 1: Mandatory Reporting

How to use it: first explain why the technique exists, not only what it is. Families cooperate when they understand the purpose. Second, model it in a low-stakes example, such as how to greet a neighbour. Third, have the family rehearse the real situation while you coach gently. Fourth, ask, "What felt natural, and what felt strange?" Fifth, agree on a practice plan for the week.

Real life tip: use the family's own names and situations in the rehearsal. Practice that sounds like their real life transfers to their real life.

Common mistake: correcting too much too soon. Let the first attempt be imperfect. Confidence comes before polish.

Technique 2: Documentation

How to use it: teach it in three sentences. One, what it is. Two, when to use it. Three, what to say. Then show it. Then have the family show you. Then ask each member to name one moment this week when they will use it. Write those moments down and review them next session.

Real life tip: ask a respected elder or pastor to endorse the technique publicly. Cultural endorsement speeds adoption dramatically.

Common mistake: using the technique only in the office. If it stays in the office, nothing changes at home.

Technique 3: Testimony

How to use it: start with a story. Tell a short story of a family who used the technique and what changed. Stories lower resistance and create hope. Then walk through the steps on paper so the family can see them. Then practise in pairs inside the room, with one person as the coach. End by asking, "What would make this hard at home, and how will you handle that?"

Real life tip: keep a small collection of true, anonymised stories from your practice and community. Stories travel further than instructions.

Common mistake: ignoring the obstacles the family names. If they say "my husband will not participate", that is the real problem to solve before the technique can work.

Technique 4: Legal Consultation

How to use it: use the teach-back method. Explain the technique briefly, then ask the family to teach it back to you in their own words. If they cannot, the teaching was not clear enough. Then rehearse it once, and once more with a small obstacle added, such as someone interrupting. Finish with a written note in simple language that they can keep at home.

Real life tip: write the note in the language the family thinks in, or provide a short voice note if literacy is limited. Accessibility is respect.

Common mistake: assuming understanding because heads were nodding. Nodding is politeness; teach-back is proof.

Technique 5: Safety Planning

How to use it: begin with a plain invitation. "This is a way to help you talk better. Let us try it together." Then demonstrate it yourself, with a colleague or with your own words. Let the family practise while you watch, and give kind, specific feedback. Send the skill home as a small assignment. A technique works only when it is practised at home.

Real life tip: many Nigerian families respect proverbs. Teach this technique with a short proverb so it is remembered long after the session.

Common mistake: do not stack techniques. One per session, mastered, is worth more than five half-learned.

I encourage you, my dear students. Do not be discouraged when a family does not change quickly. Change takes time. Be patient. Be kind. Be prayerful. Your work matters: you are helping marriages, protecting children, and healing communities. God bless you as you continue.

Key Terms to Remember
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Law: the legal rules that govern practice, including confidentiality, reporting, and family matters.
- Confidentiality: the duty to keep client information private, within clear legal and safety limits explained at the start of therapy.
- Custody: the legal arrangement for where a child lives and who makes decisions for the child after separation or divorce.
- Reporting: the legal and ethical duty to inform authorities when a child or vulnerable person is in danger.
- Court: the legal setting where custody, protection orders, and other family matters are decided; therapists may provide factual reports.
